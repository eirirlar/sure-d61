# Template — sell-in document to CINEA

Reusable structure and voice guide for any Sunlit Sea document going to CINEA, project officers, or funder audiences of the same shape (EIC evaluators, review panels). Applies to attachments accompanying change proposals, mid-term reports written to be read, un-solicited project descriptions, and cover letters that need to carry weight.

## The reader

A CINEA project officer reads under time pressure, on top of a queue of similar documents. They scan first, then decide whether to read. Assume they will read the first sentence of the document, the first sentence of each section, and the budget table. Everything else has to earn its way into their attention.

The reader is not the sponsor of the project. Their default posture is *prove it*. They react well to facts, named parties, specific outcomes and numbers with units. They react badly to adjectives, hedges, and administrative framing.

## Length

One to three pages when rendered. If the document does not stand up at two pages, adding a fourth will not save it — cut instead.

## The opening — the single most important sentence in the document

The first sentence must (a) put a concrete image in the reader's head and (b) make them want the second sentence. It must not describe the document itself, explain how the document came to exist, thank anyone, or restate what was asked for.

Bad openings — do not use:

- "Working draft of…"
- "This document describes…"
- "Attached please find…"
- "As requested by the Project Officer…"
- "In the context of the ongoing SuRE project…"
- "The purpose of this note is to…"
- "It is with pleasure that Sunlit Sea AS submits…"
- "Following our recent correspondence…"
- "Prepared by Sunlit Sea AS to accompany…"

Good openings — model these:

- Concrete scene: *"In Porsgrunn, Norway, a 3.2 MW floating solar plant is being permitted and built for connection to the Herøya industrial-park grid."*
- Direct proposition: *"Sunlit Sea proposes to install a research-grade demonstrator inside the Gunneklevfjorden plant and hand SuRE twelve months of quality-controlled field data before project end."*
- Fact-forward, tension-forward: *"SuRE's current monitoring dataset covers inland reservoirs and continental-European coastal sites. A Norwegian near-shore reference is missing."*
- Track record forward: *"Sunlit Sea has been operating a 105 kWp aluminium-float FPV installation at Skiftestjørna since autumn 2024. Production over the first full operating year has exceeded expectations."*

The best opening for a given document is usually the one closest to the reader's own live concern. If the reader is worried about a missing demonstrator, lead with the demonstrator. If they are worried about budget discipline, lead with what the money does not fund. If they are worried about execution risk, lead with what already exists.

## Section skeleton

Every document to CINEA fits inside the following ten sections, with variation only in how much space each section warrants. Do not invent new section shapes.

### 1. Hook and proposition — one short paragraph

Purpose: put the concrete thing into the reader's head and state the ask in one paragraph.

Content: what is being built or done, where, at what scale, with which named parties; the budget headline in one clause; the fit with the funder's programme in one clause.

Voice: present tense, active voice, named parties, no adjectives of self-praise, no meta-commentary about the document.

Length: 60–120 words. One paragraph.

Extension-proposal pattern. When the document proposes a change or extension to an already-funded project, the strongest hook frames the proposal as the *delivery* of an existing committed deliverable — scaled, adapted, relocated or enriched — rather than as a new proposition. *"D6.4 is delivered at Gunneklevfjorden as an enlarged and instrumented pilot"* is a stronger opening than *"we propose a new demonstrator"*, because it anchors the ask to something the reader has already backed. Look for the existing deliverable, task or objective ID that the proposal advances, and open with it.

### 2. Proposition detail — one or two paragraphs

Purpose: expand the one-paragraph hook into a full description of what is being proposed, still without descending into implementation detail.

Content: the scope, the scale, the deliverables in one sentence each, the boundary of what is included and what is not.

Voice: describe what happens, not what is intended. *"The demonstrator is installed by Prosolar"* beats *"It is intended that the demonstrator will be installed by Prosolar."*

### 3. Feasibility evidence

Purpose: show that the thing being proposed is real, not aspirational. This section wins or loses the reader.

Content: sub-blocks for the axes of risk the reader will imagine — site and permits, grid and off-take, engineering and execution, funding of anything the funder is not being asked to fund, track record on a comparable prior project.

Voice: fact-first sentences. Each bullet or line names a specific party and a specific status. Not *"we have partners"* but *"Herøya Nett has confirmed grid capacity and identified the connection points."* Not *"permits are underway"* but *"Miljødirektoratet has accepted subsurface anchoring on the capped seabed."*

Structural rule: every claim in this section is a fact that could be independently verified by the reader picking up the phone.

### 4. Activities enabled by the funding

Purpose: show what the money does. This is where documents that describe *roles and person-months* instead of *activities and outputs* collapse. Reviewers want to see verifiable work, not organograms.

Content: a lettered or numbered list of concrete activities. Each activity has: a name (a phrase, not a paragraph), a one-sentence description of the work, a named owner, and a stated output that a reader could later ask to see. Avoid activity names that describe organisational categories (*"project management"*); use activity names that describe work being done (*"adaptation of the aluminium float and mooring configuration to the Gunneklevfjorden environmental envelope"*).

Voice: each activity is a small unit of prose, not a bullet inside a bureaucratic table. If it can be reduced to a bullet without losing meaning, the activity is not concrete enough.

Anti-pattern: *"The CTO will spend 12 PM on WP6 activities."* This tells the reader nothing. Replace with the activity the CTO is executing and the output that will exist at the end of it.

### 5. Value to the funder's programme

Purpose: connect the proposal to the funder's stated goals, in the funder's own vocabulary.

Content: for CINEA / SuRE, this means naming the specific work packages, tasks, deliverables and KERs the proposal contributes to, and describing data flows or capability additions concretely. For EIC or Horizon Europe generally, this means connecting to the call's expected impact and outcomes.

Voice: use the funder's exact terminology and ID hierarchy — HLO, SO, WP, Task, D-number, KPI ID, KER title — as those IDs appear in the grant agreement. Describe the proposal as *extending*, *delivering*, *elaborating* or *validating* those pre-existing items, not as parallel work with new labels. Signalling that the writer has read the funder's own text and is engaging on the funder's own scaffolding is worth more here than any amount of prose.

Rule: if the proposal is contributing to more than one existing item, list each with its ID and a one-sentence description of the flow — *"Task 6.4 (extended): Prototype production and field deployment. The Gunneklevfjorden pilot delivers D6.4 at enlarged scale."* — rather than describing the contributions as a narrative paragraph. The list format is easier to review and easier to check against the grant agreement.

Structural rule: this section is where the *so what* of the proposal has to land. A reviewer stopping here should already know why the proposal deserves the money.

### 6. Team and execution capacity

Purpose: show the applicant can execute. Distinct from feasibility (which is about the project) — this is about the team.

Content: relevant prior projects (with scale, date, outcome), named external execution partners with their roles, the internal capability being brought.

Voice: past tense for track record, present tense for current arrangements. Superlatives evaporate; scale, dates and outcomes carry weight.

Length target: short. Four to eight sentences. A long team section signals the wrong applicant is applying.

### 7. Timeline

Purpose: show a credible sequence of events with dependency logic that survives contact with reality.

Content: a table with two columns — period, activities. Periods anchored to the funder's decision date (T0, T+3 months, T+6 months) rather than to fixed calendar dates, unless the funder has committed to a specific decision date. Include activities beyond the funded period only where they are on the applicant's own responsibility — this signals the applicant does not walk away when the money runs out.

Voice: activities in the timeline map to the activity names in section 4. If they do not map, one of the two sections is wrong.

### 8. Budget summary

Purpose: give the reader the numbers in one glance, without formulas or cell references.

Content: cost categories in the funder's expected schema (personnel, subcontracting, travel, equipment, other, indirect), a subtotal, the funding-rate calculation, and the requested grant amount. One row of narrative below the table connecting the biggest cost lines back to the activities. If the funder is not being asked to fund something that a reviewer might assume is bundled in — commercial hardware, existing partner contributions — name that explicitly.

Voice: numbers with units, no adjectives, no cell references, no formula language. Any cell references or spreadsheet cross-talk belongs in a separate internal document.

### 9. Risks and mitigations — only when it benefits the document

Purpose: acknowledge the hard parts before the reviewer does.

Content: two or three specific risks the reviewer will already be thinking about, each with a one-sentence mitigation the applicant has actually thought through. Do not invent risks to look thorough; do not list risks that have no mitigation.

Voice: matter-of-fact. *"If CINEA approval arrives after M32, the installation window compresses. Sunlit Sea coordinates scope reduction with IFE in that case, keeping the monitoring campaign as first priority."* Better than either silence or *"we anticipate no risks."*

Skip this section if there are no material risks a reviewer would foreground themselves.

### 10. Contact

Purpose: one person, one email, one phone. Not a directory.

Content: legal entity name, contact person and role, email, phone.

## Voice — rules that apply everywhere

- Named parties, not roles. *"Miljødirektoratet has confirmed…"* beats *"The environmental authority has confirmed…"*
- Present tense for what happens, past tense for what has happened, future tense sparingly. Avoid *shall*, *will endeavour*, *intends to*.
- Numbers with units and thousand-separators (space, per EU convention). *3.2 MW*, *470 000 EUR*, *twelve months*.
- Active voice unless passive genuinely fits. *"Prosolar installs the electrical works"* beats *"The electrical works will be installed."*
- No adjectives of self-praise (*leading*, *world-class*, *cutting-edge*, *unique*, *innovative*). Facts carry weight; adjectives evaporate.
- No emphasis-by-assertion (*precisely*, *importantly*, *crucially*, *notably*, *it is worth noting*, *not merely X but Y*). These signal weak underlying content.
- Lead with the positive claim, not the negation. *"The monitoring package is rented for the project period"* beats *"We are not buying equipment that would sit idle."*
- No document meta-commentary. Do not describe what the document is; be the document.

Register — cold pitch vs extension proposal. The original grant proposal is a competitive first pitch that may legitimately carry ambition-heavy language ("SuRE will enhance…", "exceptionally well positioned"). An extension proposal is different: the reader has already committed money and is asking whether this applicant knows their scope. Favour understated confidence over first-pitch rhetoric. The reader wants evidence that the applicant has thought carefully about the change, not enthusiasm about the opportunity. Match the register of the grant agreement, not of the winning proposal.

## Checklist before sending

1. Does the first sentence put a concrete image in the reader's head?
2. Does every section's first sentence earn the second sentence?
3. Are all named parties spelled correctly and consistently?
4. Are all numbers presented with units and consistent formatting?
5. Would a reviewer who reads only the first sentence of each section still know the proposal?
6. Are there any leftover repo file paths, cell references, task numbers or draft markings?
7. Does the budget table cross-reference back to the activities section?
8. Does the timeline anchor to a decision date the applicant does not control, or to fixed dates that will drift?
9. Is there any adjective of self-praise anywhere in the document? If yes, cut.
10. Does the document stand alone without any of the internal working files it was built from?
