**EXECUTIVE SUMMARY**

The document herein reports the activities carried out in Task 6.3 concerning tests performed in SINTEF laboratories to characterise the failure modes and strength capacities of the new connection system designed by the project SUNLIT SEA during the SUREWAVE project. This connection is used for connecting the FPV modules together: the new connection system was designed based on the results from D6.2 which clearly highlighted that the first version of the connection system would not meet the requirements on properties and survivability. The connection system still uses a Polyurethane (PU) material which was tested in D6.1.

Section 1 covers the deliverable with an introduction and outline of the objectives of the deliverable.

Section 2 focuses on the evaluation of the mechanical properties and strength of the new PU connections system.

Section 3 focuses on the fatigue properties of the PU material and the estimation of fatigue life of the new connection system.

The report ends with conclusions in section 4.

#  Introduction

The report summarises the work done in task 6.3 of the SUREWAVE project. Initially, the aim of the task was to assess the fatigue behaviour of the critical connections described and tested in D6.1. These critical connections were However, the results from Task 6.2 revealed that the previously designed hinge system is structurally weak, affecting the survivability and operability of the floating PV solution. These findings prompted a shift in focus for Task 6.3 towards testing a new generation of connection systems between FPVs, as they were identified as the most critical components in Task 6.2

The new connection system was designed by SUNLIT Sea, in order to avoid early buckling of the first generation of the connections on one hand and to have higher strength properties in tension on the other. [Figure 1](#_Ref190247362) illustrates the new connection system tested in this report. SINTEF utilized both standard and in-house test methods, allowing for setup adaptations to meet specific requirements. Custom test setups were also designed to fit the specimens in the lab equipment. Advanced strain measurement techniques (DIC) and cameras were employed during some tests, providing detailed information on localized deformation in the joints.

![](../images/2025-02-12_surewave_d6.3/image6.png)

[]{#_Ref190247362 .anchor}*Figure 1 : Picture of the new connection system*

The connections were tested in tension, compression and shear to assess the new mechanical properties and to provide input to the work done in WP5 on the FE analysis of this connection system. In addition, fatigue tests were carried out on the Polyurethane material used to make the connection systems in order to evaluate the long-term fatigue properties of the newly designed connections. These results are presented in the present report.

The new test campaign focuses on a component which represents a portion of the full connection system. Components produced by injection moulding were delivered by SUNLIT Sea to SINTEF. ## Tensile tests on the PU connections

### Experimental setup

Tensile tests were carried out in order to characterise the axial stiffness of the component, which is the most critical, and to provide input for the fatigue characterisation presented in section 2 of the present report.

A picture of the test setup is given in [Figure 5](#_Ref187925319). Two steel pipes are connected to the male and female parts of the connection. The bottom pipe (female part) is clamped to the tensile machine while the male part is fixed to a tailored machine grip that is fixed to the machine crosshead in order to apply the tensile load. The machine used was an Instron with a maximum capacity of 350 kN. The speed of the machine crosshead was fixed at 0.5 mm/s during the tests, which can be considered as quasi-static.

![[]{#_Ref187925319 .anchor}Figure 5: test setup used for the tensile and compression tests.](../images/2025-02-12_surewave_d6.3/image6.jpeg){alt="A machine with a piece of metal Description automatically generated" width="5.50632874015748in" height="4.128533464566929in"}

During the tests, the strains and displacements in the specimens were monitored using the DIC (Digital Image Correlation) technique. To this end, a speckle pattern was painted on each of the specimens, and its evolution was followed by a Basler camera. The acquisition system recorded images at a 0.5 Hz frequency with an average resolution of 900×2980 pixels. The acquisition system of the camera was synchronized with the tensile machine data acquisition system. All recorded images were post-processed using the eCorr software[^2]. The objective of the DIC is to provide full-field strain measurement during the test, using a digital grid. This provides access to local strain field measurements.

### Results

[Figure 6](#_Ref188003480) presents the force-displacement results obtained from the tensile tests on the connection components. The 5 first tests (1-5) are the normal connection consisting of the assembly of a male and female part. In order to quantify the influence of the gap induced by the injection process, male-male and female-female assemblies were also tested, which have tighter gaps between plugs during assembly. These are referred to as MM (male-male) and FF (female-female) in the following.

![[]{#_Ref188003480 .anchor}Figure 6: Results of the tensile tests on connection components.](../images/2025-02-12_surewave_d6.3/image11.png){width="6.033659230096238in" height="3.927321741032371in"}

[Figure 6](#_Ref188003480) shows that the force increases, when the assembly is loaded, until a maximum around 2 kN at a displacement of 30 mm. The tests are very repeatable overall. A slight difference can be seen between the normal (male-female) and MM/FF connections: the response of the MM and FF being stiffer. This is clearly connected to a tighter gap between the two parts of the connections which allows for a better force transfer. However, this does not seem to have a strong influence on the maximum recorded load. All the results from the tensile tests are summarised in [Table 2](#_Ref188004247), as well as some statistics for the different tests and some estimations of the stiffness of the components at different displacements.

# Fatigue properties of the PU connections

## Fatigue tests of the PU material

### Fatigue methodology

#### Experimental setup: 

The fatigue tests of the PU materials were originally intended to be made on the first generation of the connection systems that were tested and reported in D6.1. However, due to the huge elongation of the material (typical for an elastomer) and the limited displacement of the tensile machine available at SINTEF, it was not possible to perform fatigue tests on the components themselves. Thus, an alternative approach was used, consisting of performing fatigue tests on tensile specimens machined from the connections. Several iterations were made in order to get the correct specimen geometry, based on trial and error testing and FEA (Finite Element Analysis), in order to optimise the maximum machine displacement available vs the maximum load reachable. These iterations are illustrated in [Figure 15](#_Ref187660053).

![[]{#_Ref187660053 .anchor}Figure 15: Different specimens configuration used to perform the fatigue tests on the PU material. The bottom one is the one used for the tests.](../images/2025-02-12_surewave_d6.3/image23.png){width="4.453987314085739in" height="3.2399354768153983in"}

The specimens have a dog-bone shape in order to localise the deformation during the tests, with a very small gauge length to reach the desired load values with a limited machine displacement (+ 150mm allowed). The machine used was a ZWICK hydraulic. The details of the final geometry used are given in [Figure 16](#_Ref187660276).

![[]{#_Ref187660276 .anchor}Figure 16: Geometry of final the dog-bone specimen used to carry the fatigue test on the PU material.](../images/2025-02-12_surewave_d6.3/image24.png){width="5.555400262467192in" height="2.537949475065617in"}

The fatigue tests were displacement (strain) controlled. Load-control was first attempted but the machine could not be controlled with sufficient accuracy in this mode, due to increased elongation over time, and extreme softness of the material. The cyclic displacement was controlled as depicted in [Figure 17](#_Ref189051667).

![[]{#_Ref189051667 .anchor}Figure 17: Illustration of the cyclic loading definition followed during the fatigue tests.](../images/2025-02-12_surewave_d6.3/image25.png){width="6.358018372703412in" height="3.450495406824147in"}

First, a mean displacement was imposed to the specimens, then a displacement amplitude around this mean displacement was imposed as cycles. The different tests run during the campaign are given in [Table 6](#_Ref187662257).

In [Table 6](#_Ref187662257), the "corresponding %US" refers to the percentage of the maximum load achieved during the tests compared to the ultimate strength measured for the material and reported in D6.1. For example, for specimen 3, 89.2% means that the maximum load during the test represents 89.2% of the ultimate strength of the material. The corresponding stress is established using an FE model of the dog-bone specimen to calculate the stress corresponding at a given displacement; the model is described in section [3.1.1.2](#fe-modelling-approach-to-estimate-the-stress-in-the-dog-bone-specimens) below. Note that in some case the %US is more than 100%. This is because the %US is calculated from the mean value of the US in D6.1, and it is possible that some specimens break above the mean value, yielding to a %US superior to 100%.

For all the tests, the frequency was set to 1 Hz. It is possible that self-heating might influence the results on elastomers, especially at high frequency, but these effects are neglected in this study. Moreover 1 Hz is in a range very representative of what a material would experience in real sea conditions, where most of the wave have peak periods between 1 and 0.1 Hz, see D2.1.

The material law used in the FE model to describe the PU material is an hyperelastic model based on the \*MARLOW strain energy function[^3]. It was calibrated using the tensile test data input to Abaqus, and obtained from the tensile test campaign performed in the SUREWAVE project and reported in section 2 of D6.1. [Figure 20](#_Ref187664294) shows the good correlation obtained between the Abaqus FE model using the Marlow hyperplastic constitutive model and the experimental results obtained during the first campaign of SUREWAVE. The FE results are presented using fully integrated elements and reduced integrated elements formulation in order to evaluate the impact of the elements choice on the predicted response, which appear to be negligible in this case.

### SN curves results

[Table 7](#_Ref187670085) summarizes the results from the different fatigue tests performed during the campaign. Overall, the higher the maximum stress during the tests, the lower the number of cycles to failure. There is a noticeable dispersion during the tests, which is expected for an elastomeric material. The dispersion is higher at high stress amplitude and tends to decrease at lower stress amplitude. It is interesting to notice that tests 5 and 7 were stopped before failure was reached due to the very high number of cycles to failure. This in terms will underestimate the number of cycles to failure at low stress amplitude, but it is also a conservative procedure, as more cycles are expected than what was currently run. Test 6 was found to be very low as compared to the other tests at this amplitude, because the specimen was highly degraded prior to testing, thus degrading the fatigue life (Burnt in the notch part during the machining). It was decided to represent it anyway on the figure, and is accounted in the calculation of the SN curves, leading to a more conservative estimation of the fatigue life. Overall, the high dispersion that we observed in fatigue was also observed in previous tests campaign of the PU during the project, also during the first one on the first-generation hinges, where the deviation was noticeable (See D6.1 and section 2.3 of the present document). In fatigue, the deviation is higher because it is coming from a combination of three sources: The material intrinsic properties, the defaults during production process and the nature of the fatigue tests themselves will all contribute to the tests deviation.

## Evaluation of the fatigue life of the new connection system

### FE model definition

The model used to calculate the stress in the component was built in Abaqus, using an imported CAD model provided by SUNLIT SEA and corresponding to the component geometry tested in section 1. The model was discretised with solid elements and consists of two connection parts (male and female) in PU material, one rope piece modelled with solid elements and two steel cylinders that were used to apply the tensile loads to the connection. The complete model is represented in [Figure 22](#_Ref187760643).

![A blue and red wireframe Description automatically generated](../images/2025-02-12_surewave_d6.3/image34.png){width="2.9923075240594925in" height="1.766411854768154in"}![A wireframe of a blue object Description automatically generated](../images/2025-02-12_surewave_d6.3/image35.png){width="2.991994750656168in" height="1.6498337707786526in"}

[]{#_Ref187760643 .anchor}Figure 22: Overview of the FE model definition.

In total, the model consists of 67387 elements. The Marlow constitutive model described earlier was used to describe the two PU connections parts, while the steel cylinders were assumed as rigid materials. Finally, the rope was modelled using an anisotropic elastic model, with the main Young modulus set to 634 MPa (evaluated using the data of the supplier given here[^4] (960 kg to failure at 30% elongation yields to an elastic material with a Young modulus of 634 MPa, assuming linear elasticity). In order to capture the rope behaviour (no bending and compressive stiffnesses) the Young moduli in the transverse directions were set to very low values (i.e.: E2=E3=20 MPa) and the shear moduli were set to 5 MPa). These are our assumption and note that these are strong assumptions that are thought to be representative of the rope used but that cannot be fully verified without testing of the rope, which is outside of the scope of this work. The bottom cylinder was completely fixed, while the top cylinder is imposed a linear displacement over time (at a speed of 10 mm/s).

### FE results and stress estimations in the connection

Finite Element (FE) simulations were conducted to analyze the behavior of the components under tension, compression, and shear loading conditions. The results for the tensile tests are shown in [Figure 23](#_Ref187767678), while the outcomes from the compression simulations are presented in [Figure 26](#_Ref187835662). Additionally, the shear results are displayed in [Figure 27](#_Ref188879566). These simulations provide a comprehensive understanding of the material\'s performance across different loading scenarios, highlighting the varying stress distributions and behaviours in tension, compression, and shear.

Overall, the comparisons between these different loading conditions demonstrate that the model can describe the component\'s behaviour with sufficient accuracy. As a result, it will be further utilized to estimate the stress distribution and subsequent fatigue life of the component. While the current model performs well, there is potential for improvement by more accurately capturing the behaviour of the rope, which could enhance the overall accuracy of the simulations.

From the FEA it appears that the maximum principal stress experienced by the PU component is **11.09 MPa**, which happens for a displacement of **28 mm**. At this displacement level, the connection is not functional anymore, as the plugs are disconnected from each other and the connection is permanently damaged. Thus, it is unlikely that this state will happen in operation, or the connection will need to be repaired. Still, using this stress value as a conservative value, it is clearly visible that the stress in the component **will not exceed the 15.6 MPa value defined in section [3.1.3](#fatigue-life-estimate-based-on-sn-curves)** for a 25 years lifetime. Using this value in equation (1) from [3.1.2](#sn-curves-results) with the coefficient from [Table 8](#_Ref187759392) yields a number of cycles to failure of

$$N_{f} = \left( \frac{\sigma}{A} \right)^{\frac{1}{b}} = \left( \frac{11.09}{52.25} \right)^{1/( - 0.059)} = 2.5e^{+ 11}$$

at this stress value.

It should be noted that this is a very rough fatigue estimation of the component, and that mixed modes loading (i.e. combined tension and shear for example) has not been considered here. Nevertheless, it is believed that the assumptions taken in this study and the conclusions are both reliable and conservative.

**If a steel rod system is used**, the force in the PU component is noticeably higher than if a rope system is used, as discussed in section 2.3. A FEA of such system shows that the force **cannot exceed 6.9 kN in order for the principal stress to remain under the safety value of 15.6 MPa**. It is still **3 times** the maximum load observed in the PU component test with PP rope.

# Conclusions

The current deliverable focused on reporting the results obtained from the experimental campaign performed in task 6.3 to assess the ultimate strength of the new connection design developed in the SUREWAVE project which will be used in the FPV solar system. In addition, some fatigue tests and preliminary lifetime estimations were also presented. From the results, the following conclusions can be made:

**[Concerning Ultimate strength and components stiffnesses :]{.underline}**

- The new connection system has a higher ultimate strength than the first version tested in D6.1 in tension, which is very noticeable: the portion tested had **an average of 2 kN to failure**, but represents only 1/5^th^ of the total real connection. A gain of almost **3 times compared to the original connection.**

- **The ultimate strength in tension will also vary noticeably depending on the rope system used to maintain both sides of the connection system in place. (From 2kN to 8.36 kN)**

- The deviation during the tests was also significant due to early cracks triggered during some tests. This is probably due to bad production process. **It is thus critical to have very good control over the production of the connections during mass production to get the expected mechanical properties.**

- The **compression behaviour has been significantly improved**, which will avoid earlier buckling and snapping loads compared to the original hinge design presented in D6.1. The strength to buckling in the component tested was **around 20 kN**.

- The new connection system kept its high shear deformation indicating that **tension is the only failure mode to consider** during operation.

**[Concerning the fatigue behaviour:]{.underline}**

- From the fatigue campaign run on the Polyurethane materials and the FEA performed, it seems that the stresses in the connection tightened with a PP rope remain very limited even when the maximum force is reached, with a **maximum calculated stress of 11.09 MPa**. This would then lead to a number of cycles to failure of approx. $2.5e^{+ 11}$, by extrapolating the SN curves obtained experimentally, which is **well beyond the 7.88e^+08^ corresponding to 25 years**. It thus indicates that fatigue would not be a main failure mode for these connections. However, the influence of ageing on the PU material was not accounted for in this campaign, so some additional checks should be performed in future research.

- If a steel rod is used instead of a PP rope, the load can get significantly higher in the PU component, and **the 7.88e^+08^ will be reached if the force in the component does not exceed 6.9 kN.**

Following the points highlighted during the testing of the new connections, it appears that **the new system has much better performance than the first version**, in all critical aspects underlined in D6.1. **Performance could however be further increased** if the rope system is changed with a stiffer one, for example with a steel wire instead of a PP rope. Overall, the new connection **is more robust and operates at higher load levels, and fatigue does not seem to be a strong issue as initially anticipated.**
