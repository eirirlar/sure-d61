# Investor review – KER1 business plan, sections 2–5

Reviewer perspective: strategic energy-company investor or impact fund, screening ahead of a possible 2027 round. Draft reviewed: 2026-11-14 business plan, sections 2–5 (organisation, product, market, business model). Team, operations and financials not yet available and not assessed. Roughly 15 minutes of reading.

## 1. First impression (the 2-minute read)

What I take away: a two-founder Norwegian company with a small Gen 1 calm-water business (165 kWp in operation across two plants, own factory closed in 2024) is now developing a wave-tolerant cast polyurethane float with full-edge hinges, funded mostly by EU grants. It wants to build its own first plants in Norway in 2027–2028, then sell kits, then possibly become an EPC. Pipeline is 6.75 MWp at three named Norwegian sites.

What I do not take away in two minutes: how big the opportunity is, what wave conditions the product handles, what it costs to make today, what a customer saves, how much money is being raised and for what. The first page reads as a company history and an EU-project summary, not as an investment opportunity. The opening facts an investor notices are "factory wound down because it cost more to run than volumes could carry", "small team, much depends on the two founders", "no patent", "limited cash", "not yet proven in the field". All honest and all appropriate somewhere, but on page one they define the company before the opportunity does.

Overall: credible engineering, honest tone, good grasp of standards and permitting. The investment case itself (market size, unit economics, why Nordic water solar pays at Nordic prices, why this team wins) is still implicit. As written I would take a second meeting out of curiosity about the technology, not because of the plan.

## 2. Top 10 issues, ranked

### 1. The product's operating envelope is never stated

Location: section "Sunlit Sea CONNECT", "Distinctive features"; Competitors table.

Why it matters: the entire positioning is "designed for waves", yet the plan gives no significant wave height, wave period, wind speed or current the platform is rated for, with and without breakwater. The only number is a hinge ultimate load of 37 kN (up from 14 kN), with no statement of what load the design case requires, so the investor cannot tell whether 37 kN is a large margin or barely enough. Meanwhile the competitor table credits Fred. Olsen 1848 with a DNV review for Hs up to 3.5 m. Against a quantified competitor, an unquantified claim loses.

Suggested fix: add one line or a small table: design Hs and Tp for the platform alone, for the platform with directional breakwater, and the test conditions reached at SINTEF and MARIN (scale, Hs, survival). Put the required hinge load next to the 37 kN result. Use the same parameters in the competitor table.

### 2. The Nordic price argument works against the plan as written

Location: "Sector" (Norway paragraph) and "Pricing strategy" (ceiling and cost of electricity bullets).

Why it matters: the plan quotes southern Norwegian prices of 74–82 øre/kWh for Q4 2025. Q4 is the quarter when solar in southern Norway produces almost nothing; an energy investor will spot this immediately and discount the rest of the market section. The pricing section then cites €34/MWh European solar PPAs, says Nordic solar capture prices are low, says Nordic cost of electricity is higher than the Mediterranean €0.062/kWh without a number, and adds that academic estimates for Northern European open-sea solar are "several times higher still". The reader is left with the strong impression that the Nordic product does not pay, and no counter-number.

Suggested fix: replace the Q4 price with a summer or annual solar-weighted capture price for NO1/NO2/NO5. Then give one worked behind-the-meter example for Skien havn or Gunneklevfjorden: estimated LCOE in øre/kWh for that plant versus the host's delivered cost (energy plus grid tariff plus consumption tax), and the PPA price agreed or targeted. If the case relies on avoided grid-connection delay, quantify that too (months of waiting, value of earlier power). Drop the "several times higher still" sentence or move it to the risk section with context.

### 3. No market size an investor can use

Location: "Stage of development and competitive nature", final paragraph.

Why it matters: the plan states there is no reliable market size and relies on a bottom-up pipeline of 6.75 MWp plus 30–50 MW long-term potential at Storavatnet. At €0.30–0.40/W, 6.75 MWp is roughly €2–2.7 million of kit revenue in total. That does not support an equity round. The global and EU solar statistics in "Sector" (664 GW, 3 TW, 65.1 GW, 718 GW by 2030) are irrelevant to sizing and dilute the section; the offshore figures (Spain 4.5–6.5 GW, Dutch 45 GWp by 2050) are for open sea, which is not the near-term product.

Suggested fix: build a simple serviceable market estimate for the segment actually targeted: number of coastal industrial sites and ports in Norway, Sweden and the Baltic with suitable sheltered water, land constraint and a large enough load, times a typical plant size, times an adoption rate. Even a rough SAM (for example "about X sites, Y GW technical, Z MW realistically addressable by 2032") is far stronger than "no reliable size". Cut most of the global solar paragraph.

### 4. Four business models at once, with no stated focus for the investor's money

Location: "How Sunlit Sea makes money" and "How the model changes with scale".

Why it matters: the company proposes to be a developer and owner-operator (capital-heavy, IPP-type returns), a kit supplier (asset-light, product margins), an engineering consultancy (8–12 % of capex), and later an EPC and long-term owner. Investors value these very differently and they compete for the same scarce management time. A two-person team running all of them in 2027–2028 is the single biggest execution concern. It is also unclear whether equity raised at company level will be used to fund plant equity, which most venture-type investors will not want.

Suggested fix: state clearly that the company is a technology and kit supplier, and that own plants are a time-limited means to create references, financed at project level (SPV, debt, Enova, host equity) and sold or refinanced once operating. Show the separation of company equity from project equity. Treat EPC and long-term ownership as options, not stages of a plan.

### 5. Missing unit economics and the funding ask

Location: "Selling prices", "Pricing strategy", "Development phases and investment".

Why it matters: the plan gives a target selling price and a target gross margin "at scale", but not the current or projected cost per watt of a CONNECT float, hinge and mooring interface at prototype, pilot and series volumes. Without a cost curve, the 30–40 % margin is an aspiration. The investment table sums to roughly €1.5–2.5 million plus project finance, but there is no stated round size, use of funds or runway. I understand financials come later, but sections 2–5 should at least name the ask and the milestones it buys.

Suggested fix: add a cost-per-watt table (today, at 3 MW per year, at 30 MW per year) with the main cost drivers (polyurethane mass per float, aluminium plate, casting cycle, hinge). Add one sentence in the objectives: "Sunlit Sea seeks about €X million in 2027 to reach Y by Z."

### 6. Timeline is tight and depends on a prototype still in casting

Location: "Current and future products", "Development phases and investment", "Objectives".

Why it matters: Prototype 4 is in casting at TRL 4–5, design freeze is Q1–Q2 2027 (with Prototype 5 as buffer), and the first plant at Skien havn starts in Q2 2027. That leaves no room for a failed test. Gunneklevfjorden at 3.2 MWp follows in 2028, a ninefold scale-up from the first plant before it has run a full winter. Kit sales in Germany in 2028 also precede TRL 6 completion and certification readiness. Investors will assume slippage of 12 months and recompute.

Suggested fix: show the critical path explicitly with go/no-go gates (design freeze criteria, first-winter results required before Gunneklevfjorden financial close). Present a base case with realistic slack rather than the best case. It is better to promise 2028 and deliver early.

### 7. The competitive table is soft on the strongest competitors and silent on Sunlit Sea's own gaps

Location: "Competitors" and "Unique value proposition".

Why it matters: Ocean Sun has installed references, a Bureau Veritas type certificate and a licensing model; Fred. Olsen 1848 has a strong owner and a DNV review. Sunlit Sea has neither a Gen 2 field reference nor third-party review, and that is not stated in the comparison. Weaknesses listed for Ocean Sun ("calm-to-mild conditions, panels laid flat") are asserted without evidence; Ocean Sun's marketing claims nearshore and offshore operation. "No supplier has a commercial multi-megawatt floating reference on European coastal water" is also true of Sunlit Sea, so it does not differentiate. The UVP sentence claims calm-water floats "fail in waves" and offshore concepts "need costly full protection", which reads as overstatement.

Suggested fix: add a Sunlit Sea row to the table with the same columns, including its own weaknesses. Compare on a few quantified axes (design Hs, certification status, installed MW, cost per watt where known or estimated, installation method). Replace qualitative weaknesses with facts or remove them. Shorten the UVP and drop the "unlike…fails" contrast in favour of the positive claim.

### 8. IP position is weak and the text emphasises it

Location: "Intellectual property"; Strengths and weaknesses table.

Why it matters: "No patent… the field is crowded with prior art" tells an investor that the core idea is not novel and the moat is execution. That can be fine, but the plan undercuts itself by also noting an earlier Gen 1 patent (now irrelevant) and planning an FTO review only in 2027, after kits are designed and possibly after first sales. A strategic investor will worry about infringing someone else's hinge or breakwater-plus-array patents.

Suggested fix: frame the moat positively and concretely: what is hard to copy (casting process yields, polyurethane formulation for seawater, test data set from SUREWAVE, site-adaptation rules, reference record). Bring the FTO review forward to before design freeze or before the round closes. Drop the Gen 1 patent sentence unless it has value.

### 9. Material durability and manufacturing location are open questions presented as plans

Location: "Technology risks and qualification", "Manufacturing", "Value chain".

Why it matters: polyurethane in seawater has known hydrolysis risks (the plan's own sources cite this), and the seawater formulation is still to be chosen. Prototypes are cast in Asia while the plan leans on EU resilience criteria and European-made components. The European casting partner is not named. For a 25-year asset, lenders and insurers will ask for durability evidence before anything else.

Suggested fix: state what ageing and seawater exposure data exist today (Gen 1 field years, any coupon tests) and when the qualification results will be available relative to the first plant. Name candidate European casting partners or state the selection timeline. Clarify that polyurethane durability is the top technical risk and how it is being retired.

### 10. Defensive or self-undermining passages that can be cut or moved

Location: several.

Why it matters: each one is minor; together they shape the tone. Examples: the Gen 1 factory closure explanation on page one; mentioning the North Sea pilot failure twice (Technology risks and Competitive nature); "no published survey of industrial buyers' decision criteria was found"; "a lesson from solar suppliers that expanded into EPC too early"; the long explanation of Norway's state guarantee only applying to large offtakers; "Breakwaters are not Sunlit Sea's product". Also "utility-scale solar" in the mission does not match 0.35–3.2 MW plants.

Suggested fix: move the factory story to the team/history section and frame it as a deliberate switch to an asset-light model. Mention the North Sea connector failure once. Replace "no survey was found" with the planned customer interviews only. Align the mission wording with the actual plant sizes (for example "megawatt-scale").

## 3. Questions I would ask the founders

1. What significant wave height and period is CONNECT designed for without a breakwater, and what have you actually tested at model and full scale?
2. What load does the design case require on the hinge, and how does that compare with the 37 kN ultimate capacity? What fatigue data do you have?
3. What does a Gen 2 float cost to produce today per watt, and what is your cost curve to series volume? Which assumptions drive it?
4. For Skien havn: what is the capex, the expected annual yield in kWh/kWp, the LCOE, the PPA price, and who holds the equity? Is the offtaker contract signed?
5. What is the status of permits, zoning and grid connection at Skien havn and Gunneklevfjorden? Is the Enova support for Gunneklevfjorden a pre-project grant only, or is investment support applied for?
6. How much measured yield and degradation data do you have from Mannheim and Skiftestjørna, and how did the plants handle winter, ice and storms?
7. How much are you raising, at what pre-money expectation, what does it buy, and how many months of runway? How much of it would go into plant equity?
8. Which of the four business models would you drop if you could only pursue one in 2027–2029?
9. Who will run sales, project delivery and operations? Which hires are planned with the round, and what happens if one founder leaves?
10. Have you priced against Ocean Sun and Fred. Olsen 1848 in any actual tender or customer conversation? What did you learn?
11. How dependent is the plan on the EIC Transition award and other grants? What happens to the exposed-site roadmap if it is rejected?
12. What is the evidence that polyurethane survives 25 years in seawater with the formulation you intend to use?
13. Why would a strategic energy company buy kits from you rather than license or copy the concept, given there is no patent?
14. What are the inbound requests from Germany, Türkiye, Italy and Denmark in size and stage? Any letters of intent?
15. How does the German Water Resources Act affect the German kit sales planned for 2028 and the German lake demonstrator?

## 4. Strengths to keep

- Concrete, named sites and counterparties (Skien havn, Hærøya, Haugaland, Storugns) rather than generic pipeline claims.
- Real operating history with Gen 1, including experience of owning, operating and selling power under PPAs. Few competitors at this size have that.
- Sharp understanding of bankability: the barriers section on lenders, insurers, the 2026 DNV standards, IEC drafts and Bureau Veritas certification is one of the best parts and signals maturity.
- Clear permitting analysis for Norway, Sweden and Germany, including the 10 MW licensing threshold that covers the whole near-term plant range.
- Use of standard modules and simple casting, which ties product cost to falling module prices and avoids specialist vessels and steelwork.
- Site-specific breakwater concept: protecting only the exposed sides is a credible cost lever, supported by the SUREWAVE cost breakdown (floats and breakwater about €209/kWp of €900/kWp).
- Honest acknowledgement of weaknesses. Keep the honesty, but move most of it to a risk section where it is paired with mitigations.
- Staged channel strategy (direct sales, then resellers, then licensees) and a modest, itemised development budget. Investors like capital efficiency.
- Sources listed with dates, which makes the market claims checkable.
