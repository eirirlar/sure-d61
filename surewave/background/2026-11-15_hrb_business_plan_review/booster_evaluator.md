# Booster mentor review: KER1 business plan, sections 2–5 (draft of 14 November 2026)

Reviewer perspective: Horizon Results Booster G2M business-plan mentor. Measured against the G2M template (version 29 November 2024), the Module D workshop slides of 13 November 2026 (three pillars, eight checkpoints, section questions on slides 23–34), the mentor notes from the 13 November session, and the four companion files (characterisation table, exploitation roadmap, risk map, Lean Canvas).

## 1. Overall verdict

This is a strong first draft. It covers every template prompt for sections 2–5, uses accredited sources, and is consistent with the companion files on the main points: timeline, pipeline, revenue streams, UVP and competitors. It is weakest on the financial sustainability and reliability pillars. The plan never shows, even in rough numbers, that the price a Nordic host can pay covers the cost of the kit plus the margin. It does not say who the paying customer is at the first two Norwegian plants. It also gives no economic objectives such as revenue, break-even year or market share. The market section is too long (about 2,700 of 6,100 words) and reads as a sector report rather than an argument. Several items belong in sections 6–9 and should move there before the December workshop.

## 2. Top 10 changes, ranked

1. Section 5, Pricing strategy: no unit economics to link price, cost and the buyer's ceiling.
   Problem: the floor (kit cost plus a 30–40 % margin), the benchmark (NREL, SUREWAVE €0.21/W at 10 MW) and the ceiling (grid price behind the meter) are each described, but none of them is quantified for a Nordic plant. A reader cannot tell whether €0.30–0.40/W is profitable, or whether a Skien havn host can afford the resulting cost of electricity. This is the first of the three revenue-model questions (is it profitable?).
   Fix: add one small worked example for a reference Nordic plant (for example 3.2 MWp at Gunneklevfjorden). Give the indicative kit cost per watt with its main cost lines and casting origin, the kit price and gross margin, the total capex per kWp, the specific yield (kWh/kWp), the resulting cost of electricity, and the delivered price behind the meter in NO2 (energy, grid tariff and taxes). Five to eight rows are enough. The full model can stay in the financial annex.

2. Sections 2 and 5: the paying customer at the reference plants is unclear.
   Problem: section 2 says Sunlit Sea will build and operate Skien havn and Gunneklevfjorden. The sales forecast books 2027 revenue for supply and engineering to Skien havn. If Sunlit Sea owns the plant, that is an internal transfer, not revenue. The meeting summary also mentions a possible joint venture with, or investment from, the local solar partner at Skien. The consistency pillar fails here, because revenue streams, model stages and the forecast do not line up.
   Fix: add a short table, one row per plant (Skien havn, Gunneklevfjorden, Storavatnet, Gotland). Columns: owner or SPV, Sunlit Sea's equity share, who pays Sunlit Sea for what (kit, engineering, PPA revenue, sale of the plant), and the planned exit year. Then restate the 2027–2029 forecast so that it counts only external revenue, or label consolidated figures as such.

3. Section 2, Objectives: no economic or financial targets.
   Problem: the template asks for "the goals to be achieved in economic/financial terms", and the slides ask for 3–5-year projections. The objectives are all technical or project milestones.
   Fix: add 3–4 measurable targets for 2027–2031, for example revenue in 2029 and 2031, the year the company reaches positive operating cash flow, MWp supplied per year, gross margin on kit supply, and the size of the equity round. Use ranges if needed, and mark them as subject to the financial annex.

4. Section 4, Market: no sized addressable market and no target share.
   Problem: the draft says plainly that no reliable size exists and relies on a 6.75 MWp pipeline. That is honest, but slide 16 asks to "translate market size and market share into anticipated number of clients". A 6.75 MWp pipeline cannot support the 2030 goal of a 10 MW contract or the long-term kit business.
   Fix: build a simple bottom-up serviceable market. Count the Nordic and Baltic industrial parks, ports and quarries with sheltered water and a large power load (the NVE and Statnett figures and the port list help), multiply by a typical plant size, and apply a realistic capture rate for 2030 and 2032. Show it as a three-level funnel (total, serviceable, obtainable). State each assumption and its source.

5. Section 4, Market: too long, and the evidence is not tied to the argument.
   Problem: the Sector, Stage of development, Barriers and Political subsections run to about 2,000 words, with many figures that do not lead to a decision (global GW totals, Asian pile-mounted plants, Spanish and Dutch offshore scenarios). With sections 6–9 still to come, the plan risks going past the 30-page limit.
   Fix: cut the market section by about 40 %. Keep one paragraph each on global and floating growth, the move from inland water to the coast, and the Norwegian land and grid squeeze. Turn barriers and regulation into one table (barrier, effect on the buyer, Sunlit Sea's response). Close each subsection with one sentence on what it means for Sunlit Sea. Add a concluding line under Porter's five forces giving the overall attractiveness and the strategic consequence (for example: the threat from substitutes is high, so compete only where land is blocked).

6. Section 4, Customers; Section 5, Early adopters: segment 2 (developers and IPPs) has no evidence.
   Problem: every named early adopter is a site host or offtaker (segment 1). No developer or IPP is named as a buyer of kits, but kit sales from 2028 are the main scaling stage. The Booster checkpoints ask for one UVP per segment backed by early-adopter feedback.
   Fix: name the developers, EPC contractors or IPPs already in contact (Prosolar, the Danish lead with its mooring supplier, the German leads through Rixen Cableway) and state what each has said or committed to. If none has yet, say so, and add developer interviews to the early-2027 customer interview round, with the questions they will test (acceptable price per watt, bankability requirements, warranty).

7. Section 5, Pricing and Section 3, Selling prices: key price assumptions are not backed.
   Problem: (a) The 8–12 % engineering fee has no source or customer evidence, and it is high next to typical owner's-engineering shares. (b) The 30–40 % margin is benchmarked against solar-tracker companies, not floating-solar or marine suppliers. (c) The draft does not say how the Generation 1 price per watt compares with the Generation 2 target. The reliability pillar asks for more proof the further the forecast moves from history, and the meeting notes say earlier models failed for this reason.
   Fix: give the Generation 1 price and cost per watt achieved and explain what in Generation 2 changes the cost (standard panels, subcontracted casting, simpler plate). Back the engineering fee with a quote or a comparable offer, or lower it and describe it as a range to be tested. State which casting origin (Asia or Europe) the €0.30–0.40/W assumes. Add a short what-if line: price at €0.25/W, casting cost +30 %, breakwater needed on one side.

8. Sections 2, 3 and 5: content that belongs in sections 6–9.
   Problem: several passages pre-empt later sections and will be duplicated. The founders' roles and the partner list (section 6, Team). Technology qualification, Manufacturing and the development-phase investments (section 7, Operative plan). Peer equity raises and the financing actors (section 9, Resources). The "financial annex will detail" notes (section 8).
   Fix: in sections 2–5, keep a one-line pointer to each topic. Move the detail to the right section when sections 6–9 are drafted: technology qualification, manufacturing and the phase table to section 7, and peer financing benchmarks to section 9. Keep the development-phases table in section 3, which slide 30 asks for, but shorten it to phase, timing, TRL reached and cost.

9. Section 3, Intellectual property: inconsistent with the companion files.
   Problem: the plan says a registered design for the float exists in the EU and Norway. The characterisation table and the roadmap describe a design-registration decision on the hinge by Q3 2027, and the phase table lists design registration as a 2027 cost. The status of the Generation 1 patent (still in force or written off) is also unclear.
   Fix: settle on one fact for each item: what is registered today, what will be filed, by when, and for which part (float or hinge). State whether the Generation 1 European patent is still in force and whether it has any licensing value. Mention that the Intellectual Assets Management service starts in January 2027 to answer how Sunlit Sea can license without owning a patent.

10. Sections 3–5: social and environmental benefits are missing, and there are no figures.
    Problem: the mentor marked the social and environmental angle as something to add, and the characterisation table now covers it: island grids such as Gotland, recyclable aluminium, LCA from SUREWAVE and SuRE, circular concrete breakwaters, reduced evaporation on reservoirs. The business plan leaves all of it out. Slide 18 also asks for graphs and illustrative tables, and the draft has tables only.
    Fix: add a short "Impact" paragraph under Distinctive features or the UVP, with one or two numbers from the LCA work if they are available. Add 3–4 figures: a product photo or CAD render, a value-chain diagram (who supplies what to whom per market), a market or pipeline chart, and a timeline of the scaling stages.

## 3. Section-by-section notes

### Section 2, The organisation

- Mission: clear and focused on the customer. "Utility-scale" does not match plants of 0.35–3.2 MW. Use "industrial-scale" or "megawatt-scale", or explain that utility scale is the long-term goal.
- Company profile: the Generation 1 history is valuable and handled honestly (factory closed because volumes could not carry the cost). Add one line on the lesson for Generation 2. It answers an investor's first question.
- Team paragraph: keep two lines here and move the rest to section 6. That section will need to show an "ambidextrous" team, able to sell and innovate at the same time, and state the hiring plan (sales lead, project staff) and governance.
- Objectives: see top change 3. "Raise growth capital from strategic investors" also needs an amount and a timing (H2 2027 in the roadmap).
- Strengths and weaknesses: balanced and credible. Consider adding opportunities and threats (a full SWOT) or point to Porter in section 4. Several strengths repeat in the competitive-advantage list. Choose one place.
- Success factors: good. Make two of them measurable, for example measured yield within ±10 % of simulation (roadmap M3), and the target cost per watt.

### Section 3, The product/service

- The product table answers the template's main requirement (current or future, launch date). Generation 1 is shown as both "line discontinued" and "On the market". Choose one: "no longer sold; plants in operation".
- Launch dates are clear. The table gives "TRL 4–5"; give one TRL per row and the date of the next TRL step, linked to roadmap milestones M1 and M7.
- The product description is clear and suits a business reader. Add the weight per float, the design wave height range for the 3rd-generation hinge (sheltered and mild-wave in numbers, for example Hs), and the expected design life. A buyer will ask what "mild-wave" means.
- Distinctive features: "37 kN" means little to a business reader without a comparison (for example the load at a given wave height, or the safety factor).
- Selling prices: the price per watt excludes panels. Also give an indicative all-in price per kWp for an installed plant, since that is what hosts and lenders compare.
- Customer portfolio: label each entry as signed, letter of intent or in discussion. Hosts and offtaker candidates are not customers yet. Be precise.
- Value chain: a good start. Raluca asked for a value chain analysis per market (who sits between Sunlit Sea and the customer, what to buy, what to sell, with whom). Turn the list into a diagram, and add Germany and the Baltic as columns.
- Technology risks and qualification: solid, but it is operative-plan content (section 7). The paragraph on the North Sea pilot setback is useful context. Keep one sentence of it here.

### Section 4, The market

- Sector: the figures are sourced and current. The link "value is shifting to solar that supplies a local load directly" is the key point. Lead with it.
- Stage of development: the IEA PVPS TRL 3–5 classification and the "nearshore is open" conclusion work well. Shorten the Asian examples to one sentence.
- Market size: see top change 4. Do not leave the bottom-up figure at 6.75 MWp. Also count the inbound requests (number and MW) to show demand.
- Barriers and regulation: thorough. The German Water Resources Act point raises a question the plan leaves open. If inland coverage is limited and coastal rules are unclear, what are the German kit sales in 2028 based on? Clarify the German target sites (artificial lakes, harbours, exposed lakes) or move Germany later.
- Innovation: the cost structure argument (structure, mooring and installation dominate once modules are cheap) is the strongest analytical paragraph in the draft. Keep it.
- Porter's five forces: present, which the slides ask for. Add the overall verdict (see top change 5).
- Customers: there is one value proposition per segment, which meets the checkpoint. The PPA behaviour paragraph is useful. The planned customer interviews in early 2027 fit the "test assumptions" checkpoint. Give the number of interviews, the target profiles and the hypotheses to test.
- Competitors: the table is complete and fair. The "Weaknesses against Sunlit Sea CONNECT" column sometimes lists features rather than weaknesses (for example Ocean Sun's "panels laid flat"). Several competitors have field references and certification that Sunlit Sea does not yet have. Add a row for Sunlit Sea CONNECT in the same columns so the reader can compare honestly.
- Defending the advantage: matches the slide-25 checklist item by item. That is good, but it repeats the IP section. Keep the list and cut the IP repetition.
- UVP: consistent with the characterisation table and the Lean Canvas. Open with the short headline from the Lean Canvas ("Floating solar for coastal water that bends with the waves") before the long sentence.

### Section 5, The business model and marketing strategy

- How Sunlit Sea makes money: the four streams match the Lean Canvas. Show the expected revenue share of each stream by stage (2027, 2029, 2031). This also answers the question "does profitability grow with volume?".
- Model change with scale: the table is clear and the EPC caution is sensible. The meeting notes place EPC at about 100 MW; the table says "tens of MW to 100 MW from about 2030". Align the two, and state the plant size or balance-sheet threshold that triggers each stage.
- Breakwater supply: clear, and it answers the mentor's risk note. Add who carries the performance warranty in each of the three routes, because bankability depends on it.
- Manufacturing: belongs to section 7. The Europe-versus-Asia choice and the reason for it (Raluca's question on risk 1) should be stated, with the cost effect, and tied to the pricing assumption.
- Pricing strategy: see top changes 1 and 7. The cost-of-electricity bullet admits that Nordic costs are higher, without a number. That gap is the plan's main reliability weakness.
- Segment and early adopters: the reasons for choosing the segment are good, and they answer the template question. Add the Gotland and Rixen Cableway leads to the early-adopter paragraph so it matches the Lean Canvas.
- Geography: a clear table. Denmark and the UK appear as sources of demand elsewhere but not here. Add them or say why they come later.
- Channels: phased step by step, which meets the checkpoint. Add the cost and expected conversion of each channel (slide 16: net revenue after distribution cost), at least in words.
- Promotion budget: the template asks how much will be spent on advertising. "About €0.3 million covering sales, permitting and promotion" does not answer it. Give the promotion figure separately (even "€20–30k in 2027").
- Sales forecast: 2028 and 2030 revenues are "to be set". 2028 is the year Gunneklevfjorden is commissioned and the first kits ship, so it is the most important year in the plan. Give ranges now, even if they are rough. Add a "contracted / probable / possible" column.
- Branding: fine and short.
- Sources: good list. Add sources for the claims that have none: module prices in October 2026, tracker-company gross margins, the Bureau Veritas certificate, Statnett queue figures, BlueNewables. Use numbered in-text references so a reviewer can trace each figure.

## 4. Things that work well and should stay

- The plain, factual style. A reader with some business sense can follow it, as slide 20 asks.
- The honest weaknesses (no patent, no field proof, limited cash, Generation 1 factory closure). They make the plan credible.
- One value proposition per customer segment, plus the overall UVP consistent with the companion files.
- The cost-structure argument in "Role of innovation and technology", backed by NREL and SUREWAVE D7.4.
- The scaling table (developer, then technology supplier, then EPC, then owner-operator) and the explicit caution on taking on EPC too early.
- The three breakwater supply routes, which answer the mentor's earlier risk note.
- Channels added step by step, and a customer-interview round planned to test assumptions.
- The competitor table and the five-forces table, both of which the slides ask for.
- The phased development table with timing and indicative investment per phase, which slide 30 asks for.
- The sourced, recent market data and the source list.
