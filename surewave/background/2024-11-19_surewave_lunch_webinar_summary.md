## 1. Project overview — Balram Panjwani (SINTEF)

- SUREWAVE: "Structural reliable offshore floating PV solution integrating circular concrete floating breakwater". EU-funded, started two years earlier, then planned to end in September 2025 (later extended to September 2026). Seven partners from industry and research in four European countries.
- Moving from sheltered (landlocked) to offshore floating PV brings currents, high winds and extreme waves. The consequences are extreme load events, constant fatigue on joints and connections, saltwater corrosion, UV degradation, biofouling, and micro-cracking and misalignment from constant motion. Because the technology is at low TRL, design standards, guidelines and regulation are lacking.
- Design targets: wind 25 m/s, waves up to 14 m, current 1.2 m/s. Materials close to 100 % circular, improved service life, and assessment of CO2 footprint, effects on marine life and levelised cost of energy.
- Concept: an external floating breakwater made of new circular materials protects the internal floating PV against the critical short waves. Long-period waves are not stopped; the whole system follows them.
- Three research pillars: an innovative breakwater concept; sustainable materials in the breakwater; intelligent predictive modelling tools.
- Sites: Baltic Sea (mild), Western Mediterranean (also mild, with different wave and wind parameters) and Greater North Sea (harsh), chosen to cover different conditions and wave directionalities.
- Objective 1, design: breakwater concept, coupling between breakwater and internal PV including mechanical joints, anchoring and mooring, and layout optimisation. Layouts compared in a matrix of shapes (circular, polygon, triangle, oval) against design parameters. The internal floating PV is Sunlit Sea's system (marine-grade aluminium, nearly fully circular, easy to install, low cost), although the SUREWAVE concept is generic. Breakwater parameters studied: width, length, height, shape, with or without keel, under various sea states.
- Objective 2, materials: replace conventional concrete (high cement content, a large CO2 source) with circular high-performance concrete and lightweight aggregate concrete for the shell, and replace the usual EPS (plastic) core with a new circular cellular material. Raw materials tested include recycled glass, lightweight aggregate, recycled concrete aggregate, slag, fly ash and other waste streams. Samples are tested for compressive strength, density, mechanical, physical and durability properties, including corrosion and biofouling.
- Objective 3, modelling: coupled aero-hydrodynamic modelling of motions and loads; machine-learning prediction of concrete properties from a database of mix compositions (cement, fine and coarse aggregates, fillers); structural integrity modelling (finite elements) for remaining lifetime. These, together with monitoring data (corrosion sensors and other sensors), feed a structural health management system for remaining fatigue life, inspection and maintenance planning.
- Testing: the weakest connections were identified (PV module to module, breakwater to breakwater, breakwater to PV) and tested for fatigue at SINTEF; basin tests at MARIN at 1:10 scale, used to validate the numerical models; a concrete breakwater prototype (high-performance concrete shell, lightweight aggregate core) to be placed in the sea in Spain, at ACCIONA's test site in the Port of Gijón, for 10–12 months.
- Key takeaways: offshore floating PV for North Sea-type conditions; a breakwater of circular, sustainable materials; machine-learning prediction of concrete properties; validation through lab, basin and prototype tests; a techno-economic assessment of the full concept at the end.

## 2. Challenges of offshore floating solar — Eirik Larsen (Sunlit Sea)

- Key challenges: harsh environment (high waves, strong winds, saltwater corrosion) causing severe failure modes; anchoring and mooring that must withstand hard-to-predict dynamic forces and are expensive; biofouling as an operational risk (cleaning trips); a high risk of economic infeasibility, as seen to some extent with floating wind; energy output and reliability affected by wave-induced mismatch losses in strings of panels, in some conditions requiring sheltering or disconnection.
- Sunlit Sea's solution: marine-grade aluminium floats with solar panels laminated on top; corrosion- and impact-resistant; good cooling for higher yield; pre-assembled modules, quick to install (an onshore project was installed in one day); configurable layouts (square, rectangular, round); no microplastics; flat and barely visible from a distance, relevant given resistance to the visual impact of wind power in Norway.
- Sunlit Sea's role in SUREWAVE: developing the wave attenuation structures and the mooring that connects breakwater and floating PV together with Clement and the other partners; testing in simulation, wave tanks and offshore; test data have been used to improve the design, for instance the hinges; investigating eco-friendly materials for the breakwater.
- Expected impact: longer system life in harsh conditions, access to open-sea sites otherwise seen as non-bankable, higher output through lower mismatch losses, less maintenance, and a scalable, configurable solution. One-sided protection, against the most critical wave direction only, is a possible variant.
- Next steps: testing in real projects, and the project's economic assessment of the whole system, which decides whether offshore sites are prioritised over nearshore or onshore ones.

## 3. Floating breakwater design — Thomas Pehlke (Clement Germany)

- Purpose: an external barrier so that the floating PV elements do not need strengthening, limiting loads on the PV for a long service life and low maintenance. Without protection the floating PV could not withstand extreme offshore conditions; high-frequency waves are the main risk.
- Design conditions: three sites (mild, moderate, harsh), analysed statistically from hindcast data; combinations of wave period and significant wave height derived with the JONSWAP spectrum and a steepness criterion, since the steepest waves are the most critical for both breakwater and PV.
- Layout: an outer breakwater ring of trapezoidal modules (avoiding curved elements to simplify production) around the internal PV, with connections for continuous force transfer. The circular layout minimises force peaks and material use.
- Module: structural concrete shell around a lightweight core (needed for buoyancy), local strengthening at the connections and internal walls. New sustainable materials with recycled aggregates and partial cement replacement (ground granulated blast-furnace slag, fly ash), compared with the conventional build of reinforced concrete with a polystyrene core.
- Cross-section chosen by CFD for good wave absorption and producibility with conventional technology: element length up to 20 m (depending on ring diameter), width 5 m, height 2.5 m including 0.8 m keels, weight about 124 t. Initial densities 1.9 t/m³ for the shell and 300 kg/m³ for the core (targets of the materials work package; actual results do not deviate significantly). Stability shown by centre of gravity, centre of buoyancy and metacentric height. Considerably larger than typical marina breakwater pontoons.
- Wave transmission: in the range critical for the floating PV (periods 1–3 s), maximum transmission 0.4. CFD shows clearly reduced waves behind the breakwater, without counting the stabilising effect of the ring and the anchoring.
- Connections between modules: transfer forces and moments in all directions except rotation about one axis, where a hinge is used because of the long cantilever. Rubber buffers take compression and shear; coupling ropes with threaded terminals take tension. Offshore rope sizes are far larger than for marina pontoons; the number of ropes varies by site.
- Anchoring: chains to driven anchor piles, also usable in deep water. Not too stiff, so that breakwater motion and forces stay moderate. Chain lengths and pile positions are chosen so that, even at maximum displacement, the chain keeps slack and loads the pile horizontally. Chain length, distance to piles and number of piles vary by site (example shown for the Baltic Sea).
- PV-to-breakwater connection: the floating PV has no separate anchoring and hangs from the ring through elastic polypropylene ropes, so it does not follow every breakwater motion. A transition element acts as load distributor: few, large connections to the breakwater; many, smaller ones to the PV. SINTEF has tested these joints for capacity and durability.

## Questions and answers

- Cost optimisation in design and materials: Thomas — module size and weight were limited so that modules can be built on land, lifted, transported and launched with conventional equipment, without dry docks. On materials, recycled content was chosen for environmental reasons (cement is limited and carbon-intensive), so cost optimisation is not the only criterion.
- Expected gain on wave-induced mismatch losses (two attendees): Eirik — not a focus of SUREWAVE, but Sunlit Sea studied it with the Institute for Energy Technology in another project, using a model based on Dörenkämper et al. (2019) and Python simulations with significant wave height as input. The loss depends strongly on string length and number of panels; in the operating range of Sunlit Sea's panels it ranged from 0 to 12–13 %. As an explicit guesstimate: around 10 % offshore without protection, perhaps 2–3 % with the breakwater, since not all waves are damped.
- Environmental monitoring and public findings: Heiko Keller (IFEU) — IFEU leads the sustainability assessment, including environmental impacts over all life-cycle steps. Long-term monitoring needs a deployed plant followed for years, which is outside a technology development project, but would be interesting later.
- LCOE target: Balram — the proposal target is 54 €/MWh; the assessment had not been done yet.
- Typical wave attenuation: Thomas — the target is to limit high-frequency waves with periods of 1–3 s, which the breakwater does well; for longer waves the whole system floats with the waves, so no breaking is needed.
- Wind turbine on the breakwater and load capacity (an attendee who chairs a company producing small wind turbines): Thomas — the item in the photo was an instrument for measuring wind speed, not a turbine. The capacity for equipment on the breakwater (transformers, inverters, small vertical wind turbines) has not been assessed; it would need a separate assessment, or separate wider platforms integrated in the system. Asked why there are no solar panels on the breakwater, Thomas said that would make the system very expensive. The attendee offered to test one of his turbines on a pilot; Balram noted that this was outside the project scope.
- Lighter concrete: the same attendee recommended Minolite. María (ACCIONA) confirmed that the project already uses Minolite, the recycled lightweight aggregate made from glass waste (D4.1), in both the lightweight aggregate concrete and the cellular concrete core, and that it works very well.
- 2D or 3D models, and peak periods above 7.5 s (a PhD student at the University of Malta working on floating breakwaters): Thomas — the simulations were two-dimensional. Site maxima: Baltic Tp 6.5 s with Hs 4.4 m; North Sea Tp up to 13.6 s with Hs 11 m. All combinations were simulated; at about 7 s and longer the breakwater has no effect, and doubling its width to 10 m would not change that, because the pontoon is too light relative to the wave loads.
- Pre-stressing for a thinner, lighter shell: Thomas — possible in principle, but a compression zone is still needed, and a certain weight is wanted for floating behaviour and transmission. Pre-stressing would probably make production more costly than ordinary reinforcement.
- Comment from the same attendee: carbon-free cement will probably be available soon, at a somewhat higher price. Balram: CO2 and cost calculations will be done in the project's sustainability assessment.
