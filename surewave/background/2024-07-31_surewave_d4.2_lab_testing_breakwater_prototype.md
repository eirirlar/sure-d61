**EXECUTIVE SUMMARY**

The document herein reports the activities carried out for the development, validation and testing at the laboratory level of the breakwater prototype.

This deliverable is an outcome of Task 4.3 and Task 4.4. Various tests were conducted on the outer shell and the interface between the outer shell and the inner core. The testing includes the assessment of the durability properties of the circular concrete solutions developed for the shell of the breakwater (High-Performance Concrete: HPC) and Lightweight Aggregate Concrete: LWAC) and the mechanical performance of the interface between the outer shell and the inner core of the breakwater, which will be critical to ensure the mechanical integrity of the breakwater component.

Chapter 1 covers the deliverable with an introduction and outlines the objectives and content of the deliverable.

Chapter 2 describes the tests and results performed to assess the durability assessment of the circular concrete solutions developed for the shell of the breakwater.

Chapter 3 describes the production of the small prototypes and the tests and results carried out to assess their mechanical performance.

Chapter 4 presents the concluding remarks and the selection of the circular concrete solutions for the breakwater prototype according to the results obtained in the previous chapters. From a durability point of view, HPC appears to be the best solution for water resistance since no water penetrated. LWAC with CEM III and the waterproofing admixture Krystaline Add1 appear to be the best solution for chloride resistance. From a mechanical point of view, HPC formulation appears to be the best solution as it showed much better performances, both for static and dynamic loading and even when accounting for the strength-to-weight ratio.

#  Introduction

The document herein reports the activities carried out for the development, validation and testing at the laboratory level of the breakwater prototype.

The testing includes the assessment of the durability properties of the circular concrete solutions developed for the shell of the breakwater as well as the mechanical performance of the interface between the outer shell and the inner core of the breakwater, which will be critical to ensure the mechanical integrity of the breakwater component.

As described in Deliverable D4.1 \[1\], two types of concrete were developed and optimized for the shell of the breakwater

1.  Lightweight aggregate concrete (LWAC)

2.  High-performance concrete (HPC).

In addition to this, Non-structural cellular lightweight concrete was investigated for the inner core.

The present work comprises:

- Durability assessment of the optimal circular concrete mix designs selected for the shell of the breakwater (HPC and LWAC).

- Development and mechanical performance testing of small-scale breakwater prototypes, featuring a shell made of either High-Performance Concrete (HPC) or Lightweight Aggregate Concrete (LWAC) with an inner core of cellular concrete.

- Concluding remarks from the experimental tests carried out and selection of the optimal circular concrete solutions for the shell of the breakwater prototype.

# Durability assessment of the optimal circular concrete solutions selected for the shell of the breakwater

For concrete floating structures, strict durability requirements are required as they are in contact with seawater. Such severe exposure conditions can cause concrete to deteriorate from the combined effects of chemical and physical processes such as sulphate attack, corrosion of embedded rebars, etc.

To help prevent premature deterioration and guarantee long-term performance, durability testing of the circular concrete solutions developed for the shell of the breakwater was carried out to evaluate water and chloride resistance penetration.

Sections 2.1 and 2.2 describe the methodology followed, section 2.3 the circular concrete solutions tested, section 2.4 the production of the small prototypes and section 2.5 the results obtained.

## Water penetration resistance (EN 12390-8). 

Water penetration resistance was measured when the concrete specimens were 28 days old according to Standard EN 12390-8:2019 \[3\]. This standard specifies a method for determining the depth of penetration of water under pressure in hardened concrete.

Concrete cylindrical specimens of size 150 mm diameter with 300 mm height were tested as it can be seen in [Figure 1](#_Ref170465602).

Water is applied under pressure at 500 ± 50kPa to the surface of concrete for 72 ± 2h. After the pressure has been applied for the specified time, the specimen is removed from the apparatus and the excess water of the face on which the water pressure is applied is wiped. Then the specimen is split in half, perpendicularly to the face on which the water pressure is applied, by indirect tensile test and the depth of penetration of the waterfront is marked and measured.

![P323#yIS1](media/image4.jpeg){width="3.134300087489064in" height="1.9653291776027997in"} ![P323#yIS2](media/image5.png){width="2.3081977252843395in" height="1.9921937882764655in"}

[]{#_Ref170465602 .anchor}Figure 1. Penetration of water under pressure test

The maximum and mean penetration depth of water under pressure is measured in each specimen (both halves). The average penetration depth is calculated by dividing the area enclosed by the penetration front by the diameter of the specimen, as shown in [Figure 2](#_Ref102642339).

## Chloride penetration resistance (NT Build 492)

Chloride penetration resistance was measured according to the nordtest method NT Build 492 \[4\]. This method specifies a procedure for the determination of the chloride migration coefficient in concrete from non-steady-state migration experiments.

Cylindrical specimens with a diameter of 100 mm and a thickness of 50 mm, sliced from cast cylinders of 100mm diameter with 200 mm height were tested. The arrangement of the migration set-up is shown in [Figure 3](#_Ref173326479). Three specimens were tested for each concrete mix design as can be seen in [Figure 4](#_Ref170732760).

The concrete specimen is preconditioned in saturated Ca(OH)~2~ solution under vacuum and then sealed to only allow chloride to penetrate at the sample ends. Subsequently, the sample is placed in a plastic container with reservoirs on each end; one surface is in contact with the catholyte solution (10% NaCl) and the other is in contact with the anolyte solution (0.3M NaOH). The electrodes (cathode and anode) are stainless steel sheets immersed in the solution, to force the ingress of chloride ions. The cathode is connected to the negative pole and the anode to the positive pole of the power supply, and an external electrical field, the potential difference between the two electrodes, is applied across the specimen to drive the chloride migration.

![P332#yIS1](media/image7.png){width="2.513270997375328in" height="1.8655205599300086in"} ![P332#yIS2](media/image8.png){width="3.7534437882764653in" height="1.8055588363954507in"}

[]{#_Ref173326479 .anchor}Figure 3. Arrangement of the migration set-up

At the end of the test, the specimen is removed from the plastic container and the sample is axially split in half and a silver nitrate solution is sprayed onto one of the freshly split sections. White silver nitrate precipitates where chloride ions are present. The chloride penetration depth is then measured from the visible white silver chloride precipitation, after which the chloride migration coefficient (D~Cl~) can be calculated from this penetration depth, sample thickness, exposure time, voltage, and average sample temperature.

![[]{#_Ref170732760 .anchor}Figure 4. Concrete specimens with white silver chloride penetration, after the end of the test](media/image9.png){alt="P335#yIS1" width="1.8671062992125984in" height="3.8732524059492563in"}

## Circular concrete solutions tested

The circular concrete solutions tested were those that were developed and optimised for the external shell of the breakwater. Two types of concrete were developed: lightweight aggregate concrete (LWAC) and high-performance concrete (HPC). The optimised mix recipes were included in deliverable D4.1 \[1\]: Sample 17 (with CEM I) and Sample 18 (with CEM III) for LWAC and Sample 2 for HPC.

In addition, an admixture was included in all the optimal mix recipes to waterproof and increase the durability of concrete. Krystaline Add1 \[2\] is a state-of-the-art waterproofing crystalline C-S-H technology admixture. It has been designed to waterproof concrete and increase its durability by using hydrophilic crystalline technology to enhance the hydration of the cement paste.

Details of the tested concrete mix recipes are shown in [Table 1](#_Ref170734942) and [Table 2](#_Ref170734953) below.

# Mechanical performance of the interface between the outer shell and the inner core of the breakwater

This section describes the testing at a laboratory level carried out to assess the mechanical performance of the interface between the outer shell and the inner core of the breakwater, which will be critical to assure the mechanical integrity of the breakwater component.

To characterise the performances, a series of mechanical tests were performed on representative small prototypes of the breakwater made of a structural concrete shell and cellular lightweight concrete core.

The results of these tests and the production of the small prototypes are summarised hereinafter.

## Experimental test matrix and small prototype definition

A series of mechanical tests were planned between ACCIONA and SINTEF to characterise the behaviour of the concrete solutions developed in Task 4.1 and Task 4.2 and found in Deliverable D4.1 \[1\]. Small prototypes of 600 mm x 150 mm x 100 mm and 300 mm x 150 mm x 100 mm were cast by ACCIONA and sent to SINTEF for testing. The small prototypes were made of a core layer of cellular concrete, surrounded by a shell layer made of either HPC or LWAC. The nominal thickness of the beam shell was set to 2 cm thick although the thicknesses can vary from one sample to another (see [Table *9*](#_Ref166498831) and [Table *11*](#_Ref167271116) section [3.3](#evaluation-of-the-static-mechanical-performances-three-point-static-bending-tests)) and have a strong effect on the mechanical properties. A sketch of the small prototypes is given in [Figure 6](#_Ref166492792).

![[]{#_Ref166492792 .anchor}Figure 6. Geometry of the concrete beams tested (Dimensions mm)](media/image15.png)

Each of the two small prototype variations (HPC shell and LWAC shell) was subjected to a series of mechanical tests that are summarised in [Table 8](#_Ref166493047) below. Four types of mechanical tests were performed, as described in the table, and each tests were repeated a minimum of 3 times. Three-point bending was performed to evaluate the mechanical properties of the small prototypes, while cyclic bending was performed to measure the dynamic response and the amount of permanent strain in the specimens during the loading-unloading phases. ## Evaluation of the static mechanical performances: Three-point static bending tests

This section describes the bending setup and the results of the three-point bending tests performed on the HPC and LWAC beams to evaluate their static mechanical performance.

### Three-point bending setup

Three-point bending tests were performed to evaluate the overall mechanical properties of the HPC and LWAC beams. The machine used for these tests was an Instron with 250kN capacity and the test setup is illustrated in [Figure 9](#_Ref166493946). The beams were placed on steel supports of 50 x 50 mm square shape and 5mm thickness. The corners of the squares were filleted with an 11mm radius. The distance between the centre of the two supports was 470mm. The indenter used to apply the force on the centre of the specimen had a similar shape as the supports. The bending tests were performed at a constant indenter velocity rate of 1mm/min, ensuring quasi-static test conditions.

![[]{#_Ref166493946 .anchor}Figure 9. Experimental setup used during the three-point bending tests](media/image220.png)

Each test was run until failure occurred in the specimens, as depicted in [Figure 10](#_Ref166494231). Most of the time, failure is initiated from the bottom of the beam, in the middle, where the tensile forces are maximum.

![P664#yIS1](media/image20.png){width="4.001013779527559in" height="1.2631069553805774in"}

![[]{#_Ref166494231 .anchor}Figure 10 . Post-failure picture of an HPC beam (Up) and LWAC beam (Down) submitted to a three-point bending test](media/image21.png){alt="P665#yIS1" width="3.972746062992126in" height="1.2352755905511812in"}

During the tests, the displacements of the indenter and the beams were monitored using the DIC technique. To this end, a speckle pattern was painted on each of the specimens, and its evolution was followed by a Basler camera. The acquisition system recorded images at a 4.5 Hz frequency with an average resolution of 900×2980. The acquisition system of the camera was synchronized with the tensile machine data acquisition. All recorded images were post-processed using the eCorr software \[7\]. Originally, the objective of the DIC was to provide full-field strain measurement during the test, using a digital grid, however, the results were not conclusive and the subset tracking method of the DIC was used instead to get accurate measurements of the displacements during the tests (see [Figure 11](#_Ref166495404)). Note that the curves presented in the results sections have been post-processed, to account for the offsets that can occur during the initial positioning of the machine.

## Effect of the thermal cycling on the three-point static bending tests

Three-point static bending tests were also performed to assess the effect of the thermal cycle on the degradation of the mechanical properties of the concrete. Three repetitions of each test were performed for both the Thermal cycled LWAC beams and HPC beams. Only the strong direction was investigated in this study.

The beams were subjected to 1-month thermal cycling. The beams (three prototypes of HPC and three prototypes of LWAC) were placed into a climate test chamber (see [Figure 15](#_Ref171201735)) to simulate different types of seawater temperatures. This chamber is fully automatic controlled, and it is programmed to perform continuous thermal cycles ranging from -15°C to + 60°C. The thermal cycles carried out on the HPC and LWAC beams consisted of lowering the temperature of the beams from 15 °C to 1°C in 3 days. Then the temperature was kept constant at 1°C for 5 days. Afterwards, the temperature was raised to 30°C in 3 days and remained at 30°C for 5 days. Finally, the temperature was lowered to 1°C in 3 days and the same sequence was repeated. The humidity was kept in 100% during the entire experiment.

## Three-point cyclic bending tests

This section explains the three-point cyclic bending setup and the results of the three-point cyclic bending tests performed on the HPC and LWAC beams.

### Three-point cyclic bending setup

Cyclic tests were performed on the same machine as the quasi-static tests: An Instron 250 kN. The setup for the tests is depicted in [Figure 16](#_Ref166675138) and more info on the support and indenter geometries are given in section [3](#three-point-bending-setup) of the present report.

[]{#_Ref166675138 .anchor}Figure 16. Picture of the cyclic three-point bending setup

During the tests, the prototypes were subjected to load-controlled triangular cycles programmed into the machine. Three sequences were implemented in succession, each with a different maximum load amplitude. The first cycle amplitude ranged from 5-30% of the maximum peak measured during the quasistatic tests (see sections 3.3.2 and 3.3.3 for static results). The second cycle amplitude was set to 5-60%, and the third to 5-90%. Finally, a ramp was applied to induce failure of the specimens at the end of the last cycle sequence. Each sequence consisted of 10 cycles with a 10-second period. A time history of the load imposed during the tests is provided in [Figure 17](#_Ref166675594) in the case of the HPC tested in the weak and strong direction and in [Figure 18](#_Ref166675596) in the case of the LWAC beams in the weak and strong direction. Since the ultimate strength varied from LWAC and HPC as well as between strong and weak directions, the maximum loads were adapted accordingly.

## Evaluation of the interface properties: Pullout tests

This section describes the setup and the results of the pullout tests performed on the HPC and LWAC beams.

### Pullout test setup

Pullout tests were performed to determine the mechanical performance of the interface between the core material and the shell material. To this end, the beam used in the three-point bending tests was machined to free the sides of the shell layer from the inside core. Steel inserts were machined to be gripped in the machine and they were glued to the shell with an Epoxy ESK 50 glue, having 20 MPa of tensile strength (More than 10 time the compressive strength of the core materials that is around 1 MPa, see Deliverable D4.1 \[1\]) ensuring than failure would occur in the specimens.

# Conclusions

Deliverable 4.2 summarises the results obtained by the SUREWAVE team involved in the development, validation and testing at the laboratory level of the durability properties and mechanical performance of the breakwater prototype. It mainly consists of activities performed during Task 4.3. As a part of this deliverable, we rigorously tested the mechanical performance and durability properties of the outer shell and the interface between the outer shell and inner core.

Concerning the [durability assessment]{.underline}, water and chloride resistance experiments were carried out on the circular concrete solutions developed and optimized for the external shell of the breakwater in task 4.1 and task 4.2 and presented in Deliverable D4.1 \[1\]. Two types of concrete were developed: lightweight aggregate concrete (LWAC) and high-performance concrete (HPC). In addition, an admixture, Krystaline Add1 \[2\], was included in all the optimal mix recipes to waterproof and increase the durability of concrete.

Based on the results obtained, all the circular concrete solutions tested show very good water and chloride resistance penetration. This fact is expected as the concrete solutions developed for the shell of the breakwater were designed taking into recommendations from National and International Codes and Standards (ACI, EN-206, Spanish Structural Code, etc.) to guarantee low permeability. Any mix design that reduces permeability will improve durability, as the passage of water through the concrete will be reduced. Specifically, the concrete mixes were developed using low water-cement ratios (\<0.40), minimum cement content of 400kg/m^3^ and sulfate-resistant cement.

In addition, it is proven that Krystaline Add 1 provides excellent waterproofing performance. This effect is well noted in the LWAC solution with CEM III. In fact, the mean water penetration depth obtained for LWAC-CEM III+KRY was 0.6mm compared with 8.2mm for LWAC-CEM III, and a reduction of 50% of the chloride diffusion coefficient was also observed.

Regarding the [mechanical performance]{.underline}, several mechanical tests were performed on representative beams of LWAC and HPC materials shell combined with the core of cellular lightweight concrete materials to evaluate the performances of the solutions developed. Bending tests, cyclic tests, pullout tests and drop tower impact tests were presented in the report and discussed. The main conclusions are summarized hereinafter:

- The static three-point bending tests show good mechanical performance of both LWAC and HPC compounds although the HPC has much higher mechanical performance overall with an increase of more than 55% of the strength measured from the tests. HPC also outperforms the LWAC if the strength-to-weight ratio is considered by 50%.

- The effect of thermal cycling on the mechanical properties of the beams was found to be very negligible.

- Cyclic tests performed on the beams show noticeable permanent strain induced during the deformation in tensile areas, probably due to cracking, as it is generally expected in concrete, and for both LWAC and HPC concrete.

- P The pullout test revealed that the interfaces in both LWAC and HPC beams exhibited very low mechanical properties. This outcome is primarily attributed to the inherently low mechanical properties of the core cellular concrete rather than the interfaces between the cellular concrete and HPC, or between the cellular concrete and LWAC. This result was anticipated, given that the cellular concrete serves merely as a filler and does not bear any structural responsibilities.

- Finally, the drop tower tests show that although the two concrete solutions are considered as brittle, the ability to dissipate incident kinetic energy is relatively high, due to the ability of the material to develop cracks which will dissipate energy until complete failure. Again, the HPC appears to have the best performance during impact, with a higher peak force observed during the tests.

To conclude, from a durability point of view, all the concrete solutions tested showed extremely high water and chloride resistance after 28 days of curing. Regarding water resistance, HPC appears to be the best solution since no water penetrated. Concerning chloride resistance, the best sample appears to be LWAC with CEM III + KRY.

From a mechanical point of view, the HPC formulation appears to be the best solution to be used as a breakwater shell as it has much better performances, both for static and dynamic loading and even when accounting for the strength-to-weight ratio.

**Based on these mechanical tests it is concluded that the HPC is the most suitable material for the shell.** The next step includes the design and development of the breakwater with a reduced scale (at least 1m wide x 0.5 height x 2m length) with the concrete solutions selected in this deliverable.
