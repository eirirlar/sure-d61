**EXECUTIVE SUMMARY**

This deliverable, a result of Task 5.2, discusses the hydrodynamic and aerodynamic analysis of the SUREWAVE FPV, both with and without a breakwater.

Initially, 2D CFD simulations were conducted to understand the hydrodynamic behaviour. Additionally, the plan was to perform 3D CFD simulations in severe sea states, but 3D CFD proved to be computationally demanding. Therefore, for the hydrodynamic aspect, we employed low to mid-fidelity, efficient linear methods across all sea states. CFD simulations were conducted to obtain the viscous damping forces affecting sway, heave, and roll motions on a single breakwater, and these forces were subsequently integrated into our methodology. The study involved four stages: examining the numerical settings needed for calculating the motion response of coupled FPV modules in waves, validating these models with Stadt towing tank test results provided by project partner SIS, investigating connector loading in FPV system with increasing float size or number with the floating breakwater, and deriving mooring design loads to assess if the design could withstand extreme metocean conditions.

In parallel, mooring and interconnector loads for the breakwater ring are quantified using a combination of wave diffraction and time domain simulations. Designs are assessed for the 10MW and 2MW designs for three predefined field sites (Baltic, Mediterranean, and North Sea). Results showed that proof loads of the mooring lines were exceeded for the Baltic Sea, while maximum tensions for the Mediterranean and North Sea sites were well below proof loads (hence designs could be considered alternative). Following these insights, the mooring designs were optimized using a combination of time domain simulations, surrogate modelling and genetic algorithm based optimization techniques to reduce costs whilst ensuring structural integrity. This resulted in optimized mooring designs with up to 56% reduction in mooring weight.

The aerodynamic simulation of the FPV was conducted both with and without a breakwater. A correlation was developed to estimate lift and drag forces as a function of the number of PV modules. CFD simulations were used to understand how breakwaters can enhance FPV strength and performance under specific wind conditions. The impact of breakwater placement and angles of attack on lift and drag coefficients is presented. Additionally, the CFD simulations helped determine the required gap between the breakwater and the solar panel modules in worst-case scenarios. These comprehensive aerodynamic and hydrodynamic analyses will be used for design optimization. The data will be also used for structural integrity assessment and future design improvements.

# Introduction

In the SUREWAVE project, an innovative concept of a Floating Photo-Voltaic (FPV) system protected by an external floating breakwater is developed. An initial design of a 10MW system has been prepared as part of WP3 (deliverable D3.2-floating breakwater design and D3.2-Anchoring and connections to PV system). The capability of this design to withstand the expected environmental conditions needs to be confirmed by means of numerical simulations, supported by model tests. This report describes the results of numerical simulations that are performed to:

1.  []{#_Ref160467093 .anchor}Investigate numerical settings that are needed for the calculation of the motion response of coupled PV modules in waves with low-fidelity linear diffraction models (chapter 2).

2.  Validate the results of low-fidelity linear diffraction models with model test results (response of 5 coupled PV modules in waves) from the Stadt towing tank that are kindly made available to the project (chapter 3).

3.  Investigate the connector loading in PV fields with increasing size/number of floats and in presence of a floating breakwater (chapter 4).

4.  Derive mooring design loads that can be used to assess if the design is capable of withstanding the metocean (wave) conditions that are described in D2.1-Use case scenario for typical, rough location (chapter 5).

5.  Quantify aerodynamic loads onto the floating PV system (chapter 6)

Conclusions and recommendations for further design steps are presented in chapter [7](#conclusions).

The numerical simulation setup has also been used as input to the first SUREWAVE wave basin tests to select suitable loading conditions of the PV modules and environmental conditions (D6.2-report on basin test results).

# Linear diffraction analysis of coupled PV modules

In this chapter suitable settings are investigated to obtain converged results for the loads and motions on multiple coupled PV modules using linear diffraction analysis (MARIN's DIFFRAC program, see appendix E). First a single panel is investigated (section [2.1](#one-module)). Then two panels are investigated since this introduces additional hydrodynamic complexity due to the gap between the floats (possible resonant water motions). Finally, the effect of marine growth on the motion response and hinge loads is investigated.

A linear diffraction analysis was done for the PV module. Such an analysis assumes a non-viscous rotation-less flow (potential flow) and small waves. This allows for linearization of the nonlinear equations describing water motions and floater motions and results in fast and efficient calculations. The downside of this approach is that the results should be treated with caution when applied to very high waves (such as the 1,5 and 50-year return period wave conditions described in D2.1).

The results of such an analysis are:

1.  Added mass and damping coefficients describing the reaction forces of the water when the floats are moving. These coefficients depend on the oscillation period/frequency.

2.  Wave forces due to incident (incoming) waves approaching the floats and wave reflections on the floats. These coefficients depend on the wave period/frequency and the incident wave angle. It should be noted that we distinguish linear loads and nonlinear loads (wave drift loads). The former cause motions of the floats at the periods of the incident waves. The latter are important for mooring loads and can induce large horizontal motions of and mooring forces in the assembled PV system.

3.  Motions of the floats. These also depend on the wave period/frequency and the incident wave angle.

For more details see Appendix E. In the simulations presented here no viscous damping was added.

The PV module is modelled as a rectangular box, although in reality there is some curvature in the structure on the edges. The effect of this on the dynamic response is expected to be small because these small details are much smaller than the length of the waves. Simulations were done for wave frequencies up to 6.0 rad/s with a step of 0.1 rad/s. This corresponds to a wave length of 1.7m.

First the amount of mesh elements was investigated which is needed to obtain converged results. The meshes consists of 5x5, 10x10 and 15x15 panels on the lower side and upper side of the panel. The four vertical sides have 1 panel in the vertical direction and 5,10 and 15 panels in the horizontal directions, see [Figure 1](#_Ref128055338). Only the wetted part of the PV module needs to be modelled.

It can be concluded that also for the situation with multiple PV modules it is sufficient to use 10x10 panels. Therefore, in all the results presented in this document, the mesh with 10x10 panels was used.

For floating bodies in close proximity, often resonant water motions occur related to the pumping of water and sloshing of water in the gap separating the floaters. In a linear analysis without viscous damping, these resonant water motions are grossly exaggerated, but these can be reduced by adding damping to the water motions in the gap. In these simulations that was not observed in the wave frequency range that was analysed, probably due to the limited draft of the floats.

The relative motion RAO ([Figure 6](#_Ref127953747)) shows that the largest horizontal relative motions are expected to occur for a wave frequency of 3.2 rad/s (wave period 1.96s) and the largest vertical relative motions for a wave frequency of 5.2 rad/s (1.21 s). These motions will be (partially) restrained when the modules are connected with hinges and therefore the largest hinge forces can also be expected in this range of frequencies. [Figure 7](#_Ref128056019) shows a visualisation of the dynamic pressure on the free surface and the PV modules in a short wave (wave frequency 5.0 rad/s) where relative heave motions are largest. It should be noted that the breakwater is expected to have a high effectiveness in this wave period range and a large part of the wave energy will be blocked.

![[]{#_Ref128056019 .anchor}Figure 7: Dynamic pressure on the free surface and on the two coupled modules in a regular wave with a frequency of 5.0 rad/s (DIFFRAC)](../images/2024-04-16_surewave_d5.1/image9.png){alt="P360#yIS1" width="6.6930555555555555in" height="3.1in"}

Because of the linearity assumption, the hinges between the modules can only be modelled as linear springs in six degrees of freedom. These springs restrict the relative surge, sway, heave, roll, pitch and yaw motions. Any hinge non-linearities can be modelled in semi nonlinear time-domain models such as aNySIM, which is used for the breakwater mooring simulations in chapter [5](#mooring-loads-of-the-10mw-design) (for more details, see Appendix D). Initial hinge stiffness parameters were provided by Sunlit Sea. At a later stage detailed (nonlinear) hinge parameters became available from tensile tests by SINTEF, but these were not yet available for the simulations reported in this document. The hinge horizontal stiffness is 85,000 N/m and the hinge rotational stiffness is 1.375 Nm/rad. Two of these hinges are located between the modules, see [Figure 8](#_Ref159843088).

Simulations were performed with these hinge stiffnesses with the following procedure:

- The stiffness values of the hinge are transformed to hinge stiffness matrices at the centre of mass of the floats.

- The hinge stiffness matrices are added to the equation of motion of the floats which is solved at the centre of mass of the body.

- The motions at the centre of mass of the floats are transformed to the motions at the hinge locations (due to rotations the translations surge-sway-heave at the hinge are different than those at the centre of mass)

- The relative motion of the two floats at the hinge location is determined in all 6 degrees of freedom by subtracting the motions of the floats.

- The relative motion is multiplied with the hinge stiffness which results in the loads in the hinge.

![[]{#_Ref159843088 .anchor}Figure 8: Visualisation of the hinge and two modules in aNySIM.](../images/2024-04-16_surewave_d5.1/image10.png){alt="P372#yIS1" width="5.509722222222222in" height="2.2083333333333335in"}

The amplitude of the hinge force variations as a function of wave frequency is shown in [Figure 9](#_Ref127968257). It can be seen that the force variations are largest for waves with a frequency around 4.0 rad/s (1.6s). This frequency is slightly higher than the frequency at which the relative horizontal motion peaks ([Figure 6](#_Ref127953747)).

![[]{#_Ref127968257 .anchor}Figure 9: Hinge force variations in regular waves with 1m amplitude.](../images/2024-04-16_surewave_d5.1/image11.png){alt="P377#yIS1" width="4.275in" height="3.2583333333333333in"}

The hinges affect the motion response of the modules. The nonlinear wave drift forces depend on the motion response and therefore these forces will be affected by the presence of the hinges.

[Figure 10](#_Ref127971443) shows the mean wave drift force on the two modules. It can be seen that the drift force on the exposed module (BODY 2) is much larger in magnitude, and that the wave drift forces start to become significant only for short waves (frequencies higher than 3.0 rad/s). Due to the wave shielding effect it can be expected that for a longer row of modules the wave drift force is negligible for modules further down the row. The total drift force on the two modules combined is negative, meaning that the modules are pushed backwards in their mooring in the direction of wave propagation, as expected.

# Validation with Stadt towing tank wave basin tests

[]{#_Toc164177988 .anchor}2.1 Basin tests

Wave basin tests of Sunlit Sea's floating PV system were performed in the Stadt Towing Tank, Norway. These tests were not part of the SUREWAVE project, but the results were made available by Sunlit Sea for the numerical model validation presented herein.

The basin tests were performed at prototype scale for arrays of 1x3, 1x6 and 1x9 interconnected PV modules. The water depth in the basin was 4.0 m. Several irregular and regular wave conditions were tested, all at normal incidence. The model was kept in place through soft springs connected at fore side of the array. During the tests, motions were measured using an optical measurement system (see [Figure 14](#_Ref160696069)) and were translated to CoG. Besides, the wave height was logged at two or three locations in the basin.

- For the 1x3 array: Log 21; Pink Noise, Hs = 0.1 m, Tp = 0.7-3.0 s

- For the 1x6 array: Log 11; JONSWAP, Hs = 0.3 m, Tp = 2.75 s

## Results

[Figure 16](#_Ref160713004) presents a comparison between DIFFRAC and experimental RAOs as function of normalized wave length λ for the 1x3 (left) and 1x6 (right) array. Floater 1 is the most upwave module. The pitch RAOs were normalized with the wave number *k*. The horizontal axis is normalized with the length of the array (3 or 6 panels) for surge and with the length of a single module for heave and pitch.

The numerical and experimental results are generally similar. Most notably, the experimental surge RAOs seem shifted along the horizontal axis towards shorter wave lengths compared to the numerical results. The DIFFRAC calculations show a depression of the surge RAO at λ/Larray ≈ 1, i.e., when the wave length is approximately equal to the length of the array, which is as expected. It is not clear why the experimental results have this depression at a slightly shorter wave length (λ/L~float~ ≈ 0.9). The numerical results show a slightly lower surge response for the mid floater (#2 in the left hand plots), which is not seen in the experimental results.

The numerical heave and pitch RAOs approach a normalized amplitude of 1 in the limit of long waves, corresponding to the PV modules following the water surface (as expected). The experimental pitch RAOs approach a slightly lower amplitude of about 0.8 -- 0.9 in the limit of long waves; these values below 1 may relate to uncertainties in measurements and data processing. For λ \< 2\*L~float~, heave and pitch amplitudes drop below 1. In the range of short waves (λ \< 2\*L~float~), the heave and pitch RAOs reduce from the fore (#1) towards the aft (#3, #6) floaters due to shielding effects.

Numerical results for heave and pitch motions are generally similar to the basin test results, although some differences are notable. For instance, the heave RAOs seem slightly overpredicted for short waves (λ \< 2\*L~float~) while the pitch RAOs are overestimated for long waves (possibly related to measurement uncertainties). ## Discussion

The comparison against experimental results shows that DIFFRAC can reproduce the general motion behaviour, including shielding by the fore floaters. This provides confidence that the tool can be used for a more explorative study into, e.g., effects of marine growth (Section [2.3](#effect-of-marine-growth)) and different configurations (next Chapter). However, it should be noted that the DIFFRAC calculations have not been validated in terms of hinge loads, and it is expected that the nonlinear stiffness characteristics of the hinges (not captured by DIFFRAC) have a strong effect on the maximum hinge loads.

In addition, it is noted that the motion behaviour of the FPV system is only validated for a relatively small farm (1 x 6 at max). For much larger farms, physical processes that are not considered in the present linear first-order potential flow approach may become important, such as second order excitation and dissipation of wave energy throughout the farm. These processes are not represented in the present diffraction approach but may be considered through higher fidelity tools (time domain simulations, CFD, basin tests) at later stage.

In conclusion, DIFFRAC can reproduce the general motion behaviour, but the computed hinge loads and the motions in large fields come with high uncertainty due to the inherent limitations of this low-fidelity tool.

# CFD analysis of coupled PV modules

The linear low-fidelity analysis is performing satisfactory in mild and moderate wave conditions. It is expected that for higher waves such an analysis has its shortcomings because of effects such as wave breaking, wave overtopping and large motions of the PV modules. Therefore, some initial investigations were performed to investigate the suitability of CFD calculations for the coupled analysis of the modules in high waves. The simulations were made using MARIN's in-house CFD code ReFRESCO (<https://www.marin.nl/en/about/facilities-and-tools/software/refresco>). The following simulation setup was chosen:

- Three coupled modules in a 2D setup (1 computational cell in transverse, y-direction)

- Irregular waves typical for the Baltic sea with a return period of 5 years (Height=4.0m, peak period=9.1s)

- Hinges with a linear stiffness between the modules according to the values specified by partner Sunlit Sea

The irregular waves were first generated using the open-source wave model REEF3D-FNPF (https://reef3d.wordpress.com/) with a 3-hour storm duration. This model does not consider the PV modules; it only generates the CFD initial and boundary condition. For the CFD simulation a wave group was selected from this three-hour storm that includes the highest wave crest. The velocities, pressures and wave elevation were transferred from the REEF3D model to the CFD model. The CFD simulations do include the PV modules and hinges.

The first wave crest in this wave group causes overtopping of the PV modules as shown in [Figure 17](#_Ref168321883). The CFD calculation successfully continues after this event has passed.

![[]{#_Ref168321883 .anchor}Figure 17: Wave overtopping event in CFD in 5-year return period wave using three connected PV modules.](../images/2024-04-16_surewave_d5.1/image21.png){alt="A screen shot of a computer screen Description automatically generated" width="4.747222222222222in" height="1.4916666666666667in"}

The decision to switch from high-fidelity (CFD) simulations to low-fidelity models is driven by several factors. Firstly, the high-fidelity CFD method lacks robustness when simulating the response of photovoltaic (PV) modules under steep and breaking wave conditions. Lastly, low-fidelity simulations, despite their model inaccuracies, can be adjusted with safety factors to provide a more practical and reliable assessment of PV module performance

## Post-processing

The RAOs provide the response in regular waves. In reality, waves are irregular and the response depends also on the energy content and distribution of the wave field. To convert the RAOs to expected values in irregular sea states, the most probable maximum (MPM) is a commonly used variable. The MPM of a linear parameter in a 3-h sea state was calculated based on the linear response amplitude operator (RAO) and the input wave spectrum (S~w­~) as follows:

+=======================================================================+

> $$RMS = \ \sqrt{m_{0}}$$
>
> $$MPM = \ \sqrt{2.\ln N}.RMS$$
>
> $$SDA = \ 4*RMS$$
>
> Where:
>
> S~w~(ω) is the wave spectrum
>
> m~0~ is the zero-order spectral moment of the response spectrum
>
> RMS Root Mean Square, is the square root of the zeroth moment.
>
> N represents the number of wave cycles in a 3-h sea state (N = 10,800/T~z~),\

## Effect of floating breakwater

The floating breakwater (FBW) aims to reduce the amplitude of the wave experienced by the PV modules. In order to consider the attenuation by the FBW, the linear Macagno formulation was used (see equation 3-12 in SUREWAVE D2.2 (Pehlke, 2023a)):

![P597#yIS1](../images/2024-04-16_surewave_d5.1/image23.png){width="2.436053149606299in" height="0.48247265966754155in"}

The transmission coefficient C~t,M~ is defined as the ratio between transmitted and incident wave height (C~t,M~ = H~t~/H~i~). Since the relation is linear, it can be combined with the RAOs of motions and forces to obtain RAOs in presence of the breakwater:

> $${RAO}_{att}(\omega) = \ {C_{t,M}(\omega)RAO}_{os}(\omega)$$

Here, subscripts "att" and "os" are used to indicate situations with the wave field attenuated by FBW presence and open seas, respectively. In the calculations, a breakwater width W~p~ = 5.0 m and a draft D~p~ = 2.03 m are assumed, consistent with D3.2 (Pehlke, 2023b)

## Discussion

As mentioned earlier, the high-fidelity CFD method is not robust enough to simulate the response of PV modules accurately under steep and breaking wave conditions, which are critical for estimating hinge loads. To model hinge forces, a computationally demanding Fluid-Structure Interaction (FSI) approach is required. However, this FSI model is even more computationally intensive and necessitates rigorous validation before it can be trusted Additionally, hinge loads depend on the number of PV panels, and performing high-fidelity simulations for large arrays is computationally demanding. Consequently, we opted for a low-fidelity model.

The results presented in this Chapter are obtained with a low-fidelity numerical tool that was validated in terms of motions (not in terms of hinge loads) and for small arrays only (see also discussion in Section [3.3](#discussion)). The linear diffraction analysis does not include nonlinear physics related to wave overwash, (breaking) wave impacts, and nonlinear stiffness of the hinges.

Based on results in Section 3, as well as studies on similar multi-body systems, it is expected that the linear diffraction analysis (low fidelity models) adequately captures the motion behaviour, also for higher sea states. In terms of hinge loads, it is expected that the present linear approach yields non-conservative estimates of the maximum hinge loads. As observed during wave basin tests (Task 6.4), the low compressive stiffness of the hinges leads to buckling and snap loading. Because such buckling and snap loads are not considered in the present analysis, the maximum hinge loads are likely underestimated. To better assess the hinge loads, more detailed time domain simulations that include the nonlinear hinge stiffness characteristics are recommended. For our project partner, SIS, understanding the hinge load is crucial for the commercial exploitation of their products. In the next phase of the project, we will collaborate to analyze the hinge loads using data from wave tank experiments, SINTEF\'s ultimate strength lab tests, and time-domain simulations incorporating nonlinear hinge stiffness.

Despite the assumptions in the linear diffraction approach, the results shed relevant qualitative insights into the effects of wave incidence, sea state, and farm size. The results show that maximum hinge loads depend strongly on wave incidence, peak period, and farm size. The maximum horizontal hinge loads increase strongly with increasing farm size. The floating breakwater reduces these horizontal loads only to certain extent. A different approach to mitigating high hinge loads could be to split a large PV farm into different subfarms that are moored separately. Also, a surface mooring that pretensions the FPV system may help reducing the PV loads. This is to be explored further through time domain simulations in which a surface mooring, as well as the nonlinear stiffness behaviour of the hinges, can be taken into account.

## Setup of the numerical model

The simulations are performed using the aNySIM-xmf semi-nonlinear Time Domain Simulation (TDS) model. It can solve the equation of motion (Newton's 2^nd^ law) of any number of bodies due to arbitrary external excitation forces. For the circular breakwater the following force components were modelled:

- Wave loads due to incident and diffracted (reflected) waves

- Hydrodynamic reaction forces of the water due to motions (hydrostatic forces, added mass, damping)

- Viscous damping forces

- Forces in connectors between breakwaters

- Forces in mooring lines

- Forced due to wind and current

In the following sections each of these components is discussed in more detail.

Wave loads and hydrodynamic reaction forces

The wave loads and hydrodynamic reaction forces are again calculated using linear diffraction theory (see also Chapter [1](#_Ref160467093) and appendix C). For that purpose the underwater part of the breakwater is divided into panel elements as shown in [Figure 32](#_Ref160467142). A total of 1500 elements was used which was found to be sufficient to have converged results for the range of wave frequencies of interest in this study. A tapered shape is used (see [Figure 33](#_Ref160467164)) such that no gaps exists between the breakwaters in the circular layout. It can be seen that the area of the breakwater that connects to other breakwaters (the short side just below the waterline) has no panels since water cannot flow there in the linear analysis.

The circular layout is shown in [Figure 34](#_Ref160468998). The 56 breakwaters have a total of 84,000 panels. Inside the circular breakwater standing waves between the breakwaters will develop. In a linear potential flow analysis these standing waves are undamped and can become unrealistically high. The method to suppress these standing waves is to add damping to the waves propagating inside the circle. The non-dimensional damping parameter that was used in this study was ε=0.025 which is a value commonly used in linear diffraction theory for floating bodies in close proximity.

Similar to the floating PV panels the linear diffraction analysis results in wave load coefficients and reaction forces that depend on wave period/frequency. (wave load coefficients also depend on wave direction). These are stored in a database that can be read and used in the time domain simulation in site-specific wave conditions (wave spectra).

Damping values from CFD simulations

The wave load simulations are done using potential flow theory (see appendix C) in which viscous effects are not accounted for. Viscous effects can have a significant effect on the damping of breakwater motions near resonance. Resonance occurs when restoring/stiffness forces (mooring, hydrostatics) and inertia forces (acceleration of mass and added mass) cancel each other. In that case the response is proportional to the excitation force divided by the damping coefficient and therefore the response can be large in case of low damping values. It is therefore important to take into account all possible damping values.

CFD simulations were done to obtain the viscous damping forces on a single breakwater for sway, heave and roll motions. Because the breakwater shape is rather constant over its length, 2D simulations of a cross-section were made and the end effects were neglected. The simulations were made using MARIN's in-house CFD code ReFRESCO (https://www.marin.nl/en/about/facilities-and-tools/software/refresco) by imposing a forced motion to the breakwater at the (expected) natural periods of the various motions at a draft of 2.02m. The following motions were imposed in three separate simulations:

  -----------------------------------

![[]{#_Ref160535580 .anchor}Figure 37: Mesh around the 2D breakwater section](../images/2024-04-16_surewave_d5.1/image53.png){alt="P757#yIS1" width="4.6790485564304465in" height="2.4006944444444445in"}

The reaction forces of the water on the breakwater were calculated and stored for further analysis. The reaction forces can be written as:

> $$F_{r} = - A\ddot{x} - b^{(1)}\dot{x} - b^{(2)}\dot{x}\left| \dot{x} \right| - cx$$

Where

$F_{r}$ = reaction force/moment

$x,\ \dot{x},\ \ddot{x}$ = motion, velocity and acceleration of motion mode (sway, heave, roll)

$A$ = added mass coefficient

$b^{(1)}$ = linear damping coefficient

$b^{(2)}$ = quadratic damping coefficient

$c$ = hydrostatic spring coefficient

The damping coefficients $b^{(1)}$ and $b^{(2)}$were found by least-squares fitting of these coefficients to the computed force time trace. The 3D damping values were subsequently obtained by multiplying the 2D damping coefficients with the ration of the breakwater length and the width of the 3D section. This results in the following damping values:

  --------------------------------------------
  motion   $$b^{(1)}$$     $$b^{(2)}$$
  -------- --------------- -------------------
  Sway     29600 Ns/m      60400 Ns^2^/m^2^

  Heave    171000 Ns/m     57600 Ns^2^/m^2^

  Roll     44700 Nms/rad   0.0 Nms^2^/rad^2^
  --------------------------------------------

These damping values were subsequently used in the time-domain simulations for each of the 56 breakwaters.

Mooring lines

The mooring line specifications were provided by Clement and taken from report D3.2. A different mooring layout was used for each of the three locations as indicated previously in section [5.1](#introduction-1). The mooring lines consists entirely of chain with a portion of chain on the seabed. Based on the specified line weight, line stiffness, water depth and anchor locations the nonlinear load excursion curves of each mooring line was calculated. This describes the relation between motions of the fairlead (the point where the mooring line is connected at the breakwater) and the forces in the mooring line at the fairlead. When the breakwater ring is moving (due to wave action) the changes in mooring line forces can be calculated and fed back into the equation of motion of the breakwater ring. The dynamic effects in the mooring line loads are neglected in this study (drag, inertia loads).

Mediterranean 165 kN

Connectors between breakwaters

At the time of writing of this report the details of the connectors were not known yet. The connectors will be designed such that they restrict relative motions between the breakwaters, allowing only for relative rotational (pitch) motions, see [Figure 38](#_Ref160544925).

Simulations were performed to find the maximum loads in the mooring lines and connectors that can be expected in these 50-year wave conditions. It is important to realize that the maximum load in a 3-hour sea state (with identical spectra) is a random variable because the actual encountered wave signal is a random realization from that spectrum. The maximum 3-hour load is therefore not a useful statistical quantity. # Mooring optimization

## Introduction

Sections [6](#mooring-loads-of-the-10mw-design) and [7](#mooring-loads-of-the-2mw-design) showed the hydrodynamic assessment of the mooring configuration in D3.3 through time domain simulations along the 50-y return period storm contour. Results were analysed in terms of maximum mooring loads, and were compared to the proof load that may not be exceeded to ensure structural integrity of the lines. The analysis showed that for both the 10 and the 2MW farms, the proof loads were exceeded for the Baltic Sea, while maximum line loads for the Mediterranean and North Sea were well below the proof loads. This means that for the Baltic Sea, the mooring should be improved to ensure structural integrity; while for the other field sites, the mooring is conservative and a reduction in mooring line mass can reduce the mooring costs.

Therefore, the present section presents an optimization of the mooring layout. The main aim is to reduce the costs of mooring, whilst (still) ensuring structural integrity. It is assumed that the costs of mooring correlate strongly with the total steel weight of the mooring lines. Therefore, the analysis focuses on minimizing the mooring mass.

## Methods

The mooring optimization consists of the following steps, which are explained in the subsections below:

1.  Selection of design parameters, objectives, and constraints

2.  Hydrodynamic simulations within predefined design space (design of experiments approach)

3.  Surrogate modelling based on hydrodynamic simulations

4.  Optimization by means of Pareto front

5.  Verification of targeted design optimum

## Design parameters

The simulation approach described in Section [6](#mooring-loads-of-the-10mw-design) is the base for the analysis. The model structure was embedded into MARIN's optimization framework HYDRA. This framework combines the time domain software aNySIM-XMF (MARIN, in-house) with the optimization program Dakota[^3].

Three design parameters were varied in the optimization:

- Mooring radius

- Chain diameter

- Mooring line length

The latter parameter (line length) was varied by introducing a dimensionless factor, f~ml_length~, defined as

f~ml~,~length~ = L~ml~ / (mooring_radius^2^ + waterdepth^2^)^0.5^

The denominator corresponds to the diagonal between anchor point and fairlead in still water equilibrium, as illustrated in [*Figure 57*](#_Ref172029691). This means that f~ml~,~length~ = 1 corresponds to a line length equal to the diagonal. By introducing this parameter, the dimensional line length scales automatically with water depth and mooring radius. The mooring radius is defined as the horizontal distance between hang-off point (fairlead) at the breakwater and the anchor position.

For sake of simplicity, the mooring material (steel) was kept constant and a single-type segment was assumed. Further optimization on material properties and multiple segments may help to further reduce costs, but is beyond the scope of the present work.

Next, hydrodynamic simulations were performed for each design run. In the post-processing, first the median of maximum loads over the 3 seeds for each sea state was computed, and next, the maximum value over the 8 simulated sea states was taken as the governing maximum line tension.

## Surrogate modelling

The design runs were evaluated in terms of three responses:

- Total mooring mass of a single line (design objective #1)

- Maximum line tension at fairlead (design objective #2)

- Maximum line tension at fairlead, normalized by proof load (design constraint)

To relate the input (three design factors) to each response variable, surrogate models were built based on the 17 simulation results. Different types of surrogate models were tested (Kriging, polynomial fits). Quadratic models led to reasonably good to good fits (coefficients of determination r^2^ \> 0.85) and were used in the remainder analysis.

## Optimization

Next, a design optimization was run on the surrogate models. While the main aim of the analysis is to minimize mooring mass (as measure for costs), it was decided that also maximum line tensions at the fairlead should be considered. The reason is that an initial analysis showed that designs with minimum mooring mass were typically those with heavy but short lines (small radius), which led to high instantaneous tensions at the fairlead. These designs can be considered unfavourable from the point of view of the structural integrity of the breakwater connection and because of the high vertical pretension that leads to submergence of the breakwater ring.

In addition to the two design objectives (minimize mooring mass and minimize line tensions at fairlead), one constraint was imposed: maximum line tensions shall not exceed proof loads. For the 10MW farms, the design limit of the maximum mooring radius was set to the radius of the breakwater ring (168 m) to prevent collisions between the inward lines (ref. [Figure 52](#_Ref175143290)). For the 2MW farms, this constraint was released because the inward lines are deployed from under a slight horizontal angle (see Pehlke, 2024).

To find the optimum designs, a multi-objective genetic algorithm (MOGA) approach was adopted, using the open-source software Dakota to run the optimization. Because the surrogate models are computationally highly efficient, a large number of evaluations (up to a few thousand) could be realized to approach the optimum solutions. The optimum solutions are found in the form of a Pareto front.

## Verification of optimum

Based on the Pareto front, the optimum solution is selected. To verify the solution, a new hydrodynamic simulation is ran using the obtained optimum values for the design factors. In this verification step, the hydrodynamic simulations are again performed for 8 sea states x 3 seeds. The results are evaluated in terms of maximum loads in relation to proof loads. For some design cases it followed that the obtained optimum resulted in proof load exceedance; for these cases, simulations for additional design variations close to the optimum were performed until an acceptable design was found.

# Aerodynamic loads

FPV system face significant challenges from external forces, especially wind gusts, which have the potential to cause substantial damage to the entire floating structure. Accurately estimating the forces exerted by these wind gusts is crucial for ensuring the stability and resilience of FPV installations. Conducting physical measurements on these systems can be both time-consuming and expensive. Several wind tunnel studies have attempted to estimate the aerodynamic forces on PV panels. For instance, Ogedengbe (2013) (Abiola-Ogedengbe et al., 2015) conducted wind tunnel experiments using scaled aluminium models to calculate pressure coefficients across panel surfaces. While some studies, such as those by Bogdan and Cretu, 2019 (Bogdan & Creţu, 2019); Sheikh, 2019 (Sheikh, n.d.), have utilized scaled models in wind tunnels, these experiments are expensive and limited in scope to only a few PV modules. An alternative approach to estimating the forces acting on these structures is through the use of numerical models. The numerical approaches allow for the simulation of various environmental conditions and can provide valuable insights into the behaviour of floating solar panels under different scenarios. Computational Fluid Dynamics (CFD) is a powerful tool for predicting and analyzing fluid flow phenomena. Numerous studies have been carried out to understand the behavior of the FPV structures, focusing particularly on the aerodynamic forces acting on the PV arrays. Many previous studies have been conducted on the fixed PV systems and these studies can be broadly categorized into three groups: ground-mounted, commercial roof-mounted, and residential roof-mounted systems. Some studies focus solely on investigating wind effects on ground-mounted systems (Aly, 2013, 2016; Aly & Bitsuamlak, 2014), while others examine both ground-mounted and roof-mounted systems, providing comparative data on wind load effects between the two(Kopp, 2012; Stathopoulos et al., 2014). The majority of previous experimental studies on roof-mounted systems have primarily focused on commercial buildings. Several studies have investigated wind loads on solar panels using various methods. Bitsumalik (Bitsuamlak et al., 2010) employed CFD to evaluate wind loads on ground-mounted solar panels across four different scenarios, demonstrating strong agreement between numerical simulations and experimental data. Jubayer and Hagen (2012) (Jubayer & Hangan, 2012) conducted both numerical and experimental analyses, examining wind angles ranging from 0 to 180 degrees to determine coefficients of drag, lift, and overturning moment. Warsido (2014) (Warsido et al., 2014) investigated the sheltering effect of consecutively placed solar panels through wind tunnel experiments, exploring a larger number of panels and different spacing factors compared to previous studies. Radu et al.(Radu et al., 1986) highlighted that the first row of solar panels can reduce wind load on subsequent rows due to a sheltering effect. Similar to the SUREWAVE concept, Garcia et al. (Torres García et al., 2014) introduced the concept of wind breaks to mitigate wind loads on solar troughs for optimized structures.

The majority of existing studies have predominantly focused on analyzing wind loads on solar panels in ground-based installations. However, the challenges encountered by floating PV systems deployed on water bodies are different. Unlike ground-mounted systems where the forces are managed by fixed support structures and panels are firmly anchored to the ground, floating PV systems face inherent flexibility and buoyancy issues. Simply increasing the weight of materials to strengthen them may not be adequate, as an overweight system could lead to sinking to the bottom. Given the need to balance buoyancy and weight for stability, and the inability to increase system weight to withstand wind loads, it is necessary to conduct a comprehensive assessment of drag and lift coefficients at varying inlet angles. Such analyses are crucial for designing structurally robust systems including mooring and anchoring system capable of withstanding fluctuating wind conditions. There have been some studies on the aerodynamic assessment of floating PV system. Choi et al (Choi et al., 2022) investigated wind load effects on a solar panel array for floating photovoltaic systems. Their studies revealed higher drag and lift coefficients for the first and last rows of panels due to direct wind exposure, with subsequent rows experiencing reduced forces due to a sheltering effect.

The main purpose of this study is to evaluate breakwater efficiency for reducing aerodynamic forces on floating photovoltaic (PV) panels through CFD simulations. By strategically positioning a breakwater ahead of an 8x8 matrix of PV panels and varying angles of attack, this study assesses the impact of breakwater placement on lift and drag coefficients. Unlike previous studies that primarily focused on ground-based installations, this study addresses the unique challenges faced by floating PV systems deployed on water bodies. The findings highlight the potential of breakwaters in mitigating aerodynamic forces under specific wind conditions, offering valuable insights for optimizing breakwater design and placement to enhance the resilience and performance of floating PV systems in various environmental contexts.

1.  Methods

For this study, CFD simulations were conducted using OpenFOAM, an open-source CFD software package. The SimpleFoam solver was employed to solve the steady-state Reynolds-averaged Navier-Stokes (RANS) equations. The turbulence model chosen for this study was the Spalart-Allmaras model, which is widely used for simulating turbulent flows for aerodynamic application with moderate computational cost.  Boundary conditions were specified to model the flow around the PV modules realistically. At the inlet boundary, a fixedValue condition was applied to specify the velocity profile based on the desired angle of attack. At the outlet boundary, a zeroGradient condition was used. For the remaining boundaries, slip conditions were assumed to simulate the flow behavior. However, for the surfaces of the PV modules and for the water body, a noSlip boundary condition was applied to represent the physical interaction between the water and the solid surfaces. 

2.   Aerodynamic Results

CFD simulations were conducted for four different configurations of the PV module matrix: 4x8, 8x8, 12x8, and 16x8. Within each configuration, simulations were performed at various angle of attack values ranging from 0 to 20 degrees. In these simulations, we accounted for rigid hinges and assumed that the PV matrix remains fixed, with no relative motions of the floats. 4.  Validation of lift and drag relationships:

In the previous CFD simulations, varying front panel numbers: 4x8, 8x8, 12x8, and 16x8 configurations were utilized and a correlation was developed to establish the relationship between the number of front panels and the corresponding lift and drag coefficients. To validate the reliability and applicability of this correlation, additional simulations were performed at 10-degree angle of attack, focusing on a 32x8 panel configuration. The purpose was to examine the predictive capability of the correlation when applied to an array size beyond those originally considered. For comparison, an independent CFD simulation of the 32x8 panel configuration were conducted and the lift and drag obtained from these CFD simulations were compared with the developed correlation. A discrepancy of approximately 10% and 7% in lift and drag respectively was observed between the correlation and this CFD simulations.

5.  Effect of breakwater on the aerodynamic coefficients:

Introducing a breakwater not only attenuate high-frequency waves but it acts as a barrier against wind which offers a promising solution to minimize aerodynamic forces on floating photovoltaic (PV) panels. In aerodynamic simulations without breakwater, it was observed that the lift and drag forces increased with the angle of attack, indicating a potential challenge for floating PV systems exposed to wind. By strategically placing breakwaters around the floating PV array, several benefits can be realized. Firstly, the breakwater serves as a physical barrier and placing the panels in the sheltered zone behind the breakwater ensures that they experience reduced wind speeds and smoother airflow, minimizing the aerodynamic forces acting upon them. This attenuation of wind speed due to break water effectively mitigates the aerodynamic forces experienced by the panels. Moreover, the breakwater helps to create a calmer microenvironment within its sheltered area, further reducing the impact of turbulent wind conditions on the panels.

To assess the effectiveness of the breakwater in reducing aerodynamic forces on the floating PV panels, a series of CFD simulations were conducted. In these simulations, a single breakwater of dimensions 20x5x2 (LxWxH) was positioned ahead of an 8x8 matrix of PV panels, maintaining a distance of 2 and 7 meters between the breakwater and the panels. The PV panels were subjected to two angles of attack ( 5 and 10 degrees) to represent different wind conditions. [Table 13](#_Ref162952518) shows the simulation matrix performed with the breakwater.

#  Conclusions

Simulations were performed with low-fidelity models for wave loads and motion response of several floating PV/breakwater configurations:

1.  An array of five PV panels as tested in the Stadt towing tank for validation of the numerical model

2.  A matrix of up to 20x20 panels to investigate the connector loads when the size of the PV field increases. The effect of the breakwater is included by means of wave attenuation coefficients.

3.  The moored breakwater ring was designed to protect the 10MW PV field from short waves. 50-year wave conditions from Baltic, North Sea and Mediterranean were simulated to calculate the loads in breakwater connectors and mooring lines.

The conclusions are as follows:

- A good agreement is found between the measured motions of the PV panels in the Stadt towing tank model tests and the linear simulation model. Since hinge loads were not measured these could not be validated. It is expected that the nonlinear characteristics of the hinges pose challenges for these computations.

- The results show that maximum hinge loads depend strongly on wave incidence, peak period, and farm size. The maximum horizontal hinge loads increase strongly with increasing farm size. The floating breakwater reduces these horizontal loads only to certain extent. A different approach to mitigating high hinge loads could be to split a large PV farm into different subfarms that are moored separately. Also, a surface mooring that pretensions the FPV system may help reducing the PV loads. This is to be explored further through time domain simulations in which a surface mooring, as well as the nonlinear stiffness behaviour of the hinges, can be taken into account.

<!-- -->

- Marine growth on the underside of the PV module increases the mass by a factor of (almost) 5. This also results in a similar increase in hinge loads.

- The floating breakwater is effective in reducing the hinge loads in vertical direction (by \~factor 5-10), but its effectiveness in reducing horizontal loads is much lower, especially for long-period sea states and larger PV farms.

- The simulations with the breakwater ring in 50-year wave conditions contain a number of uncertainties:

  - The wave forces are based on a linear analysis which may become less accurate for extreme conditions (i.e. 50-year wave conditions).

  - The mooring lines are modelled with their nonlinear load-excursion curve. Dynamic line effects were not taken into account.

  - The connectors between breakwaters were modelled as a stiff spring/damper system with estimated stiffness/damping values (design values were not yet available). Differences with the actual design values may lead to differences in the connector loads.

  - The resonant water motions inside the breakwater ring were suppressed by damping the water motions. The sensitivity of the breakwater response with respect to the applied damping value was not investigated.

- The largest loads in the mooring lines were found at the Baltic Sea location and amounts to \~6300 kN. In all cases the loads are well below the breaking load of the line (11,856 kN as presented in D3.3)

- The largest loads in the breakwater connectors were found at the North Sea location and amounts to 4500 kN (splitting load Fx) and 55,000 kNm (bending moment Mz). These loads can be used for detailed design of the connectors.

- Simulations were repeated for a 2MW design consisting of 32 breakwaters. Maximum mooring line loads are more than 50% lower than for the 10MW design. Maximum mooring loads for the North and Mediterranean Seas are well below the line proof load while the mooring loads for the Baltic Sea are exceeded by about a factor 2. Loads in connectors between breakwaters are reduced by 25 to 35%.

- Following the assessment of the reference mooring design, it was attempted to optimize the mooring configuration for each field site and farm size using a combination of time domain simulations, surrogate modelling, and genetic algorithm based optimization techniques. This approach resulted in optimized designs that ensured structural integrity and led for Mediterranean and North Sea field sites to a 34-56% reduction of the total weight (and costs) of mooring lines.

- It is recommended to perform model tests to investigate the breakwater response in these wave conditions so the numerical model can be validated.

**[Aerodynamic assessment]{.underline}**

- The present study has shown the importance of analyzing wind load effects on PV arrays, especially in the context of floating photovoltaic (PV) systems. Through comprehensive CFD simulations, we have gained valuable insights into the factors influencing drag and lift coefficients. Our findings emphasize the importance of accurately estimating both drag and lift forces during the design phase of floating PV systems to address safety concerns such as overturning.

- The aerodynamic coefficients (lift and drag) are linear function of number of PV panels at lower angles of attack (\< 15 degree).

- The study highlights the efficiency of employing a breakwater as a protective measure against wind forces. By strategically placing the breakwater, particularly at lower angles of attack, we observed a substantial reduction in the impact of wind forces on the solar panel array, thereby enhancing the overall stability and resilience of the floating PV system.
