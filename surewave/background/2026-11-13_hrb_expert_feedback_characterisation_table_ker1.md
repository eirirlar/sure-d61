<td style="text-align: left;"><strong>Description</strong></td>
<td style="text-align: left;"><p>Sunlit Sea CONNECT is a floating solar power plant for the sea. Each float is a cast polyurethane frame on an aluminium bottom plate, carrying one standard 710–740 Wp glass-glass solar panel. Floats are joined along their full edge by flexible polyurethane hinges, so the array follows the waves instead of fighting them. Panels are bought from the mainstream solar market and dropped into the floats at deployment.</p>
<p>Where waves are too high for the platform alone, a floating breakwater is placed only on the sides where the strong waves come from, rather than around the whole plant. Sunlit Sea's planning tool, built on SUREWAVE wave and wind models, works out for each site where that protection is needed and how much energy is lost to wave motion.</p>
<p>The result: utility-scale solar power on coastal water close to industry, ports and grid connections, for customers who have run out of suitable land.</p></td>
<td style="text-align: left;"></td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td style="text-align: left;"><strong>Target market/end users</strong></td>
<td style="text-align: left;"><p>• Target market: electricity generation from solar on coastal and nearshore water in Europe, first in sheltered and mild-wave sites (Norwegian fjords and industrial basins, the Baltic, the Mediterranean), at plant sizes of 0.3–10 MW, scaling to larger plants from 2030.</p>
<p>• End users: renewable energy project developers and independent power producers who develop, own and operate the plants, and the coastal electricity users who buy the power under long-term power purchase agreements: process industry, ports, quarries and terminals.</p>
<p>• Market definition (from the Market Definition Canvas): offshore and nearshore renewable energy project developers trying to deliver utility-scale renewable electricity from water surfaces where onshore land is scarce.</p></td>
<td style="text-align: left;"><em>Excellent.</em></td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td style="text-align: left;"><strong>End-users needs / problems</strong></td>
<td style="text-align: left;"><p>• No suitable land: coastal industry and ports need local renewable power, but nearby land is taken, protected or too expensive.</p>
<p>• Existing floating solar products are made for calm lakes and reservoirs and break under sea waves; there is no proven product for coastal water at commercial scale.</p>
<p>• Investors and lenders need a design basis they can trust and a predictable maintenance cost over 20+ years.</p>
<p>• Power price must clear the buyer's benchmark: the plant must produce electricity at a cost comparable with other new renewables.</p>
<p>• Site development is slow: water-use permits, environmental assessment and grid connection take a long time and need marine expertise most developers lack.</p></td>
<td style="text-align: left;"><p><em>Excellent.</em></p>
<p><em>Any facts/examples/data are welcome to substantiate the magnitude of the problems identified.</em></p></td>

<tr>
<td style="text-align: left;"><strong>Competitive advantages</strong></td>
<td style="text-align: left;"><p>• Survives waves by design: flexible full-edge hinges spread the load along the whole float edge. SUREWAVE testing at SINTEF raised the ultimate load capacity of the critical connection from 14 kN to 37 kN with the third-generation hinge.</p>
<p>• Protection only where needed: breakwater placed against the critical wave directions of each site instead of a full ring, which cuts the cost of protection at nearshore sites.</p>
<p>• Standard solar panels: uses mainstream 710–740 Wp glass-glass panels, so the product follows the price fall of the global solar market instead of carrying a custom-panel cost.</p>
<p>• Simple manufacturing: cast polyurethane frame and flat aluminium plate, made with moulds and standard metalwork, scalable from 3D-printed moulds at pilot scale to industrial casting.</p>
<p>• Easy installation and service: assembled and launched from ordinary quays without specialist offshore vessels; a damaged hinge can be replaced without recovering the array.</p>
<p>• Proven economics in the right sites: SUREWAVE economic assessment shows a 10 MW Mediterranean plant at a levelised cost of energy of €0.062/kWh, 10.7 % internal rate of return and 8-year payback.</p></td>
<td style="text-align: left;"><p><em>Excellent.</em></p>
<p><em>The differentiators comprise all angles: technology, use, economics, etc.</em></p>
<p><em>Note: social/environmental aspects.</em></p></td>

<tr>
<td style="text-align: left;"><strong>Use model</strong></td>
<td style="text-align: left;"><p>Manufacturing and sale of a new product, combined with services:</p>
<p>• Supply of the floating platform (floats, hinges, mooring interfaces) to project developers and engineering-procurement-construction contractors.</p>
<p>• Site-specific engineering using Sunlit Sea's planning platform (wave and wind conditions, breakwater placement, wave-loss yield estimate).</p>
<p>• In the Nordic market, Sunlit Sea also develops, builds and operates its own plants and sells power under a power purchase agreement, then sells the operating plant to a long-term owner (develop-operate-sell). This gives the reference plants that later buyers need.</p>
<p>• Breakwater units and structural health monitoring are sourced from SUREWAVE partners or qualified suppliers under bilateral agreements.</p>
<p>• Engineering partners: IFE (structural simulation) and Metsolar in Lithuania (photovoltaic module integration). Prototype casting and panel frame design are bought from prototyping suppliers in Asia; series production is planned in Europe, starting with in-house moulds in Norway and a European casting partner.</p>
<p>• Sales outside the Nordics through partners: in Germany via Rixen Cableway, an existing Generation 1 customer.</p></td>
<td style="text-align: left;"><em>Very good.</em></td>

<td style="text-align: left;"><strong>Adopters’ problems/needs</strong></td>
<td style="text-align: left;"><p>• They want cheaper, local, fixed-price renewable electricity and lower Scope 2 emissions, but have no land for solar.</p>
<p>• They need a supplier who takes responsibility for the whole floating system (floats, mooring, wave protection) and can show a working reference.</p>
<p>• They need the plant to fit their site: water depth, seabed conditions (for example capped seabed after remediation), shipping lanes and permit limits.</p>
<p>• Developers need projects small enough to finance now (0.3–3 MW) with a path to larger plants.</p></td>
<td style="text-align: left;"><em>Excellent.</em></td>

<tr>
<td style="text-align: left;"><strong>Alternative solution</strong></td>
<td style="text-align: left;"><p>• Buy grid power, possibly with a power purchase agreement from onshore wind or solar elsewhere (no local generation, exposed to grid tariffs and price spikes).</p>
<p>• Rooftop or ground-mounted solar on the limited land and roofs available (small capacity).</p>
<p>• Calm-water floating solar products from lakes and reservoirs (not built for waves; limited to the most sheltered spots).</p>
<p>• Offshore wind for larger buyers (much larger scale, long lead time, not suited to small coastal sites).</p>
<p>• Early offshore solar concepts still at pilot stage.</p></td>
<td style="text-align: left;"><em>Fair enough.</em></td>

<tr>
<td style="text-align: left;"><strong>Unique Value Proposition</strong></td>
<td style="text-align: left;">Our SUREWAVE-validated nearshore floating solar platform helps coastal industry and renewable developers who want to grow renewable power where onshore land is blocked, by matching the design to the real wave conditions of each site (breakwater and structure sized to the waves it will actually meet) and by bringing the cost of electricity down with standard solar panels and simple manufacturing, unlike calm-water floating solar that fails in waves and offshore concepts that need costly full protection or specialist vessels.</td>
<td style="text-align: left;"><em>Excellent.</em></td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td style="text-align: left;"><strong>Competitors</strong></td>
<td style="text-align: left;"><p>• Ocean Sun (Norway): thin membrane floats. Strengths: installed references, low material use. Weaknesses: best suited to calm-to-mild conditions; non-standard panel mounting.</p>
<p>• SolarDuck (Netherlands): elevated triangular platforms. Strengths: offshore pilots, strong offshore wind partnerships. Weaknesses: large elevated structures with a high cost per watt; installation needs offshore marine operations.</p>
<p>• Oceans of Energy (Netherlands): modular offshore platform, North Sea pilot. Strengths: North Sea track record. Weaknesses: costly structure, early commercial stage.</p>
<p>• Fred. Olsen 1848 and Saipem / Moss Maritime: offshore-industry players with floating solar concepts. Strengths: offshore engineering capacity and balance sheet. Weaknesses: concepts priced for large offshore projects, not small coastal sites.</p>
<p>• Ciel &amp; Terre and other inland floating solar suppliers. Strengths: large installed base, low cost. Weaknesses: plastic pond floats not designed for sea waves.</p></td>
<td style="text-align: left;"><em>Excellent.</em></td>
</tr>
