SUREWAVE — Structural Reliable Offshore Floating PV Solution Integrating Circular Concrete Floating Breakwater. Deliverable number: "Only internally — Rev. 01". Dated 3 February 2025 (PDF creation date). Written by Clement Germany GmbH.

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1. Layouts

The circular layout was determined in Deliverable D3.3 [1] as the best layout solution for the SUREWAVE project. The evaluation was based on the LCoE-calculations in the SUREWAVE project description with 10 MWp as the minimum plant size. In terms of structural feasibility, it might be useful to not have the full 10 MWp field in one breakwater ring but in several rings. Therefore, it was decided to compare the large 10 MWp layout with five smaller 2 MWp layouts with the same principles.

The P_mpp of each solar panels is given in the product specifications of Sunlit Sea [2] with 537 Wp. Therefore, a total of approx. 18,600 solar panels are required to meet the 10 MWp plant size requirements of the project description. For a smaller layout, approx. 3.700 solar panels are required to achieve 2 MWp per ring.

### 1.3. Materials

The breakwater pontoons are composed of an outer reinforced concrete shell and an inner light weight core.

Figure 5 - Floating breakwater pontoon - section

The deliverable D4.1 [4] concludes concrete mix designs for the breakwater outer shell as follows. There are two reasonable mixes for the LWAC (lightweight aggregate concrete):

- Sample 17 mix design was selected as the optimized circular LWAC solution with a consistency of 455 mm, a 28-days compressive strength of 49 N/mm² and a density of 1.898 kg/m³.
- Sample 18 was compiled using the same mix design as sample 17 but using green cement with a lower carbon footprint. A 28-days compressive strength of 68 N/mm² was achieved and a density of approx. 2.000 kg/m³.

As sample 18 has significantly higher strengths with only slightly higher densities, and as a more ecological cement was already used, the values of sample 18 are considered in the further analysis.

For the HPC (high-performance concrete), the following mix design was selected:

- Sample 2 mix design with a consistency of 665 mm, a 28-days compressive strength of 128 N/mm² and a density of 2.463 kg/m³.

The higher strength of the HPC is also associated with a higher density compared to the LWAC, which would in principle result in a higher overall mass. However, the higher strength also means that the wall thickness can be reduced, resulting in material savings.

The deliverable D4.1 [4] concludes also a cellular concrete mix design for the breakwater inner core as follows:

- Sample 26 mix design was selected as the optimal cellular concrete solution with a density of 365 kg/m³.

This cellular concrete is intended to replace the polystyrene used in conventional construction methods. The typical density of polystyrene is 30 kg/m³.

## 2. Materials

### 2.1. Material demand for pontoons

#### 2.1.1. General

As described in chapter 1.3, different combinations of materials could be used. The following combinations have been chosen:

- Option 1: Conventional design with concrete shell and polystyrene core, whereby the properties of the LWAC were used for the concrete
- Option 2: Hybrid design with LWAC concrete shell and two-piece polystyrene and cellular concrete cores
- Option 3: SUREWAVE design with LWAC concrete shell and cellular concrete cores
- Option 4: SUREWAVE design with HPC concrete shell and cellular concrete cores

The following boundary conditions were defined to be constant for the associated material determination in order to achieve comparability, also with regard to the effectiveness of the wave attenuation:

- Outer dimensions and cubature (except ratio of buoyancy body and keel height)
- Freeboard
- Total draft

### 2.4. Supply chains

It is assumed that the pontoons are manufactured close to the port so that the finished pontoons do not need to be transported to the port of delivery. However, all materials that are not yet installed must first be transported to the port of delivery. Based on typical delivery routes, the following transport distances are assumed:

- The concrete will be produced close to the production yard with a maximum distance of 5 km. Therefore, the transport of concrete is negligible. The concrete components are assumed to be available within a distance of 100 km and can be transported by trailers on land.
- It is assumed that the components for the lightweight core (cellular concrete or polystyrene) are available within a distance of 250 km and can be transported overland on trailers. When transporting polystyrene, it is not the weight but the volume that is the limiting criterion. A maximum of 80 m³ of polystyrene can be transported per standard trailer (tautliner), compared to approximately 24 tonnes for bulk goods.
- Reinforcement, structural steel and tubular steel piles are assumed to be available within a distance of maximum 500 km and can be transported by trailers on land.
- Chains of this size are typically transported by ship from overseas over a distance of more than 15,000 km.
- PVC-pipes, polypropylene ropes and rubbers are assumed to be available within a distance of maximum 250 km and can be transported by trailers on land.

### 2.5. Pontoon production

The main energy consumption in the production of pontoons is due to the production and mixing of the concrete and the short transport of the pontoons within the production areas.

#### 2.5.1. Energy consumption for concrete production

The average energy consumption of a concrete mixing plant varies according to the season. An average of 1.8 kWh/m³ can be assumed for summer operation and approximately 3.2 kWh/m³ for winter operation, including heating. An average value of 2.4 kWh/m³ of concrete is therefore assumed. This applies to the concrete of the outer shell and the cellular concrete of the core. These values are based on a TECWILL Cobra C120 mobile concrete mixing plant, which can be installed directly at the production yard to avoid additional transportation of the fresh concrete.

#### 2.5.2. Energy consumption for transport within the production areas

The pontoons can be transported within the production area to clear the formwork areas using a suitably sized crawler crane. It is assumed that this crane needs an average operating time of 1.5 hours per pontoon to clear the formwork area and another 1.5 hours to move the finished pontoons to the loading area in the port. The associated consumption is approx. 40 litres fuel per hour or 120 litres per pontoon.

Figure 10 - Example of a crawler crane for pontoon transport on production yard

#### 2.5.3. Production time and manpower requirements

It is assumed that 320 man-hours are required to construct a breakwater pontoon. These hours should be divided between a team of 10 workers, with different people working on the formwork, weaving the reinforcement and concreting. It is possible and advisable to work in parallel, depending on the availability of labour.

## 3. Transport and installation

### 3.1. General

It is assumed that the pontoons are manufactured near the port (see supply ports in chapter 1.2) and that the other material required has already been delivered to the port (see supply chains in chapter 2.4).

The number and size of the pontoons is independent of the location but depends on the size of the system. This also applies to the PV system. The anchoring depends on both the layout and the location.

As described in chapter 2.1.1, there are different material compositions for the pontoons, with the hybrid design being considered the most suitable for this purpose.

As the limiting criterion for the ship is the load capacity of the deck area per square metre, the pontoons cannot be stowed in multiple layers. As the external dimensions of the pontoons are the same for all material compositions, the storage space requirement is also the same. This means that the choice of vessel is not directly dependent on the weight of the pontoons.

### 3.7. Assumptions for the evaluation

The respective quantities were combined with the time estimates for the individual work steps. This results in a total time requirement. The following assumptions were also made:

- The transport vessels can be loaded continuously or 24 hours a day.
- The transport vessels can travel continuously or 24 hours a day.
- The daily working time at the final location is considered to be 10 hours.
- This results in a total requirement of days. The consumption of the vessels is calculated in these 10 working hours depending on the activity. In the remaining time, the consumption for own use and holding the position is taken into account.
- In addition to the respective vessel's crew, 20 operatives are scheduled for the assembly of the pontoons. This already includes the labour force in the workboats.
- In addition to the respective ship's crew, 20 operatives are scheduled for the assembly of the PV modules.
- If several vessels are required, these are selected according to the progress of construction and the storage area required.
- In all cases, the PV modules are transported and installed separately from the pontoons and anchoring.
- Maintenance at the breakwater pontoon connections mainly needs to be done at the pontoon connections and requires approx. one hour for a team of 5 operatives. The consumption is provided for one maintenance operation. It is assumed that the maintenance at the breakwater pontoons shall be done approx. 4 times a year.
- According to the manufacturer, 196 PV modules can be installed by 3 workers in one day. To use the installation equipment effectively, a team of at least 20 workers is required to complete the installation of a 2 MWp system (3712 modules) in approximately 4 days. The 10 MWp system (18,624 modules) requires five times as many people, or 16 working days. The same time is assumed for dismantling a PV system.
- The consumption of two working boats is included in the item "Offloading of pontoons and installation".
