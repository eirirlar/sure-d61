**EXECUTIVE SUMMARY**

The document herein is a proof of the breakwater prototype developed with a reduced scale and is associated with task 4.4.

The development of the prototype includes the design and production of the prototype.

The prototype was designed and manufactured with the circular concrete solutions developed in previous tasks (task 4.1, task 4.2 and task 4.3) and reported in previous deliverables (D4.1 \[1\] and D4.2 \[2\]). The circular solutions developed were lightweight aggregate concrete (LWAC) and high-performance concrete (HPC) for the shell and cellular concrete for the inner core of the breakwater. These circular solutions were designed using recycled and other secondary materials or by-products such as recycled aggregates from construction and demolition and glass waste, fly ash and ground granulated blast furnace slags.

Chapter 1 covers the deliverable with an introduction and outlines the objectives and content of the deliverable.

Chapter 2 describes the demo site where the prototype will be deployed for testing in a relevant environment.

Chapter 3 presents the design of the breakwater prototype and the mooring system.

Chapter 4 describes the production of the breakwater prototype accompanied by figures to better understand the overall production process.

The concluding remarks in Chapter 5 demonstrate that it was feasible to manufacture the breakwater prototype without major difficulties.

#  Introduction

The document herein is a summary of the activities carried out in Task 4.4 for the production of the breakwater prototype with a reduced scale.

The prototype was designed and manufactured with the circular concrete solutions developed and tested in task 4.1, task 4.2 and task 4.3 and already reported in deliverables D4.1 \[1\] and D4.2 \[2\]. As a reminder, two types of concrete were developed for the shell of the breakwater: lightweight aggregate concrete (LWAC) and high-performance concrete (HPC). On the other hand, cellular concrete was developed for the core of the breakwater. The optimised mix recipes used for the development of the breakwater prototype were Sample 18 for LWAC, Sample 2 for HPC and Sample 26 for cellular concrete. These circular solutions were designed using recycled and other secondary materials or by-products such as recycled aggregates from construction and demolition, glass waste, fly ash and ground granulated blast furnace slags. More details of these optimised concrete and the mix recipes are shown in the above-mentioned deliverables.

The prototype will be installed at the R&D maritime environment demo site of ACCIONA in Port of Gijón (Spain) to validate the long-term degradation of the circular concrete solutions. This monitoring activity is part of WP6.

The present work comprises:

- Description of the demo site

- Design of the breakwater prototype and mooring system

- Production of the breakwater prototype

## Sizing and design of the breakwater prototype

In our original plan, we intended to use circular lightweight aggregate circular cellular concrete as the inner core. However, during the evaluation phase, we encountered significant issues with the sizing of the prototype. Stability and buoyancy checks revealed that the prototype was extremely large, almost equivalent to a full-size unit, making it unfeasible to manufacture within the project's scope.

To address this challenge, we incorporated a water-resistant material with a very low-density material (approximately 10 kg/m³) with low water absorption properties, such as expanded polystyrene (EPS), into the inner core of the prototype. The inclusion of EPS significantly reduced the weight of the prototype and prevented it from sinking in case of unexpected water absorption in the cellular concrete.

These adjustments were necessary to ensure the feasibility and functionality of the prototype within the project's constraints. Additionally, we provided a protective layer of high-performance concrete (HPC) on the bottom side of the breakwater to prevent direct contact of the EPS with the water surface, thereby avoiding the diffusion of microplastics into the sea and promoting marine growth.

While this concept (see [Figure 5](#_Ref175142666)) is not fully 100% circular for the small-scale prototype, it will be possible to achieve full circularity in the full-scale version of breakwater. Our initial calculations indicate that a full-scale breakwater made of circular mixes will be stable and floatable.

![[]{#_Ref175142666 .anchor}Figure 5. 3D model of the concept of the breakwater prototype](media/image8.png){width="5.920833333333333in" height="3.2534722222222223in"}

For the sizing of the prototype, the following aspects were taken into account:

- Wall thickness = 0.06 meters.

- Top slab thickness = 0.06 meters.

- Bottom slab thickness = 0.1 meters.

- LWAC density = 1900 kg/m^3^.

- HPC density = 2370 kg/m^3^.

- Cellular concrete density = 365 kg/m^3^.

- EPS density= 10 kg/m^3^.

- Marine Growth:

  - Thickness (Based on recommendations in \[3\]): 0.06 meters.

  - Average density in air: 1325 kg/m^3^.

- Metacentric height \> 0.25 meters (in roll \-- mostly transverse to the incoming waves with the highest probability).

- Freeboard \> 0.3 meters.

Initially, the breakwater dimensions resulted in a square prototype in the plan/top view (with length equal to the width). However, given that most waves will be incoming from one side (perpendicular to the harbour wall), corresponding with 180˚ in [Figure *6*](#_Ref175142702), it was decided to ensure stability in that direction by setting the length-to-width ratio to 2.

## Mooring design

After establishing the initial sizing based on stability and buoyancy checks, a numerical model of the prototype was developed to assess loads and motions at the site. All relevant environmental factors, including currents, waves, and tides, were taken into account. Due to the absence of project-specific data for the deployment site, all environmental information was sourced from the oceanographic section of the national ports website. \[4\].

The numerical model of the prototype was built with Orcaflex. It takes into account the linear hydrodynamic coefficients, both radiation and diffraction as well as Froude Krylov wave loads.

The station keeping is assured by means of a four-line catenary spread mooring system, each line consisting of one chain section at the seabed connected to a polyester rope, each connected at the top to the corresponding fairlead of the SUREWAVE prototype, as shown in [Figure 8](#_Ref175210204).

![[]{#_Ref175210204 .anchor}Figure 8. Mooring system configuration on the Orcaflex numerical model](media/image11.emf){width="5.361353893263342in" height="4.037314085739283in"}

The mooring design is based on a set of design load cases. The considered limit states for the mooring design of the floating caisson design were:

- Ultimate limit state (ULS) to ensure that the individual mooring lines have adequate strength to withstand the load effects imposed by extreme environmental actions.

- Accidental limit state (ALS) to ensure that the mooring system has adequate capacity to withstand the failure of one mooring line.

- Robustness check: In addition to the peak period and sea level sensitivity and factor obtained, a set of robustness checks have been performed. These aim to verify that the uncertainties related to very low incoming peak periods or relatively high significant waves are covered.

All loads obtained in the robustness check, without applying load factors, are well below the design load specified for the mooring components. This indicates that even under the unlikely environmental conditions assumed, there are no sudden changes in the expected loads, and the entire deployment is expected to withstand the induced forces.

An initial sensitivity analysis was performed to get the tentative mooring chain diameter required for the deployment. It was based on a single seed and a 3-hour time domain simulation. The wave seed was kept equal in all simulations to enable comparative analysis.

The mooring line anchors were positioned at a radius of 100 meters in the plan/top view to prevent any uplift loads at the anchor points. The polyester section, which is 17 meters long, connects to additional 75-meter-long chain sections. This setup creates a relatively loose catenary shape, allowing for caisson movements while minimizing vertical loads exerted by the mooring system on the prototype. The analysis gave a chain diameter of 10 mm and a polyester rope diameter of 20 mm were appropriate.

The design of anchoring of mooring lines of the prototype is based on gravity anchors, to be realized through a deadweight per line. The weight is defined assumingfriction of the deadweight material with the seabed. It is estimated that the seabed is currently composed of discharged quarry embankment, which has been preloaded during the installation of caissons in the port expansion work. It should be noted that the geomorphological plan of the site does not present the current characteristics of the seabed given the intervention that the area has suffered due to the port expansion works. The characteristics of this dumping material are unknown, as is the degree of current compaction/densification of the seabed. In [Figure *9*](#_Ref175211526), provided by the port, the imprint left by the caissons stored on the seabed can be seen, which confirms that this material is densified/compacted.

![[]{#_Ref175211526 .anchor}Figure 9. Seabed status after the port expansion works (source. Port of Gijon)](media/image12.png){alt="Una caricatura de una persona Descripción generada automáticamente con confianza baja" width="4.85347987751531in" height="2.2857141294838144in"}

The standardization bodies require that gravity anchors be designed to prevent sliding at ULS (foundation failures). In drained conditions, the Eurocode (\[5), \[6\]) proposes that the maximum horizontal load for the extreme condition of pure slip be limited to:

$$H_{d} \leq R_{d} + R_{p;d}$$

$R_{d} = \mu \bullet {V'}_{d} \bullet \tan\delta_{d}$ Or $H_{d} < \mu \bullet ({V'}_{d} \bullet \tan{\delta_{d})/\gamma_{R;h}}$

Where:

- $H_{d}:$ Horizontal design load or horizontal component of the load acting on the foundation

- $R_{d}:$ Design resistance of the foundation to an applied load

- $R_{p;d}:$ Design resistance of the ground pressure on a side of the foundation

- ${V'}_{d}:$ Design load of a vertical load or its normal component to the foundation base

- $\delta_{d}:$ ${\phi'}_{cv;d}$ for in situ concrete and $\delta_{d} = \frac{2}{3}{\phi'}_{cv;d}$ for precast concrete

- ${\phi'}_{cv;d}:$ Design shear resistance angle

- $\gamma_{R;h}:$ Partial safety factor for slip resistance

Under these assumptions, the concrete volume required to comply with slip stability is 2.97 m^3^ for a smooth surface and 2.1 m*^3^* for a rough surface.  Thus, due to the uncertainty regarding the seabed soil composition, the worst-case scenario in terms of friction is considered in the mooring design, i.e. 2.97 m*^3^*.

# Production of the breakwater prototype

This section describes the manufacturing process and the materials used in the production of the SUREWAVE breakwater prototype.

## Materials Description

Different concrete blends were used to manufacture the breakwater prototype. The circular concrete mixes were developed and tested in task 4.1, task 4.2 and task 4.3 according to production requirements established by Clement. The optimised mixes were upscaled and adjusted for larger production volumes and then used to manufacture the prototype. # Conclusions

The main goal of this deliverable is to describe the production process of the breakwater prototype.

The SUREWAVE breakwater prototype was sized and discussed in this document. The sizing of the prototype was largely influenced by the possibility of having water ingress and the generic marine growth estimates, given there is no site-specific data available about the latter. These factors led to a larger prototype than anticipated, which may be adjusted once the site-specific marine growth is better quantified and verified, assuming there is no water ingress.

The mooring system was designed to ensure the station-keeping of the prototype while minimizing vertical loads, thereby preventing significant changes in its floating position. This was achieved by incorporating polyester sections with very low submerged weight at the top of the mooring lines.

Several uncertainty factors and scenarios were considered to ensure the system\'s strength, yet it still allows the breakwater prototype to move with the waves, avoiding excessive loads on both the caisson and the mooring lines. Station-keeping is maintained through a four-line catenary spread mooring system, with each line consisting of a chain section on the seabed connected to a polyester rope, which is then attached to the corresponding fairlead on the breakwater prototype.

Regarding the production process of the breakwater prototype, there were no major complications during the preparation of the mould with the steel reinforcement or when the prototype was cast. The finishing of the top surface of the prototype was rougher than expected, but this is not unusual when casting with LWAC.

The next step will include the mechincal testing of the components and deployment of the breakwater prototype at the demo site located in the Port of Gijon in Spain for its validation. This work will be reported in deliverable D6.5.
