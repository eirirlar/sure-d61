## Executive summary

This is the consortium's plan for exploiting the results of SUREWAVE after the extended project ends on 31 December 2026. SUREWAVE developed an offshore floating photovoltaic (FPV) system that combines a floating breakwater in circular concrete with a redesigned FPV array, a modelling toolchain and a structural health management system. The project started at technology readiness level (TRL) 2–3 and aimed at TRL 4–5 for the system at its end (Grant Agreement, Annex 1). This plan uses TRL 4–5 for the integrated system and for Sunlit Sea's CONNECT platform: SINTEF's technological risk assessment (D7.2), summarised in the integrated sustainability assessment D7.6 (IFEU), rates the integrated concept at TRL 5 on MARIN's wave-basin tests and SINTEF's component tests, and the proposal of September 2026 to EIC Transition, the European Innovation Council's scheme for taking research results towards the market, starts from TRL 4 (Section 4.1). ACCIONA's reduced-scale concrete breakwater prototype, monitored by Ceit, was exposed at sea for about nine months in the Port of Gijón, without the FPV array (Section 6.2).

Sunlit Sea leads work package 8 (WP8) on dissemination, exploitation and communication. As WP8 lead it proposed seven Key Exploitable Results (KERs), one anchored in each beneficiary, and mapped to them the 27 results that the partners reported. Of these, 23 form the seven KERs; four, on network, visibility and work in technical committees, are reported as impact (Section 3.1). KER1 Integrated floating PV system with breakwater is the global solution of the proposal, the product P1 *Offshore FPV plant*, to be marketed by Sunlit Sea AS. Sunlit Sea also led the work package on the global framework and specifications (WP2), in which it established the Stakeholder Advisory Board (D2.4). In the work package on the development of the floating PV solution (WP3), led by Clement Germany, it led the design of the floating PV components and of the global system (D3.1, D3.4). It led the economic assessment (D7.4) and redesigned the hinge (Section 6.1).

KER1 integrates the breakwater, its mooring and anchoring and the transition element designed by Clement Germany; these components remain Clement Germany's (Section 7.1).

The other six KERs are exploited by the partners that developed them: KER2 Circular concrete formulations (ACCIONA), KER3 Floating breakwater design (Clement Germany), KER4 Modelling and testing methods (SINTEF), KER5 Hydrodynamic models and basin testing (MARIN and SINTEF), KER6 Structural health monitoring and eddy-current sensing (Ceit), and KER7 Integrated sustainability assessment (IFEU). Section 3.4 traces each of the proposal's 11 expected results to what was delivered.

This plan gives each KER a level of detail set by the rule in Section 2.4:

- KER1 has a full business plan, prepared by Sunlit Sea with the Horizon Results Booster, the European Commission's free support service for the exploitation of project results.
  - First product: Sunlit Sea CONNECT, Sunlit Sea's Generation 2 floating platform and the core of KER1, for sheltered coastal sites. It replaces the Generation 1 aluminium float, which the SUREWAVE array used. Sunlit Sea started the Generation 2 redesign from the SUREWAVE hinge tests and models and continues it in the parallel project SuRE (Section 4.1). CONNECT goes first into Sunlit Sea's Norwegian reference plants from 2027 and is sold as a kit from 2028. At sheltered sites the plants need little or no breakwater, so the plant cost is mainly the FPV system. The first Norwegian reference plant, in a port in 2027, uses small wavebreakers (light floating barriers against wind waves and vessel wakes) on its exposed side and gives the first field record of the platform behind protection.
  - Breakwater classes: where a site needs more protection, a breakwater is placed only on the exposed side, in one of three classes (light, medium, heavy). Sunlit Sea sized the classes from the SUREWAVE breakwater; Clement Germany is to confirm them. If the EIC Transition proposal is funded, a field demonstrator follows in 2028–2030. The EIC Transition proposal targets the first commercial site with a breakwater class after the project, from about 2030, and Sunlit Sea's financial plan models it in 2031, in its EIC Transition scenario. The site is expected at an inland lake or inner water exposed to wind waves, where other uses such as berths can carry part of the breakwater's cost. The plant owner buys the breakwater. Where Sunlit Sea delivers the full system as engineering-procurement-construction (EPC) contractor, it buys the breakwater and includes it in the EPC price.
  - Open sea: the full open-sea plant with a ring breakwater is the long-term configuration. Sunlit Sea plans an open-sea pilot proposal about 2031, after the exposed-site field record, and commercial plants from about 2035.
- The results that feed into KER1 (breakwater design, concrete, testing, modelling, monitoring and sustainability assessment) are described by their supply or service role, the type of agreement and the logic for a fair price.
- Every partner has a short exploitation pathway and a four-year action plan for 2027–2030. They follow the partners' written exploitation summaries: ACCIONA's of November 2025, Clement Germany's of September 2026 and the other five of September and October 2026.

The SUREWAVE economic assessment D7.4, led by Sunlit Sea (version 2.0), gives the D7.4 reference plant, a 10 MWp (megawatt-peak) open-sea plant in the Mediterranean with a conventional-concrete breakwater, a levelised cost of electricity (LCOE) of €0.076/kWh, an internal rate of return (IRR) of 15.6 % and a net present value (NPV) of €7.3M, with a payback of 10 years. In the Baltic Sea the plant is at break-even, and in the North Sea it is not viable at current costs (Section 4.6). The value-proposition tests (Section 4.3.1) show that buyers and lenders want reference plants and an independent design review before they buy. Sunlit Sea's financial plan shows that the first plants need investment support of about a quarter of their cost (Sections 4.3.1 and 4.7).

KER1's financial plan shows break-even in earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation (EBITDA) for Sunlit Sea in 2030 in all four funding scenarios (base, EIC Transition, EU upside and grant downside), provided the Norwegian reference plants come on time, and a positive net result from 2031. The base case assumes an extension of Sunlit Sea's work in SuRE, a separate Horizon Europe project that funds different work; the decision is pending at CINEA (Section 4.7). In the base case about 28 MWp is delivered over 2027–2031, of which about 7 MWp in 2030 and about 16 MWp in 2031. About 28 % of that volume is at sites with permits, named sites or leads; the rest is target volume. The plan raises €5.5M of equity in two rounds, in 2027 and 2029; the base case uses the first round and a small part of the second, and the rest is a buffer against delay and for growth beyond the plan. Plant investments are financed at plant level, except Sunlit Sea's own first Norwegian reference plant and a minority share in the first multi-megawatt plant. The largest single risk is a one-year delay of the Norwegian reference plants. The risk map places four risks in its action zone: high cost outside the best sites, buyers waiting for a track record, dependency on key people, and the Norwegian reference plants a year late (Section 4.8). The route and targets differ from the proposal's 25 MW offshore pilot in 2025–2027 and its 1,240 MW installed by 2032. Section 4.10 sets out the changes and the reasons.

KER1 is protected by trade secrets and copyright. Patenting of the Generation 2 hinge and float was assessed with a patent attorney and not pursued, because of extensive prior art; a decision on design registration follows by the third quarter (Q3) of 2027, after the partners have confirmed their contributions to the hinge redesign (Sections 7.2 and 7.4). For four years after the project, to 31 December 2030, each beneficiary uses its best efforts to exploit its results; for results not exploited by 31 December 2027, the beneficiaries use the Horizon Results Platform to find interested parties (Sections 6.8 and 13).

SUREWAVE met or exceeded 11 of the 16 dissemination and communication targets of the Grant Agreement and D8.5 in the first table of Section 2.5. The exceptions are peer-reviewed publications, conference workshops, infographics (the sixth is scheduled for 15 December 2026), the final summary video and a press release at the start of the project. Of the 22 further reach targets in the second table, three are met and one is met for 2024 only; three are partly met, five are not met, seven were not measured, no new PhD is recorded, and two are open or apply after the project. One of the met targets is D8.5's target of at least two new commercial agreements or partnerships: it is met for partnerships, with SuRE and CIRCWIND, two Horizon Europe projects that adopt SUREWAVE results; commercial agreements are still to come.

Projects that started before SUREWAVE or run alongside it have used or use its results. MariSol (Research Council of Norway, 2020–2025, Sunlit Sea with the Institute for Energy Technology (IFE) and other partners) used the SUREWAVE nearshore models to calculate a wave-corrected performance ratio. In SuRE (Horizon Europe, September 2024 – August 2027), Sunlit Sea develops Generation 2 prototypes 4 and 5; SuRE grew out of contacts made through the SUREWAVE Stakeholder Advisory Board. SINTEF reports use of its methods in CIRCWIND (Horizon Europe), the Norwegian research centre SFI BLUES and the national project FlexiShape. A national project of SINTEF with the industrial partner GEOPON and Ceit's industrial transfers of its eddy-current technology are planned. The EIC Transition proposal for the version of KER1 for exposed sites, behind a directional breakwater, is under evaluation (Section 10).

## 1. Purpose and scope

The Grant Agreement describes D8.4 as a plan including: (1) Key Exploitable Result (KER) analysis and analysis of the intellectual property rights (IPR); (2) value innovation analysis and market analysis; (3) value proposition and value proposition testing; (4) development of business models, business plans and exploitation action plans. It is the output of Task 8.4, *Exploitation pathways and business plan*. Task 8.4 is to lay the basis for the exploitation agreements between technology developers and exploiters, and to set the ground for decisions on the business models for technology transfer and the rationale for establishing a fair price. Objective O8 of the proposal asks for a definitive business model and business plan for the global solution. The plan is to include the specific complexity of the offshore renewable energy market, IPR, the regulatory framework, market uptake barriers and the fostering of massive replication into the offshore energy sector. This plan replicates at sheltered and nearshore coastal sites first and in the open sea later; Section 4.10 gives the reasons.

The proposal names the global solution P1, *Offshore FPV plant*, as the product to be marketed by Sunlit Sea, with the other partners compensated through licences or royalties for the results that contribute to it. The Grant Agreement states that the individual results R2–R10 are exploited by the partners that developed them. R11, the integrated sustainability assessment framework, is listed with the other results but has no owner in the proposal's table of preliminary KERs; this plan places it with IFEU, which led the integrated assessment D7.6 (Section 3.4). Section 3 maps all results to the KERs.

This plan has three levels:

1. A map of all seven KERs: owner, readiness, exploitation route and relation to the global solution (Section 3).
2. A full business plan for KER1, including the financial plan (Section 4).
3. For the results that feed into KER1: the supply or service role, the type of agreement and the fair-price logic (Section 5). For every partner: an exploitation pathway and a four-year action plan (Section 6).

Section 2.5 reports the dissemination and communication results; Annex A gives the item-by-item record behind them. Sections 7–13 cover intellectual property; standardisation; exploitation agreements; earlier, parallel and follow-on projects; the use of Horizon Europe support; consortium-level risks; and the reporting and post-project obligations under the Grant Agreement, with the targets proposed for the annual exploitation reviews. Section 14 gives the timeline and Section 15 the conclusions. Annex B lists the SUREWAVE deliverables cited, with their lead beneficiaries and dissemination levels.

Each KER is assessed against four criteria:

- the owner's stated commercial intent;
- market readiness;
- its relation to the global solution: core, supplying KER1, or standalone;
- the commitments in the proposal.

The criteria are applied as a rule, not as a score. A KER gets a full business plan when it is a new product line for its owner, is the core of the global solution, and the proposal asks for a business plan for it. A KER that supplies or serves KER1 within its owner's existing business gets an exploitation pathway, a four-year action plan and its role in KER1. Section 3.3 gives the assessment for each KER.

## 3. Key Exploitable Results

### 3.1 How the KERs were identified

The KERs were set in five steps.

1. Starting point. The proposal named one global solution, P1 *Offshore FPV plant*, to be marketed by Sunlit Sea, and 11 expected results, R1–R11. R1 is the global solution itself. The proposal's table of preliminary KERs gave R2–R10 an owner, a type, a business strategy and a target market; the table has no R numbers, and the mapping to R2–R10 follows the result titles. R11, the integrated sustainability assessment framework, is listed with the other results but has no row in that table. D8.5 (month 18, March 2024) reported the mid-term status of R2–R10 and added six findings from the first test campaigns, among them snapping loads on the hinges, the benefit of lighter floats, and the cost of full breakwater protection, which led to the idea of protecting only the exposed sides. The other three concern off-the-shelf panels, the use of panel aluminium frames and sustainable concrete materials. Section 2.1 gives the outcome of each.
2. Candidate KERs. From the proposal's results and D8.5, the WP8 lead proposed seven candidate KERs, one anchored in each beneficiary's main contribution. They were listed in a common input template sent to the partners on 29 September 2026. For each result the template asked for the type, the KER it maps to, a short description, the audience, the steps taken towards exploitation, market maturity, current and target TRL, the exploitation route, the time to market, and the expected revenue or deployment four years after the project.
3. Partner input. ACCIONA's summary (26 November 2025) and Clement Germany's (4 September 2026) were written before the template was sent. The other five partners replied to the request of 29 September between 30 September and 5 October 2026, in their own format rather than in the template fields (Section 2.2). The seven summaries give each result's description and exploitation route; none gives a TRL, a time to market or a revenue figure. No partner proposed a KER of its own. The seven summaries report 27 exploitable results. Publications and dissemination activities were collected by a separate request (Section 2.5). Subtask 8.2.2 of the Grant Agreement foresaw two internal exploitation workshops of all partners to define the definitive KERs. The project record shows the consortium meetings, the Horizon Results Booster sessions of October and November 2026, which concerned KER1 and Sunlit Sea, with a further workshop planned for December (Section 2.3), and this written input round. It holds no record that the two workshops were held as separate events. The definitive KERs were set through the written input round and the WP8 lead's screening and mapping (steps 4 and 5).
4. Screening. The WP8 lead checked each reported result against the technical deliverables and the technical brochure (D8.3), and classified it: two commercial tools in use (Sunlit Sea's nearshore modelling in its site-planning tool, MARIN's wave-diffraction solver DIFFRAC), three validated material formulations (ACCIONA's high-performance concrete (HPC), lightweight aggregate concrete (LWAC) and cellular lightweight concrete (CLC)), one hardware platform on a defined path to market (Sunlit Sea's Generation 2 platform), one sensor technology at demonstrator stage (Ceit's eddy-current corrosion sensor), and methods, software and service lines that the partners add to their existing portfolios. Gains in network and visibility (one from Clement Germany, two from Sunlit Sea) and Clement Germany's contribution to engineering practice and technical committees are reported as impact, not as KERs; the other 23 results form the seven KERs. IFEU reported one result, framed as strengthened visibility, network and expertise in sustainability assessment; KER7 describes IFEU's application, in D7.6, of the integrated life-cycle sustainability assessment method behind that expertise. Where sources differ, this plan uses the figure of the result's owner: from the owner's deliverable where it gives an exact value, otherwise from the owner's summary. The check found these differences:
   - LWAC: ACCIONA's input gives its mix as 1,950 kg/m³ and 68 MPa, the values of D4.1's Sample 18, made with a CEM III cement (blast-furnace cement, in which slag replaces part of the clinker), which the prototype's top slab used (D4.3); D4.1 preferred Sample 17 (Section 6.2). D4.2 gives 1,850 kg/m³ and D8.3 about 1,800 kg/m³. This plan uses ACCIONA's figure for the mix used in the prototype.
   - Cement in the circular HPC: ACCIONA's input gives 5 % of the cement replaced by fly ash and slag; D4.1's mix uses 400 kg/m³ of cement instead of 500, with fly ash and slag; D7.3 gives a 20 % lower clinker content. All sources agree on the 128 MPa strength. This plan uses the D4.1 recipe.
   - Mooring optimisation: MARIN's summary expresses the reduction as a ratio of mooring-chain mass, and D5.1 (MARIN) in per cent of mooring-line mass for each site and farm size. This plan uses D5.1's per-cent figures (Section 6.5).
   - Hydrodynamic modelling: the IPR management plan D1.8 attributes the hydrodynamic models to MARIN. SINTEF's input and its preprint on keeled-breakwater dynamics (Preprints 202610.0011, 2026) document SINTEF's own high-fidelity breakwater modelling. This plan lists both, each owned by its developer.
   - Hinge inputs: D1.8 names SINTEF, Ceit and MARIN as the partners whose assessments informed the hinge design. Ceit's structural integrity assessment (D5.3) analyses the breakwater. Its conclusions note that the project had found the hinges to be the part of the system most prone to failure, without analysing them, and announce further modelling of the new hinge, to be reported with the new hinge concept; that modelling is not among the reported results. The modelling of the new connection reported in the project is SINTEF's finite element analysis in D6.3. This plan names SINTEF's connection tests and analysis and MARIN's basin tests as the inputs to the redesign. D1.8 also asks Sunlit Sea and the documented contributors to confirm the contributions to the hinge redesign before any registration, licence or commercial deployment (Section 7.4).
5. Mapping. Each result was placed under the candidate KER it mainly contributes to. Every reported result could be placed under one of the seven candidates, so the seven were kept. Each KER is built around its owner's main exploitation route. SINTEF's main contribution is KER4. SINTEF reported its aerodynamic and hydrodynamic modelling together; this plan places the aerodynamic method with SINTEF's other methods in KER4 and the hydrodynamic modelling with MARIN's in KER5, because SINTEF's and MARIN's hydrodynamic modelling serve the same customers for hydrodynamic design services, while ownership stays separate (Section 7.1). The links between results were recorded:
   - KER1 integrates the breakwater, its mooring and anchoring and the transition element designed by Clement Germany (D3.2, D3.3), with MARIN's mooring optimisation (D5.1).
   - SINTEF's connection tests and MARIN's basin tests with elastically scaled hinges informed Sunlit Sea's hinge redesign; Section 4.1 gives the hinge generation each tested and how the generations are numbered.
   - Clement Germany's breakwater options use ACCIONA's concrete.
   - Ceit's structural health management system uses SINTEF's bending tests of ACCIONA's concrete mixes, Clement Germany's breakwater geometry, MARIN's hydrodynamic load histories and Ceit's own finite element models.

## 4. KER1 definitive business plan

### 4.1 The result and the product

KER1 is the SUREWAVE global solution: floating PV for coastal and offshore waters, protected by a floating breakwater where the waves require it. It integrates Sunlit Sea's floating PV array with Clement Germany's breakwater, transition element, mooring and anchoring (KER3):

- the internal FPV array of floats joined by flexible connections, designed by Sunlit Sea (D3.1 and the global system design D3.4, both led by Sunlit Sea);
- the external protective breakwater, designed by Clement Germany (D3.2);
- the transition element, a floating pipe of 219 mm diameter between breakwater and array, connected to both with pre-tensioned polypropylene ropes, designed by Clement Germany (D3.3);
- the connections between the breakwater units, and the anchoring and mooring, designed by Clement Germany (D3.3), with the mooring optimised by MARIN (D5.1, led by MARIN);
- the electrical system of the array.

Integrating these components in KER1 transfers no ownership of them (Section 7.1). The confirmation of contributions that the IPR management plan D1.8 asks for before a commercial agreement is set out in Section 7.4.

The open-sea plant studied in the project, called the D7.4 reference plant in this plan, is an array inside a ring breakwater 346 m across. Clement Germany's D3.3 set the plant at 10 MWp, the minimum plant size of the proposal's cost-of-energy calculations, and sized the ring for about 18,600 panels of 537 Wp. The global system design D3.4 placed 7.16 MWp in the ring with the floats used at the time and noted that a more efficient use of the sheltered space would raise it. Clement Germany later compiled the material quantities for the 10 MWp plant and for a 2 MWp version with a ring 173 m across; the economic assessment D7.4 takes its anchoring, mooring, transition-element and connection quantities from them. D7.4 keeps the rings and breakwaters but replaces the panels with modules of about 730 Wp. D7.4 attributes the change of panels to the new hinge system. The larger panels change the number of panels, the array layout and the installed power. Its 10 MWp plant has 10,375 kWp installed (10.4 MWp), and its investment figures refer to that capacity. D7.4 assesses a single 2 MWp array, five 2 MWp arrays and the 10 MWp plant. It states plant sizes in MW of installed peak capacity; this plan writes MWp for the plants it assesses.

Hinge generations are numbered separately from the platform generations. The figures below are measured or calculated on different things, and each is named with what it applies to:

- First-generation hinges. In SINTEF's laboratory tests, complete hinges reached a mean tensile load of 3.69 kN and buckled at low compressive loads (D6.1; Preprints 202611.1612). D6.1 compares this with the 8–10 kN expected from the WP5 simulations and recommends a redesign that at least meets that requirement and avoids the collisions that the low compressive strength can cause. In MARIN's first basin campaign the hinges buckled in harsh seas (Energies 17(9) 2059, 2024), with snap loading after the buckling (D8.3).
- Redesign. Sunlit Sea redesigned the connection and delivered injection-moulded test components to SINTEF (D6.3). The third-generation hinge spans the full float edge, 1.88 m (D7.6).
- Tests of the redesigned connection (D6.3). SINTEF tested a segment that D6.3 gives as one fifth of the full connection. Its mean tensile load was 2.04 kN with the polypropylene rope that holds the two halves together, and 8.36 kN with a steel rod in place of the rope. Its mean compressive capacity was 20.23 kN; D6.3 concludes that the improved compression behaviour will avoid the early buckling and snap loading of the first generation. With the rope, the fatigue analysis gives about 2.5 × 10¹¹ load cycles to failure, against 7.88 × 10⁸ cycles for 25 years at one wave a second. With a steel rod, the 25 years are reached if the force in the polyurethane part stays below 6.9 kN. D6.3 calls the fatigue analysis a rough estimate that does not cover mixed-mode loading. Ageing of the polyurethane is not included. The test components had shrinkage gaps of 1–2 mm and some early cracks, and D6.3 finds that control of the production process is critical for series production.
- Second basin campaign. The first campaign had shown frequent connector buckling. MARIN's second campaign used second-generation connectors, longer and thicker than the first generation, with higher compressive stiffness (Energies 19(7) 1609, 2026), fitted in pairs between adjacent floats (D7.2, Preprints 202611.1173). Sunlit Sea's technical brochure D8.3 reports no measurable hinge buckling or snap loading in this campaign; MARIN's papers do not state it. MARIN used the measured motions of the modules and the breakwater to validate its diffraction model before simulating the 2 MWp farm (Energies 19(7) 1609). The full-edge third-generation hinge was not in the basin model.
- Design values (SINTEF's technological risk assessment, D7.2 and Preprints 202611.1173, summarised in D7.6). The design break load of the full-width third-generation hinge at the ultimate limit state, the highest load the hinge is designed to carry, is 37 kN, against 14 kN for the paired second-generation corner hinges. The maximum hinge loads simulated in the 50-year sea states (D7.6, summarising D7.2; Preprints 202611.1173) are 34 kN at the Baltic site and 33 kN in the Mediterranean, below the design value, and about 40 kN in the North Sea, about 8 % above it; with the paired second-generation hinges they were 45, 43 and 54 kN, against those hinges' design break load of 14 kN. D7.6 states that the robustness of the redesigned hinges could not be fully validated within the project. Exposed open-sea sites in the North Sea need a further hinge generation (Section 4.8).

D6.3 scales the segment result to the full connection: five segments with the rope carry about 10 kN, almost three times the 3.69 kN of a complete first-generation hinge. On the same scaling, five segments with a steel rod carry about 42 kN. #### 4.1.1 Breakwaters for nearshore sites

SUREWAVE designed one breakwater cross-section: Clement Germany's unit for the open sea (D3.2), in a full ring around the plant (D3.3). Most sites in Sunlit Sea's markets are nearshore or inland. There the waves are limited by the fetch, the stretch of open water the wind blows over, so they are lower and shorter than at sea and come from some directions only. Sunlit Sea has sized three breakwater classes for such sites; the SUREWAVE unit is the heaviest of them. The two lighter classes are scaled from it with its design data (D3.2, D5.1), with the same construction and double-keel shape. The costs use D7.4's breakwater and anchoring costs as modelled, plus Sunlit Sea's own estimates for fabrication, fittings, transport, supplier margin and mobilisation, which D7.4 does not contain (Section 4.6.2). The classes are a sizing study, not a design: they are to be confirmed, engineered and supplied by Clement Germany, the owner of the breakwater design (KER3), on the terms in Section 5.

The sizing method:

- Design waves: fetch-limited wave spectra of the JONSWAP type (from the Joint North Sea Wave Project), the standard spectrum for wind seas, with the growth curves of the US Shore Protection Manual of 1984. The design wind is 22 m/s at 10 m height, a working assumption of Sunlit Sea to be checked against the wind data of each site.
- Wave transmission behind the breakwater: the Macagno formula, which gives the share of the wave height that passes a fixed floating breakwater, as used in D3.2 and D5.1.
- Criteria at the array: short waves, with periods of 3 s or less, at most 0.45 m significant height. D3.2 names the band of 1–3 s as critical for the array (Section 6.3 gives the transmission of the SUREWAVE unit in it), and D5.1 finds the array's hinge loads peaking in the band. The 0.45 m is a 0.5 m limit for the short waves, Sunlit Sea's working assumption for the platform, with a margin of 10 %. All waves together: at most 0.6 m significant height for the platform for sheltered sites and at most 0.8 m for the exposed-site version under development. These limits are Sunlit Sea's working assumptions, to be confirmed in the demonstrator.
- Cross-sections: a width-to-draft ratio of 2–3, as for the SUREWAVE unit, and a minimum width of 2.5 m for stability and access. A search found the lightest section meeting both criteria for each wave class, and the chosen classes are somewhat larger, as a margin: 2.5 × 1.0 m against 2.5 × 0.9 m (light), 4.0 × 1.5 m against 3.0 × 1.2 m (medium), and the SUREWAVE unit against 4.5 × 1.5 m (heavy).

| Breakwater class | Site wave conditions (design significant wave height in a 22 m/s design wind) | Cross-section | Status | Platform behind it |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Light | Mild, up to about 0.65 m, where the short waves exceed 0.45 m or the design wave height exceeds 0.6 m: lakes and inner waters with a fetch of up to about 1.6 km | About 2.5 m wide, 1 m draft; about 30 t per 20 m unit | Desk sizing | CONNECT, the platform for sheltered sites |

The draft is measured to the bottom of the keels, so a unit weighs less than a solid block of the stated width and draft. A light unit of about 30 t can be cast, trucked and lifted with ordinary harbour equipment.

The platform needs no breakwater where the short waves stay below about 0.45 m and the design wave height below about 0.6 m (working limits, to be confirmed in the demonstrator). At mild sites the short waves usually exceed their limit first, so a light breakwater is needed even where the design wave height is close to 0.6 m. Longer waves pass a floating breakwater largely unreduced, so at medium and rough sites the limit for all waves decides the class, and rough sites need the exposed-site version of the platform even behind the SUREWAVE unit. Above about 1.3 m the waves that pass the breakwater exceed that version's working limit. There, or where open-sea swell reaches the site, the full SUREWAVE open-sea design applies: the same unit in a ring, with mooring and anchoring sized for open-sea storms, for design waves of up to 6.5 m in the Mediterranean (D5.1). The open-sea design is checked against another criterion. SINTEF's risk assessment (D7.2) reports the hinge loads of the array simulated in each site's 50-year sea states and compares them with the hinge's design break load (Section 4.1), instead of applying the working wave limits used here for the commercial platform. By that check the hinges carry the extreme loads at the Baltic and Mediterranean sites and not yet in the North Sea.

Each site is classified with Sunlit Sea's site-planning tool. The tool is built on the SUREWAVE nearshore wave and wind models, from ERA5 (the climate reanalysis of the European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts) and GEBCO (the General Bathymetric Chart of the Oceans) data, and gives the wave and wind conditions along the full perimeter of a planned plant. From these conditions and fetch-limited wave growth, the design waves are set for each direction. This gives the site a wave class and an exposure sector, from about 20° (waves from one side) to half a circle (180°). The breakwater is placed only across the exposed sector, widened by 10° on each side for the spread of wave directions in a wind sea, as an arc or a straight line, whichever is shorter. It is extended at the ends so that waves bending round them do not reach the array. It stands 15 m from the array, the distance in D7.4's cost model for its 2 MWp ring. Its length, and so its cost, follows from the sector, the plant size and that distance.

Three findings from this work shape the product:

- The breakwater cost per kilowatt-peak roughly halves from a plant of about 3 MWp to one of 10 MWp (light class, one exposed side) and rises steeply below 1 MWp: at a plant of a few hundred kWp it is close to ten times the value at 10 MWp. Small plants need natural shelter.
- A breakwater can serve other uses at the same time: berths, bathing or sauna platforms, boathouses or a café. Where the site owner has such a use, part of the breakwater's cost is carried by it. This fits leisure lakes, marinas and harbours.
- All three classes have natural roll and heave periods close to the peak periods of their design waves: about 2.7 and 2.5 s against 2.3 s (light), 3.4 and 3.2 s against 3.1 s (medium), 3.8 and 3.6 s against 3.7 s (heavy), scaled from the periods D5.1 gives for the SUREWAVE unit. The Macagno formula treats the breakwater as rigid and is least reliable near these periods, where a moving breakwater lets more wave energy through. The classes may need more mass or damping than the first sizing gives. This is to be checked with Clement Germany and in basin tests.

### 4.3 Market

Floating solar reached at least 8.7 GW worldwide at the end of 2024 (Photovoltaic Power Systems Programme of the International Energy Agency, IEA PVPS, Trends in Photovoltaic Applications 2025). Of the 7.7 GW installed by the end of 2023, almost 90 % was in Asia; the Netherlands and France were the largest markets outside Asia (IEA PVPS Task 13, Floating PV Plants, 2025). Most European plants are on quarry lakes, sandpit lakes, irrigation ponds and other man-made inland water. IEA PVPS rates floating PV on inland water above TRL 8 and offshore floating PV at TRL 3–5, with only a few pilot and demonstration projects (Task 13, 2025).

Floating solar at sea is at pilot stage in Europe. The largest European marine units reported are 0.5–1 MW: SolarDuck's 0.5 MWp pilot in the Dutch North Sea, installed in July 2024 (RWE, 2024), and BlueNewables' 1 MW demonstrator of two 500 kW platforms at the Port of Valencia, whose first unit has been in testing since mid-July 2026 (pv magazine, 14 July 2026), with the full 1 MW reported in testing in October 2026 (Energías Renovables, October 2026). Sunlit Sea's review of company announcements and trade press from 2024 to 2026 found no commercial multi-megawatt floating solar plant on European coastal water. The potential is documented in two countries:

- Spain: 4.45–6.48 GW of floating solar at sea within the national maritime spatial plans, in a study by the University of A Coruña (Journal of Cleaner Production, 2026, reported by pv magazine, June 2026).
- The Netherlands: scenarios with 200 GWp of solar power in 2050, of which 45 GWp at sea (TNO, the Netherlands Organisation for Applied Scientific Research, floating solar programme page, accessed 2026).

The first commercial niche is plants of 0.3–10 MWp in sheltered coastal water next to industry and ports. Such sites have local power demand and a water area, but no land to spare. In Norway, the Norwegian Water Resources and Energy Directorate (NVE), the energy regulator, expects grid scarcity in large parts of the country and land-use conflict to limit ground-mounted solar before 2030: by February 2024 it had received applications for close to 1.4 GW of ground-mounted plants and licensed three plants with 14 MW, against about 10 GW needed for the political goal of 8 TWh of solar power by 2030 (NVE, note on solar power and other local generation, 5 February 2024). Sunlit Sea has not yet sized the sheltered coastal segment. The count of suitable coastal sites per country, planned with the customer interviews in early 2027 (Section 4.3.1), gives the first estimate.

Two customer segments:

1. Coastal industry and ports with local power demand: local, fixed-price renewable power without waiting for grid capacity, bought under long-term power purchase agreements.
2. Developers and independent power producers building 0.3–10 MWp plants on coastal water: a wave-tested floating system delivered as a kit, with site engineering that lenders and insurers accept.

An adjacent segment for the platform is large lakes and hydropower reservoirs exposed to waves. For the breakwater classes, lakes exposed to wind waves are the first sites (Section 4.6.2).

At sea in Europe, the competitors are few and at pilot stage. Outside Europe and on inland water, suppliers have installed bases, but for calm water or tropical lagoons. Status from public sources:

- Fred. Olsen 1848 (pretensioned rope mesh): pilot stage; a DNV Statement of Conformity to DNV-RP-0584 for a 3 MW island at significant wave heights up to 3.5 m, and a DNV technical review of its design methodology (DNV, July 2026; pv magazine, September 2026).
- Ocean Sun (membrane on a floating ring): Bureau Veritas type certificate for its floating structure (Ocean Sun, November 2025).
- SolarDuck (elevated platforms): the 0.5 MWp North Sea pilot above (RWE, 2024), with a Bureau Veritas prototype certificate (pv magazine, March 2024).
- Oceans of Energy (offshore platforms): an array of about 1,400 panels at an offshore wind farm in the Dutch North Sea, installed in August 2025 and withdrawn in 2026 after thermal incidents traced to a connector defect (CrossWind, May 2026); partner in the EU project BAMBOO.
- BlueNewables (raised catamaran platforms): the 1 MW Valencia demonstrator above (pv magazine, July 2026).
- Swimsol (rigid platforms for tropical lagoons): sea-based plants in the Maldives, the largest 2.4 MW (pv magazine, April 2026).
- Inland float suppliers such as Ciel & Terre: plastic pontoons for calm water; Ciel & Terre had completed about 820 MW in 280 projects by mid-2023 (TaiyangNews, 2023) and is a partner in the EU project SuRE (CORDIS, the EU's database of funded research projects).

The withdrawn North Sea array failed on an electrical connector, not on its structure. Sunlit Sea's competitor review finds electrical and balance-of-system reliability the recurring failure at sea in 2025–2026; the qualification programme of Section 4.5 covers the electrical components as well as the structure.

In Sunlit Sea's assessment, competition at sea is decided on references, on independent review of the design and on the price per project, since no supplier publishes list prices.

Value proposition for segment 1: renewable power produced on the host's own water area, next to its load, at a fixed long-term price, from a system designed for the site's waves. For segment 2: a wave-tested floating system with site engineering that sizes structure and breakwater to the site's wave conditions, at a cost per watt that makes coastal sites investable.

#### 4.3.3 Sustainability evidence

The integrated sustainability assessment D7.6, led by IFEU, compares the open-sea SUREWAVE plant of about 10 MWp, in several European sea scenarios, with electricity from coal, the fossil benchmark D7.6 uses. Compared with coal, every scenario is more climate-friendly, uses fewer fossil resources, has a low land-use footprint and has lower social supply-chain risks. The plant's impacts are higher than those of land-based PV or wind power. Local environmental impacts can occur and have to be managed. Every indicator reaches an acceptable outcome in at least one scenario, and the Mediterranean is the most advantageous location. More profitable technical choices tend to be more sustainable too, and one large uniform plant is preferred over five units of about 2 MWp for its lower material intensity, if offshore experience supports its durability and shading of sensitive ecosystems is not an issue at the site; D7.6 assessed the split layout for the North Sea only.

D7.6 sets conditions for a sustainable deployment:

- target markets where the plants replace fossil-based power and avoid competing with wind and land-based PV;
- high efficiency: high solar yield, mild waves and wind, small depths, short distance to shore or grid and to a harbour;
- no substantial disturbance of sensitive shallow-water habitats or marine-mammal migration routes;
- socially beneficial procurement.

D7.6 recommends demonstrating floating PV at sea at an intermediate scale as quickly as the remaining technological de-risking allows. The KER1 plan takes the de-risking steps at sheltered and exposed nearshore sites first, from 2027, and plans an open-sea pilot proposal about 2031 (Section 4.6.1).

This evidence supports the value proposition to lenders and permitting authorities. D7.6 assessed the open-sea plant with the aluminium floats of the SUREWAVE array (Section 4.1). For sheltered and nearshore sites, and for the polyurethane float of CONNECT, the evidence is indicative until it is assessed for those plants.

### 4.5 Development and qualification

CONNECT will be qualified before series production against the DNV floating solar documents (DNV, May 2026): DNV-ST-C108, Structural design of floats for floating photovoltaic systems; DNV-ST-E309, Station keeping of floating solar photovoltaic systems; and the recommended practice DNV-RP-0584, Design, development and operation of floating solar photovoltaic systems. The programme covers:

- break load and fatigue of the hinge, with the holding element chosen in the prototype 4 tests (Section 4.1), and fatigue of the mooring under wave cycles;
- durability of the polyurethane in sun and seawater: accelerated ultraviolet (UV) ageing to the International Electrotechnical Commission's technical specification IEC TS 62788-7-2, followed by mechanical testing, which also covers the ageing that the fatigue analysis of D6.3 left out;
- control of the casting process, since the test components of D6.3 had shrinkage gaps and early cracks;
- corrosion protection of the bottom plate;
- marine growth;
- marine electrical components and cable movement;
- field monitoring of the first plants.

- access-rights requests to SINTEF and MARIN, before the design freeze, and the confirmation of contributions to the hinge redesign (Q1 2027; Sections 7.4 and 9);

Across markets, the main barriers are bankability (lenders and insurers want an independently reviewed design and plants in operation), permits before debt, and marine contractors and capital for each plant. The 2026 editions of the DNV floating solar standards and recommended practice give the basis for an independently reviewed design.

Replication rests on four elements:

- the kit model, which lets local developers and contractors build plants without Sunlit Sea's balance sheet;
- the site-planning tool, which screens new sites and sizes the protection;
- distributors or licensees from 2030 in markets Sunlit Sea does not serve directly;
- the step from sheltered to exposed sites and then to the Mediterranean open sea, which widens the range of usable sites.

### 4.6 Economics of the D7.4 reference plant

The SUREWAVE economic assessment D7.4, led by Sunlit Sea (version 2.0, 20 November 2026), covers the full life cycle of the D7.4 reference plant: construction, 50 years of operation with PV replacement after 25 years, and decommissioning. The electricity sale price is €0.12/kWh, which D7.4 takes as the target price of the SUREWAVE planning and application process, and the base discount rate is 8.5 %. The results are given as NPV, IRR, LCOE and payback time.

| Site, 10 MWp plant (10.4 MWp installed) | NPV | IRR | LCOE | Payback |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Mediterranean | €7.3M | 15.6 % | €0.076/kWh | 10 years |
| Baltic Sea | about €0 | 8.5 % | €0.120/kWh | not reached |
| North Sea | −€5.8M | 3.4 % | €0.173/kWh | not reached |

Version 2.0 adds the clean-up of the site at the end of life and states the scope limits of the assessment, in reply to the project officer's request for revision of 16 October 2026. Its rebuilt cost model, checked against a separate implementation with automated tests, also corrects the errors of version 1.0 (section 2.5 of D7.4):

- structure maintenance and the capital cost of inverters, transformers and the supervisory control and data acquisition (SCADA) system were described but not calculated;
- the PV replacement in year 25 was missing from the LCOE;
- a non-standard discount factor was used, and operation was counted from year 0;
- the IRR was calculated on discounted cash flows over 25 years only;
- the electricity prices of the pessimistic and optimistic scenarios were swapped, and some rows of these scenarios used inputs from the wrong scenario;
- smaller errors in the freight cost, the pontoon cost totals, the PV replacement cost of the 5 × 2 MWp cases and the sensitivity tables.

In the Mediterranean, the LCOE rises from €0.062 to €0.076/kWh and the NPV falls from €8.9M to €7.3M. The IRR rises from 10.7 % to 15.6 % because version 2.0 calculates it on undiscounted cash flows over the full 50-year life. The version 1.0 figures appear in the technical brochure D8.3 and in the integrated sustainability assessment D7.6; the ranking of the three sites is the same in both versions.

D7.4 assesses plants of 2 MWp and 10 MWp built from the SUREWAVE reference design, with the aluminium floats of the SUREWAVE array; the cost of CONNECT's polyurethane float enters through Sunlit Sea's financial plan (Sections 4.1 and 4.7). The Mediterranean is the first market for the open-sea configuration. The Baltic Sea breaks even at the 8.5 % base rate, which is its IRR. The North Sea is viable only at discount rates of 3 % or lower, as available for publicly backed demonstration projects, or with lower cost or shared infrastructure. Excluding cables, D7.4 finds non-panel costs at sea close to those of onshore PV, because the modular structures avoid most of the offshore installation premium known from wind. The breakwater alone costs about €1.2M in conventional concrete, about 13 % of the investment. D7.4's cost item for the breakwater and its concrete bridge pontoons, with anchors, chains, ropes and polyvinyl chloride (PVC) pipes, is about €212/kWp, under a quarter of the investment of the D7.4 reference plant. Starting at sheltered sites, where little or no breakwater is needed, removes most of that cost from the first plants.

#### 4.6.2 First sites with breakwater protection

The first breakwater protection in operation will be the small wavebreakers of the first Norwegian reference plant, a port plant, on its side exposed to local wind waves, in 2027. It is the first field test of placing protection only where the waves come from. The first sites for the breakwater classes are expected to be inland lakes and inner waters exposed to wind waves from one direction. Lakes are an adjacent segment for the platform alone (Section 4.3), but they come first for the classes, because their waves are limited by the fetch and the light class is enough. The baseline site of the EIC Transition demonstrator, the step to exposed sites, is such a lake, reached through Clement Germany's relationship with the site owner (Section 10). There a light breakwater covers the exposed side, and the site owner's other uses, such as berths or bathing platforms, can carry part of its cost. The small wavebreakers of the first Norwegian reference plant are bought from a wavebreaker supplier (Section 4.4.1); they are not built from the SUREWAVE breakwater design or its classes.

The EIC Transition proposal targets a first commercial installation of 1–5 MWp after the project: immediately after completion in its summary, about 2030, and within 12–24 months after completion in its exploitation plan, subject to the site owner, permits and finance. The financial plan models it in 2031, the first full year after the project (Section 4.7).

If Sunlit Sea takes part in a Horizon Europe application under the call "Improved system design for innovative PV applications" (HORIZON-CL5-2026-11-D3-14) of the co-programmed European Partnership for Innovation in Photovoltaics (EUPI-PV) (Section 10), and it is funded, a nearshore demonstration with a breakwater could come earlier, in 2029.

The breakwater's share of the plant cost, the breakwater cost divided by the cost of PV plant and breakwater together, falls steeply with plant size. At a pilot-scale demonstrator, for which Sunlit Sea's planning for the EIC Transition proposal uses 50–300 kWp (the proposal leaves the size to site selection), the breakwater costs from about as much as the PV plant to several times as much. At about 5 MWp it is around a tenth of the plant cost, and at a few tens of MWp a few per cent; where the site owner's other uses carry part of its cost, the share is lower. The estimate is for a light breakwater on the exposed side of a plant built by a kit buyer. It rests on the breakwater and anchoring costs of D7.4 version 2.0 as modelled, plus Sunlit Sea's estimates for fabrication, fittings, transport, supplier margin and mobilisation. It is indicative and is to be confirmed by Clement Germany.

The demonstrator's breakwater would be funded by the EIC Transition grant, if the proposal is funded.

From about 5 MWp, a light breakwater scaled from the SUREWAVE design opens sites with wind waves to floating PV. The first commercial plant with a breakwater class serves as a reference and earns little margin. With the breakwater half carried by other uses, its cost of power is well above the average award of €0.048/kWh in Germany's solar tender of July 2025 (Bundesnetzagentur, August 2025). Selling to the grid does not cover its cost; it needs a buyer who values the power behind the meter, or investment support. The step from demonstrator to commercial plants also gives the field record for the exposed-site configuration.

The plan was completed on 22 November 2026. It projects Sunlit Sea's income statement, cash flow and balance sheet for 2027–2031, in euro at nominal prices, in four scenarios for public funding, with sensitivities on cost, price, volume and timing. This section gives the results for the company as a whole, in relative terms where a euro amount combined with the volumes would reveal a price or a cost per watt. The Booster mentor is to review the plan at the second business-planning workshop, planned for December 2026; Sunlit Sea then updates it after the first equity round, for adoption by its board in Q2 2027 (milestone M2, Section 4.9). - Beyond the plan period, the indicative outlook has kit sales of about 18 MWp in 2032, growing to 25–45 MWp a year in 2033–2035. Breakwater classes are bought as set out in Section 5. The Norwegian sites that Sunlit Sea delivers as EPC contractor need no breakwater class on current site data, and kit buyers buy any breakwater themselves as plant owners, so the plan has no breakwater class in its revenue or costs. Two sensitivities test this. The first Norwegian reference plant, which Sunlit Sea owns, uses small wavebreakers on its exposed side. They are not yet quoted and are tested at about €0.1M. That lowers cash by about €0.1M and the result over the period by about €0.05M, since the cost is depreciated over the plant's life. If the two larger Norwegian sites needed a light breakwater on one side and it were not priced into the EPC contracts, the result over the period would be lower, and 2031 EBITDA and cash would stay positive; the plan prices a breakwater into the EPC contract wherever the site study shows exposure. At sites that need a breakwater class, Sunlit Sea's gain is the kit and engineering for a site that would otherwise be closed to floating PV.

Conditions for the plan:

- Kit price: the plan's kit gross margin target is 40–50 %, and the plan meets it at today's kit price and production cost. Cost reductions from series moulds, a lighter bottom plate, casting near the markets where that lowers cost, and volume purchasing give room to lower the price where buyers need it, or to absorb a breakwater at more exposed sites.
- Investment support for the first plants: at first-of-a-kind cost, the first plants need investment support of about a quarter of their cost to match the host's alternative supply. Support of that order is sought from national schemes for first-of-a-kind energy technology. Without support, plants built from the kit are competitive where power costs more than at the first Norwegian sites. Examples are industry with higher power prices, sites limited by grid capacity, and southern Europe, where the yield is higher. In markets with poor solar irradiation, national support for floating solar is assumed.
- References and volume: the plan is most sensitive to the timing of the Norwegian reference plants and to kit volume and margin in 2030–2031.
  - If the Norwegian reference plants and kit sales come one year later, the net result over 2027–2031 falls more than in any other single sensitivity, 2031 EBITDA is slightly negative, and cash falls below zero (lowest year-end cash about −€0.3M). About €0.5M more equity keeps a cash buffer of €0.2M. The second round then moves to 2030.
  - If the 10 MW-class contract is not won, 2031 EBITDA and cash stay positive, and the net result over the period is lower.
  - Kit volume 30 % lower in 2030–2031, a kit price about 6–7 % lower, or a kit cost higher by the same amount per watt, each lowers 2031 EBITDA, which stays positive in every case; cash stays positive.
  - A combined commercial downside (one year later, kit volume 30 % lower and no further kit cost reduction) gives a negative net result in every year of the plan and a negative 2031 EBITDA. It needs about €1.0M more equity, or the 2029–2031 hires and the second mould set are held back.

All of these depend on the first plants operating as designed, and the plan ties hiring and investment to four decision gates set by those plants:

1. First equity round closed (Q1 2027): order the first Norwegian reference plant and start hiring.
2. Investment decision by the plant company at the first multi-megawatt site (Q4 2027): sign the EPC contract and commit the minority share; if the decision slips, the 2028 hires are delayed.
3. First Norwegian reference plant through its first winter, with loads and yield within design values (Q2 2028): release float production for the multi-megawatt plant and order the industrial moulds.
4. The multi-megawatt plant in operation for a year, and a letter of intent for the 10 MW-class contract (2029): raise the second round, order the second mould set and hire the application engineers.

### 4.8 Main risks for KER1

The feasibility of the intervention is rated on the same scale. A grade above 50 with a feasibility above 5 puts a risk in the action zone, and a grade up to 50 with a feasibility above 5 in the control zone. A feasibility below 5 puts a risk in the warning zone (grade above 50) or the no-action zone (grade up to 50); no KER1 risk has a feasibility below 5. ### 6.2 ACCIONA (KER2)

Value proposition: circular concrete mixes for marine structures: a cellular concrete that gives floating structures a mineral core in place of expanded polystyrene (CLC), a lighter structural concrete with recycled glass aggregate (LWAC), and a stronger concrete with high resistance to water and chloride ingress that allows thinner shells (HPC).

Market: the proposal names international contractors of large infrastructure projects; ACCIONA names its own ongoing and future construction projects and the clients of its consultancy.

ACCIONA led WP4, New circular material solutions, with the material design and characterisation (D4.1), the laboratory tests of the breakwater materials (D4.2) and the reduced-scale breakwater prototype (D4.3). It reported three results:

- Circular cellular lightweight concrete (CLC) for non-structural use: density 365 kg/m³ with 25 kg of cement per cubic metre. Recycled lightweight aggregate from glass fully replaces the expanded clay aggregate. ACCIONA positions it as the inner core of floating breakwaters, in place of the expanded polystyrene of conventional pontoon breakwaters, which it describes as costly. Clement Germany set the requirement for the core concrete at a density of about 300 kg/m³; ACCIONA cast 26 trial mixes aiming at 300–400 kg/m³ (D4.1, ACCIONA).
- Circular lightweight aggregate concrete (LWAC) for structural use: density 1,950 kg/m³ against 2,400 kg/m³ for normal-weight concrete, compressive strength 68 MPa at 28 days. Recycled glass aggregate replaces the conventional lightweight aggregate, and a cement that ACCIONA describes as low-carbon, a CEM III cement, replaces standard cement (CEM I). Recycled aggregate from construction and demolition waste also replaces 20 % of the natural coarse aggregate (D7.3). The mix was developed against both mechanical and durability requirements, as a lighter alternative for the structural shell of the breakwater. D4.1 set the LWAC requirement at a strength of at least 45 MPa and a density of at most 1,900 kg/m³. It selected Sample 17 (49 MPa, 1,898 kg/m³) as the optimised mix and reports that Sample 18, the same mix with the CEM III cement, reached 68 MPa at a density near 2,000 kg/m³. The prototype's top slab used Sample 18 (D4.3). This plan gives ACCIONA's figures for that mix.
- Circular high-performance concrete (HPC) for structural use: compressive strength 128 MPa at 28 days, against a requirement of 120 MPa (D4.1). D4.1's mix uses 400 kg/m³ of cement instead of 500, with fly ash and slag (ACCIONA). Its strength allows a thinner breakwater shell.

D4.1 records the development: eight reference and ten circular LWAC trial mixes, with recycled concrete aggregate, steel slag and recycled glass aggregate in place of natural aggregates; a reference and a circular HPC mix; and the 26 cellular-concrete trial mixes.

In the laboratory tests of D4.2 (ACCIONA), no water penetrated the HPC. A crystalline waterproofing admixture, part of all the optimal shell mixes, reduced the mean water penetration of the LWAC with CEM III cement from 8.2 mm to 0.6 mm. The chloride-migration coefficients of the shell mixes were 0.4–0.9 × 10⁻¹² m²/s, which D4.2 classes as extremely high resistance to chloride penetration (D4.2, Table 5).

SINTEF tested shell–core beams cast by ACCIONA from the HPC and LWAC (Section 6.4), among them beams that ACCIONA had first subjected to a month of thermal cycles between −15 °C and +60 °C in the climate chamber of its R&D facilities. In static three-point bending, HPC was more than 55 % stronger than LWAC and about 50 % better in strength-to-weight ratio. Thermal cycling had a negligible effect on the beams, and both mixes dissipated impact energy through cracking in the drop-tower tests. The low pull-out values came from the cellular core, which acts as a filler and carries no structural load, not from the interfaces. D4.2 concludes that HPC is the most suitable material for the shell (D4.2, ACCIONA, with the mechanical tests by SINTEF).

At breakwater level, the environmental assessment finds no relevant greenhouse-gas saving from the circular mixes against a conventional breakwater with a concrete shell and an expanded polystyrene core. In the shell, the cement content needed for the strength is similar, and the steel reinforcement makes up about half of the shell's footprint. In the core, the emissions of the recycled-glass aggregate, lime and aluminium powder balance those of the polystyrene. D7.3 notes that the mixes contain substantial amounts of by-products from other industrial processes, and that their circularity was analysed in the technological assessment (D7.3, IFEU).

In the economic assessment, both breakwater variants in ACCIONA's mixes cost more than the conventional breakwater; D7.4's conclusion is in Section 4.6.1.

ACCIONA designed and cast the reduced-scale breakwater prototype, with SINTEF and Clement Germany as contributing beneficiaries and with mixes developed to production requirements set by Clement Germany (D4.3). ACCIONA deployed it at its R&D maritime demonstration site in the Port of Gijón and exposed it for about nine months, on a four-line catenary mooring with gravity anchors. The exposure is Task 6.5 of the technical validation work package, led by ACCIONA with Ceit, SINTEF and Clement Germany as partners. Its results are reported in a joint preprint by ACCIONA, SINTEF and Ceit (Preprints 202611.1592, November 2026), from which the figures below are taken. The exposure is the subject of ACCIONA's public deliverable D6.4, Marine long-term degradation of concrete breakwater.

The last extension of the project, by three months to 31 December 2026, gives time to complete this exposure and its reporting. The prototype measures 3.40 × 1.70 × 1.05 m. It has HPC in the bottom slab and walls, LWAC in the top slab, and a core of cellular concrete with fully encapsulated expanded polystyrene, which the buoyancy of the reduced scale required.

Ceit monitored the prototype. Biofouling covered more than 85 % of every evaluated surface, with mussel layers up to 35 mm thick. Chloride exceeded the 0.6 % code limit at reinforcement depth only in the LWAC top slab, near the openings made for monitoring equipment; possible local pitting was seen there, but no measurable loss of reinforcement thickness. The authors judge the short-term marine performance satisfactory and call for longer exposure to assess durability over the life cycle.

ACCIONA's mid-term plan in D8.5 also named certification of the mixes against international sustainability standards. The final summary does not name certification, and this plan includes no certification action.

SINTEF reported six results; the aerodynamic and the hydrodynamic models are described together:

- Aerodynamic and hydrodynamic modelling. An aerodynamic method assesses wind loads on floating PV and the shielding effect of floating breakwaters. From CFD simulations of panel configurations, SINTEF derived correlations for the lift and drag forces as a function of the number of panels, for the design of mooring lines and connections. In the same simulations, a breakwater substantially reduced the lift and drag coefficients of the array for angles of attack up to 10°, with less effect at larger angles for a 2 m breakwater (Energies 17(19) 4873, 2024). High-fidelity hydrodynamic methods assess wave loading, structural response and breakwater performance under representative offshore conditions. SINTEF's smoothed-particle hydrodynamics model of a moored, keeled breakwater, built on the open-source solver DualSPHysics, was assessed against wave-basin experiments (Preprints 202610.0011).
- Finite element analysis of concrete cracking and damage: crack and damage initiation and development, and the structural response of concrete components, used to interpret the experimental results.
- The Surewave-ANN concrete-modelling platform, which predicts concrete properties from mix composition and test data for faster screening of circular and lightweight concrete mixes. It complements laboratory testing and empirical methods.
- Concrete testing with digital image correlation (DIC), developed further to measure deformation, local strain fields and crack initiation and propagation in concrete.
- Mechanical and fatigue testing of floating PV connections. The test arrangements and procedures cover the ultimate strength and fatigue of hinges, ropes and related components: specimen design, loading arrangements, instrumentation, DIC, failure assessment, data processing and interpretation. The steel-wire-rope assemblies between breakwater units, the connection type Clement Germany uses (D6.1), reached a mean of 402 kN against a rated strength of 450 kN, which D6.1 finds in agreement; four of five failures were at the terminations. The connections between breakwater and array, designed by Clement Germany to the requirement of D3.3, reached a mean of 40.88 kN against a design requirement of 42 kN. D6.1 finds this aligned with the requirement; the later preprint gives the mean as about 2.7 % below it (Preprints 202611.1612). The first two specimens failed at the interface between rope and bracket at about 38 kN. The two sources differ on which tests had that interface protected. D6.1 places a protective fabric band at the interface from specimen 4, the third valid test, which covers the last three results (41.1, 43.7 and 42.9 kN); the preprint names the last two (43.7 and 42.9 kN) as the protected ones. SINTEF's tests of the first-generation hinge and of the redesigned hinge connection (D6.1, D6.3) are given in Section 4.1; they supported the development of the third generation of Sunlit Sea's hinge.

### 6.5 MARIN (KER5)

Value proposition: hydrodynamic software, mooring optimisation and basin testing that support floating PV developers in scaling up their designs, from concept to operation.

Market: the proposal names offshore energy, including installation and maintenance vessels, and floating PV, floating wind and floating islands; MARIN names floating PV developers at every design stage.

MARIN reported three results.

Result 1, scaled-up DIFFRAC. In SUREWAVE, MARIN extended its wave diffraction solver DIFFRAC so that the number of floating bodies it can model rose from about 100 to nearly 4,000. The solver is used in MARIN's research and commercial studies and made available to licence subscribers. MARIN applied it to a 2 MWp farm of 3,660 modules inside a ring of 32 breakwaters. In steep irregular seas the ring reduced the spatially averaged axial connector loads by up to 20 % and the vertical loads and overturning moments on the module connectors by up to 50 %, with local increases from horizontal module motions and from standing waves inside the ring. The paper gives recommendations for the farm layout to reduce fatigue and survival loads on modules and connectors (Energies 19(7) 1609, 2026).

MARIN validated DIFFRAC twice. In the same paper, it compared the model with the motions of the modules and the breakwater measured in the second SUREWAVE basin campaign (Energies 19(7) 1609). In D5.1, it validated DIFFRAC against Sunlit Sea's earlier tests of 1×3, 1×6 and 1×9 module arrays at the Stadt Towing Tank in Norway. These tests were carried out outside SUREWAVE (Section 10), and Sunlit Sea made the results available to the project. MARIN computed the 1×3 and 1×6 arrays: the heave and pitch motions were generally similar to the measured ones, with some differences (heave over-predicted in short waves, pitch in long waves), and D5.1 concludes that DIFFRAC reproduces the general motion behaviour, including the shielding by the forward floats, while the computed hinge loads and the motions in large fields carry high uncertainty. Hinge loads were not measured, so they could not be validated (Section 4.1).

In the D5.1 simulations the breakwater was most effective in sea states with peak periods below 5 s, and D5.1 describes it as highly effective in reducing the vertical hinge loads. It reduced the horizontal hinge loads by about a factor of 3 at peak periods around 4 s, but by less than 50 % for the largest farm in the highest sea states. MARIN also used its CFD code ReFRESCO for the modules in high waves and for the viscous damping of the breakwater. 3D CFD in severe sea states proved too demanding computationally and lacked robustness in steep and breaking waves, and the analyses used efficient linear methods for all sea states, adjusted with safety factors (D5.1). The modelling results were presented at conferences and in publications.

Result 2, mooring optimisation with surrogate models and genetic algorithms. In Task 5.2, MARIN used genetic-algorithm optimisation for the first time, by coupling the optimisation and surrogate-modelling software Dakota to its time-domain software, to optimise the mooring of the SUREWAVE breakwater ring. D5.1 covers two ring sizes, the 10 MWp ring of 56 breakwaters and a 2 MWp ring of 32. At the Mediterranean and North Sea sites the reference moorings were conservative. Without exceeding proof loads, the optimised moorings have 34 % (Mediterranean) and 42 % (North Sea) less mooring-line mass for the 10 MWp ring, and 56 % and 59 % less for the 2 MWp ring (D5.1, Table 9; the D5.1 summary gives 34–56 % and nearly 60 % for the 2 MWp North Sea ring). At the Baltic site the reference design exceeded proof loads, and the optimised mooring that keeps within them is about 3 times (10 MWp) and 2 times (2 MWp) heavier. D5.1 takes mooring-line mass as the measure of mooring cost. The lighter moorings carry higher maximum tensions at the fairlead, up to about 2.5 times the reference value (D5.1, Table 9). D5.1 presents its optima as possible solutions on the Pareto front of mooring mass and fairlead tension: after consultation with Clement Germany, the weighting between the two was left open. MARIN's summary expresses the reduction as a ratio of mooring-chain mass; the largest reductions in D5.1, 56 % and 59 % for the 2 MWp ring, correspond to a ratio of about 2.3–2.4.

Result 3, basin testing of floating PV with elastically scaled hinges. MARIN tested the floating PV system and the floating breakwater in Task 6.4 at several design stages, in two campaigns with a 1:10 model of a module array behind a breakwater section (Section 4.1). The connectors of the first campaign lacked compressive stiffness and buckled frequently; the second campaign used the paired second-generation connectors, and the hinge record is in Section 4.1. The elastic scaling of the polyurethane hinges worked, and the insights into hinge performance were used by Sunlit Sea to re-evaluate and improve its design. MARIN reports the basin tests in D6.2, which is public on Zenodo, with the test data in a Zenodo dataset (doi 10.5281/zenodo.10960431, April 2024). The method and the results of the first campaign were published in Energies 17(9) 2059 (2024), led by MARIN with co-authors from SINTEF, Clement Germany, Ceit and Sunlit Sea.

Route: DIFFRAC licences and studies; mooring assessment and improvement, a MARIN service for existing and new markets, where the SUREWAVE results show that optimisation adds value; and basin testing for floating PV developers at every design stage, which helps build best practice for this market.

### 6.6 Ceit (KER6)

Value proposition: monitoring and structural health management tailored to the client's structures and components and, under investigation, earlier detection of corrosion damage in reinforced concrete.

Market: the proposal names floating PV plant operators, project developers and component manufacturers in the EU; Ceit names industrial end users and technology integrators in aeronautics and in civil and marine reinforced-concrete monitoring, and industrial partners for co-developed SHMS tools.

Ceit led WP5, the predictive modelling and simulation tools for material properties and structural integrity management, with its deliverables D5.3 to D5.7 (the structural integrity assessment, the monitoring strategy, the fatigue crack propagation algorithms, the integrated structural health management system and the validation report of the modelling tools), and the sensing and monitoring task of the technical validation work package (Task 6.1).

Result 1, eddy-current monitoring technology. Ceit plans to transfer the eddy-current technology to two industrial applications. In aeronautics, it will be validated for detecting air gaps in metal–fibre and fibre–fibre assemblies, for the inspection of bonded and multilayer components. In civil and marine reinforced concrete, Ceit will investigate combining eddy-current data with electrochemical sensor measurements to monitor corrosion, with fuller information on rebar condition and earlier detection of corrosion damage. The routes are industrial validation, adaptation to application-specific requirements and collaboration with end users and technology integrators.

In SUREWAVE, Ceit developed the eddy-current corrosion sensor, designed to assess a rebar from a distance of up to 5–6 cm, with the sensor outside the concrete. In the laboratory it quantified material loss around a rebar to about 0.17 mm (D5.4, Ceit, which calls this the resolution; Sensors 24(13) 4211, 2024, which calls it the accuracy). Ceit's wireless accelerometer nodes, based on ultra-wideband (UWB) radio, were checked against a wired reference system in the laboratory and assessed in MARIN's SUREWAVE wave-basin tests (Electronics 13(22) 4485, 2024, with MARIN).

Ceit provided the corrosion and acceleration monitoring on the breakwater prototype in the Port of Gijón, whose data it analysed over the exposure (Section 6.2). Environmental compensation reduced the variability of the eddy-current baseline to a band of ±135 Ω; field detection of active corrosion was not demonstrated, and a long-term corrosion trend has not yet been established. The accelerometers identified dominant structural-response frequencies of about 0.35–0.45 Hz, broadly consistent with the calculated rigid-body natural frequencies (Preprints 202611.1592).

Result 2, structural health monitoring technologies and the structural health management system (SHMS). Ceit exploits it on two routes. On Route 1, contract R&D and consultancy, Ceit applies methods used and extended in SUREWAVE, which build on its existing practice: finite element modelling of large structures, interpretation of complex loading schemes and advanced crack-propagation analysis. On Route 2, customised SHMS tools, Ceit uses the logic and architecture of the SUREWAVE SHMS demonstrator as the framework for co-developing tools with industrial partners. These are adapted to the client's components, monitoring systems, data and structural health criteria, with additional R&D by Ceit as needed. Both routes run through research contracts, joint technology adaptation and implementation with industrial end users.

The SUREWAVE SHMS demonstrator (D5.6, Ceit) combines Ceit's finite element models of the breakwater with fatigue-crack propagation and the corrosion-sensor data, estimates whether the structure reaches its design life and suggests routine and exceptional inspection times. Its inputs are Clement Germany's breakwater geometry, MARIN's load histories and Ceit's concrete model, calibrated on SINTEF's bending tests of ACCIONA's mixes. D5.7 (Ceit) benchmarked the finite element models against the guidance of the classification societies ABS and DNV, compared the inspection planning of the SHMS with the ABS guidance, calibrated the concrete model on the WP4 and WP6 test data, and used the second basin campaign to confirm that the effect of slamming loads on the breakwater is negligible. Validation with long-term field data from the prototype is further work (D5.7).

## 7. Intellectual property

### 7.1 Ownership and background

Results belong to the beneficiary that generated them, as set in the Grant Agreement and the Consortium Agreement. Joint ownership arises only where two or more beneficiaries make inseparable intellectual contributions to the same result; technical requirements, testing, modelling, validation, measurements and specialist feedback by other partners do not create it by themselves (D1.8, the IPR management plan). Each KER is owned as listed in Section 3; KER5 combines separate MARIN and SINTEF results, each owned by its developer. Background brought into the project remains with its owner, and the Grant Agreement requires the beneficiaries to identify it in a written agreement. The rules for background are set in the Consortium Agreement and the IPR management plan D1.4, and D1.8 records that background remained the property of the partner that owned or controlled it; among the background it names are the original floating PV technology and pre-existing hinge designs that Sunlit Sea brought in (D1.8).

For KER1, D1.8 records three positions. Sunlit Sea is the principal generating partner of the floating PV components and the modified hinge designs, with pre-existing background and new project results distinguished by design version. The floating breakwater design is Clement Germany's foreground, subject to confirmation of any documented inseparable contributions. The integrated offshore floating PV system configuration integrates separately owned components, methods, data and results, with no automatic transfer of component ownership. D1.8 adds that this ownership information should be confirmed by the result owners before any patent filing, registered-design application, licence, assignment, joint-development agreement or commercial deployment.

Applied to KER1, the WP8 lead proposes this split, to be confirmed with the documented contributors before the design-registration decision (Section 7.4). Sunlit Sea's SUREWAVE results are the design of the floating PV array and of the global system (D3.1, D3.4); the hinge redesign (Section 4.1); the nearshore wave and wind models, the wave-corrected performance ratio and the wave-loss module (Section 6.1); and the economic assessment and its cost model (D7.4). Its background is the Generation 1 float and hinge, used in the SUREWAVE reference design, and its earlier patent. CONNECT, the Generation 2 platform with cast polyurethane floats, developed in the parallel project SuRE on the results of the Generation 1 aluminium-float SUREWAVE array, is the commercial carrier of these results (Section 4.1). The breakwater, its mooring and anchoring and the transition element remain Clement Germany's (KER3); integrating them in KER1 transfers no ownership.

D1.8 (September 2026) records that no major ownership or access-right dispute requiring formal escalation was reported to project management.

## 15. Conclusions

SUREWAVE leaves seven Key Exploitable Results with named owners and routes. They rest on the project's technical record: the breakwater, mooring and anchoring design and the CFD study of Clement Germany; the circular concrete mixes of ACCIONA; the floating PV and global system design and the hinge redesign of Sunlit Sea; the component and connection tests at SINTEF; two 1:10-scale wave-basin campaigns at MARIN, of a 3 × 5 module array behind a breakwater section, and MARIN's DIFFRAC and mooring-optimisation work; the structural health management demonstrator of Ceit; the economic assessment of Sunlit Sea and the integrated sustainability assessment of IFEU; and the nine-month sea exposure of ACCIONA's reduced-scale concrete breakwater prototype in the Port of Gijón, without the PV array, monitored by Ceit (Section 6.2). The integrated system and the CONNECT platform are at TRL 4–5 (Section 4.1). The dissemination and communication results are in Section 2.5 and Annex A.

KER1, the integrated floating PV system with breakwater, is commercialised by Sunlit Sea, which led the global framework and specifications (WP2), the floating PV and global system design, the economic assessment D7.4 and WP8. KER1 integrates the breakwater, mooring and anchoring designed by Clement Germany (KER3). It starts with the floating platform at sheltered coastal sites, where the first Norwegian reference plants can be built at low capital cost, and moves to exposed sites with a directional breakwater and to the open sea as the field record and the cost base allow.

Protection on the exposed side starts with small wavebreakers at the first Norwegian reference plant in 2027. The breakwater classes follow at inland lakes and inner waters exposed to wind waves, where a light breakwater can share its cost with other uses. The EIC Transition proposal targets the first commercial site with a breakwater class after the project, about 2030, and the financial plan models it in 2031. In the economic assessment (D7.4), the Mediterranean case of the 10 MWp D7.4 reference plant has the lowest cost of energy, €0.076/kWh, with an internal rate of return of 15.6 % (Section 4.6). The Mediterranean is the planned first open-sea market; the financing of a first plant there is still open.

Sunlit Sea's financial plan reaches EBITDA break-even in 2030 and a positive net result in 2031, with €5.5M of equity raised in two rounds, in 2027 and 2029. It is most sensitive to the timing of the Norwegian reference plants, and the first plants need investment support of about a quarter of their cost (Section 4.7).

The partners' results reach KER1 as engineering, supply, testing, modelling, monitoring and sustainability services, paid per contract under bilateral agreements still to be concluded, or under licence where a partner's design or tool is built or embedded by others (Section 5). Each partner also exploits its results outside floating PV: in concrete construction, marine infrastructure, aeronautics inspection, research and testing services, software licences, monitoring and sustainability studies. SuRE, MariSol, CIRCWIND, SFI BLUES and FlexiShape have used or are using SUREWAVE results; a national project with GEOPON and Ceit's industrial transfers are planned, and an EIC Transition proposal is under evaluation (Section 10). The WP8 lead proposes an annual exploitation review through 2030. Each beneficiary remains bound by the post-project obligations of the Grant Agreement (Section 13).
