## Executive summary

SUREWAVE has taken offshore floating photovoltaics from technology readiness level (TRL) 2–3 to TRL 5 for the integrated concept (D7.6), validated in wave-basin tests and in nine months of breakwater exposure at sea. The second-generation platform is at TRL 4–5, and the directional-breakwater configuration for exposed sites starts at TRL 4. It combined a floating breakwater in circular concrete with a redesigned floating PV array, a validated modelling toolchain and a structural health management system. This deliverable is the consortium's plan for exploiting these results after the project ends on 31 December 2026.

The consortium has identified seven Key Exploitable Results (KERs). KER1, the integrated floating PV system with breakwater, is the global solution of the Grant Agreement and is marketed by Sunlit Sea AS. The other six are exploited by the partners that developed them: circular concrete (ACCIONA), floating breakwater design (Clement Germany), modelling and testing methods (SINTEF), hydrodynamic models and basin testing (MARIN and SINTEF), structural health monitoring and eddy-current sensing (Ceit), and the integrated sustainability assessment method (IFEU).

This plan gives each KER a level of detail matched to its weighting (Section 2.4):

- KER1 has a full business plan, prepared with the Horizon Results Booster. Sunlit Sea first sells the floating platform at the core of KER1, Sunlit Sea CONNECT, for sheltered and nearshore coastal sites. There, reference plants can be built at low capital cost and with little or no breakwater. Where a site needs protection, a breakwater is placed only on the exposed side, in one of three classes scaled from the SUREWAVE breakwater. The first reference plant, in a Norwegian port in 2027, will use small wavebreakers on its exposed side and give the first field record of the platform behind a breakwater. The breakwater classes follow at inland lakes and inner waters exposed to wind waves, through a field demonstrator in 2028–2030 and commercial sites from about 2030, where part of the breakwater's cost can be carried by other uses such as berths. The full open-sea plant with a ring breakwater is the long-term configuration.
- The results that feed into KER1 (breakwater design, concrete, testing, modelling, monitoring and sustainability assessment) are described by their supply or service role, the type of agreement and the logic for a fair price.
- Every partner has a short exploitation pathway and a four-year action plan for 2027–2030, based on the exploitation summaries the partners submitted in September and October 2026.

The SUREWAVE economic assessment (D7.4, version 2.0) gives the reference 10 MW plant in the Mediterranean a levelised cost of electricity (LCOE) of €0.076/kWh, an internal rate of return of 15.6 % and a net present value of €7.3 million, with a payback of ten years. In the Baltic Sea the plant is at break-even, and in the North Sea it is not viable at current costs. KER1's financial plan shows EBITDA break-even for Sunlit Sea in 2030 and net break-even in 2031. It needs about €5–6 million of equity in two rounds, and plant investments are financed at plant level. The route and targets differ from the proposal's offshore pilot and gigawatt ambition. Section 4.10 sets out the changes and the reasons.

Other projects use SUREWAVE results: SuRE (Sunlit Sea, Generation 2 floats, running in parallel since 2024), MariSol (Sunlit Sea with IFE, a national project that started before SUREWAVE), and CIRCWIND, SFI BLUES and Mikroflexishape (SINTEF). A national project with GEOPON (SINTEF) and Ceit's transfer of its eddy-current technology to aeronautics and reinforced-concrete monitoring are planned. An EIC Transition proposal for the exposed-site version of KER1 is under evaluation.

## 1. Purpose and scope

The Grant Agreement describes D8.4 as a plan including: (1) Key Exploitable Result (KER) analysis and IPR analysis; (2) value innovation analysis and market analysis; (3) value proposition and value proposition testing; (4) development of business models, business plans and exploitation action plans. It is the output of Task 8.4, *Exploitation pathways and business plan*. Task 8.4 is to lay the basis for the exploitation agreements between technology developers and exploiters, and to set the ground for decisions on the business models for technology transfer and the rationale for establishing a fair price. Objective O8 of the proposal asks for a definitive business model and business plan for the global solution.

The proposal names the global solution P1, *Offshore FPV plant* (floating photovoltaic plant), as the product to be marketed by Sunlit Sea, with the other partners compensated for the results that contribute to it. The other expected results (R2–R11) are exploited by the partners that developed them; Section 3 maps them to the KERs.

This plan has three levels:

1. A map of all seven KERs: owner, readiness, exploitation route and relation to the global solution (Section 3).
2. A full business plan for KER1, including the financial plan (Section 4).
3. For the results that feed into KER1: the supply or service role, the type of agreement and the fair-price logic (Section 5). For every partner: an exploitation pathway and a four-year action plan (Section 6).

Sections 7–13 cover intellectual property, standardisation, agreements, follow-on vehicles, the use of Horizon Europe support, risks and the post-project obligations under Articles 16 and 17 of the Grant Agreement.

## 3. Key Exploitable Results

### 3.1 How the KERs were identified

The KERs were mapped in four steps.

1. Starting point. The proposal named one global solution, P1 *Offshore FPV plant*, to be marketed by Sunlit Sea, and the expected results R1–R11. R1 is the global solution itself; R2–R10 each had an owner, a type, a business strategy and a target market; R11, the integrated sustainability assessment framework, is named in the Grant Agreement. D8.5 (month 18) reported the mid-term status of R2–R10 and added findings from the first test campaigns: snapping loads on the hinges, the benefit of lighter floats, and the cost of full breakwater protection, which led to the idea of protecting only the exposed sides.
2. Partner input. The beneficiaries reported through a common input template. For each result it asked for the type, a proposed KER, a short description, the audience, the steps taken towards exploitation, market maturity, current and target TRL, the exploitation route, the time to market, and the expected revenue or deployment four years after the project. The same template collected the Results Ownership List, publications, datasets, IPR and standardisation activities for the final report. All seven beneficiaries replied between 4 September and 5 October 2026, with 27 exploitable results in total.
3. Screening. Each reported result was checked against the technical deliverables and the technical brochure (D8.3), and classified: two commercial tools in use (Sunlit Sea's nearshore modelling in its planning platform, MARIN's DIFFRAC solver), three validated material formulations (ACCIONA's HPC, LWAC and CLC), one hardware platform on a defined path to market (Sunlit Sea's second-generation float), and methods and service lines that the partners fold into their existing portfolios. Gains in network and visibility (one from Clement Germany, two from Sunlit Sea and part of IFEU's result) and Clement Germany's contribution to engineering practice and technical committees are reported as impact, not as KERs.
4. Grouping. The results were grouped into seven KERs, one anchored in each beneficiary's main contribution and built around the owner's main exploitation route. A result that serves several KERs is placed under the one it mainly contributes to. MARIN's and SINTEF's hydrodynamic results form one KER (KER5), with ownership kept separate (Section 7.1). The links between results were recorded: SINTEF's connection tests informed Sunlit Sea's third-generation hinge, MARIN's basin tests with elastically scaled hinges fed Sunlit Sea's design review, Clement Germany's breakwater uses ACCIONA's concrete, and Ceit's structural health management system uses the stress fields and loads from SINTEF's and MARIN's modelling.

## 4. KER1 definitive business plan

### 4.1 The result and the product

KER1 is the SUREWAVE global solution: floating PV for coastal and offshore waters, protected by a floating breakwater where the waves require it. It consists of:

- an internal FPV array of floats joined by flexible connections;
- an external protective breakwater;
- the transition element, a floating PVC pipe between breakwater and array attached with elasticated ropes;
- the electrical and mechanical connections;
- anchoring and mooring.

The open-sea reference plant studied in the project is a 10 MWp array inside a ring breakwater, in a circular layout of 346 m diameter; a 2 MWp version is 173 m across. D7.4 assesses a single 2 MW array, five 2 MW arrays and a 10 MW plant.

Sunlit Sea's commercial product from KER1 is the second-generation floating platform, Sunlit Sea CONNECT. Each float is a cast polyurethane frame on a flat bottom plate, carrying one standard glass-glass solar panel at a small fixed tilt just above the water. The floats are joined along their full edge by flexible polyurethane hinges, so the array follows the waves. The redesign after SUREWAVE testing at SINTEF and MARIN, with fatigue and crack-propagation analysis by Ceit, raised the ultimate limit state break load of the critical connection from 14 kN to 37 kN with the third hinge generation (D7.6).

Sunlit Sea's site-planning tool, built on the SUREWAVE nearshore wave and wind models, determines for each site where a breakwater is needed. It also estimates the energy lost to wave motion. Where waves exceed what the platform alone can take, a breakwater is placed only on the sides the strong waves come from.

The commercial configurations, in order of market entry:

| Configuration | Sites | Status | Market entry |
|---|---|---|---|
| Platform without breakwater | Sheltered coastal water: fjords, industrial basins, harbours | Prototype 4 in casting; TRL 4–5 | First reference plants 2027–2028 |

#### 4.1.1 Breakwaters for nearshore sites

SUREWAVE designed one breakwater, the heavy unit for the open sea, in a full ring around the plant. Most sites in Sunlit Sea's markets are nearshore or inland. There the waves are limited by the fetch, the stretch of open water the wind blows over, so they are lower and shorter than at sea and come from some directions only. Sunlit Sea has sized three breakwater classes for such sites, scaled from the SUREWAVE design with its design and cost data (D3.2, D5.1, D7.4). The classes are a sizing study, not a design: they are to be confirmed, engineered and supplied by Clement Germany, the owner of the breakwater design (KER3), on the terms in Section 5.

| Breakwater class | Site wave conditions (design significant wave height in a 22 m/s design wind) | Cross-section | Status | Platform behind it |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Light | Mild, up to about 0.65 m: lakes and inner waters with a fetch of up to about 2 km | About 2.5 m wide, 1 m draft | Desk sizing | CONNECT, the platform for sheltered sites |
| Medium | Medium, up to about 1.0 m | About 4 m wide, 1.5 m draft | Desk sizing | CONNECT |
| Heavy | Rough nearshore, up to about 1.3 m | The SUREWAVE breakwater unit: 5 m wide, about 2 m draft, 20 m long | Tested in CFD and wave basin in SUREWAVE | CONNECT qualified for exposed sites, under development (Section 10) |

The platform needs no breakwater where the short waves, with periods of 3 s or less, stay below about 0.45 m and the design wave height below about 0.6 m (working limits, to be confirmed in the demonstrator). At mild sites the short waves usually exceed that limit first, so a light breakwater is needed even where the design wave height is close to 0.6 m. Above about 1.3 m, or where open-sea swell reaches the site, the full SUREWAVE open-sea design applies: the ring layout, with mooring and anchoring sized for open-sea storms.

Each site is classified with Sunlit Sea's site-planning tool, which is built on the SUREWAVE nearshore wave and wind models. The tool computes the design waves for each direction from fetch and wind. This gives the site a wave class and an exposure sector, from about 20° (waves from one side) to half a circle (180°). The breakwater is placed only across the exposed sector, as an arc or a straight line, extended at the ends so that waves bending round them do not reach the array. Its length, and so its cost, follows from the sector, the plant size and the distance kept from the array.

Three findings from this work shape the product:

- The breakwater cost per kilowatt-peak roughly halves from a 3 MWp to a 10 MWp plant and rises steeply below 1 MWp. Small plants need natural shelter.
- A breakwater can serve other uses at the same time: berths, bathing or sauna platforms, boathouses or a café. Where the site owner has such a use, part of the breakwater's cost is carried by it. This fits leisure lakes, marinas and harbours.
- All three classes have natural roll and heave periods close to the periods of their design waves. They may need more mass or damping than the first sizing gives. This is to be checked with Clement Germany and in basin tests.

Sunlit Sea AS is a Norwegian floating solar company founded in 2019 and based in Oslo. It has built two Generation 1 plants: one in commercial operation in Germany since 2023, and one in Norway that operated under a power purchase agreement from 2024 to 2026. ### 4.3 Market

Floating solar reached 8.7 GW worldwide at the end of 2024, almost 90 % of it in Asia and nearly all of it on calm inland water (IEA PVPS). Floating solar at sea is at pilot stage in Europe: the largest European marine units are 0.5–1 MW, and as of 2026 no supplier has a commercial multi-megawatt reference on European coastal water. National studies indicate 4.5–6.5 GW of potential for floating solar at sea in Spain, and Dutch scenarios include 45 GWp of solar at sea by 2050.

The first commercial niche is plants of 0.3–10 MWp in sheltered coastal water next to industry and ports. Such sites have local power demand, a water area and no land to spare, and grid connection is often slow. In Norway, the energy regulator expects ground-mounted solar to be held back by grid scarcity and land-use conflict.

Two customer segments:

1. Coastal industry and ports with local power demand: local, fixed-price renewable power without waiting for grid capacity, bought under long-term power purchase agreements.
2. Developers and independent power producers building 0.3–10 MWp plants on coastal water: a wave-tested floating system delivered as a kit, with site engineering that lenders and insurers accept.

An adjacent segment is large lakes and hydropower reservoirs exposed to waves.

Competitors are few and at pilot stage, and several meet in EU projects:

- Fred. Olsen 1848 (rope mesh), Ocean Sun (membrane) and SolarDuck (elevated platforms).
- Oceans of Energy and BlueNewables (offshore platforms).
- Swimsol (tropical lagoons).
- Inland float suppliers such as Ciel & Terre.

Competition is decided on references, on independent review of the design and on the price per project.

Value proposition for segment 1: renewable power produced on the host's own water area, next to its load, at a fixed long-term price, from a system designed for the site's waves. For segment 2: a wave-tested floating system with site engineering that sizes structure and breakwater to the site's wave conditions, at a cost per watt that makes coastal sites investable.

### 4.7 Financial plan

The financial plan for KER1 was prepared by Sunlit Sea with the Horizon Results Booster. It projects Sunlit Sea's income statement, cash flow and balance sheet for 2027–2031 in four scenarios for public funding, with sensitivities on cost, price, volume and timing. This section gives the resulting figures. The underlying projects, customers and prices are commercially sensitive and are not given here.

| | 2027–2029 | 2030–2031 |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue | €3–4M | €11–13M |
| Gross margin | low in 2028, the year of the first delivery contract; higher in 2027 and 2029, when engineering and power sales dominate | about 40 %, as kit sales dominate |
| EBITDA | negative | positive from 2030 |

About 28 MWp is delivered by 2031. About a quarter of that volume is at sites with permits, named sites or leads. The rest, including the first 10 MW-class contract, is target volume. The second funding round is conditional on a letter of intent for that contract.

Breakwaters are outside the plan's revenue and costs: they are bought by the plant owner, or by Sunlit Sea and passed on at cost where it delivers the full system (Section 5). Sunlit Sea's gain from a breakwater site is the kit and engineering for a site that would otherwise be closed to floating PV.

- EBITDA break-even in 2030 holds in all four funding scenarios. The net result is negative to 2030 and positive from 2031. Kit and engineering sales cover all costs from about 9 MWp of kits a year, without EPC deliveries.
- The plan needs about €5–6M of equity in two rounds, in 2027 and 2029, including a buffer for delays and guarantees. Plant investments are financed separately at plant level, with project debt, owner equity and national investment support.
- New EU project funding improves cash by up to about €0.7M over the period. The grants fund development work at close to cost: the extension of KER1 to exposed sites and reference plants outside Norway. In the EIC case, a first commercial plant at the demonstrator site, modelled in 2031 as the first full year after the project, adds about €0.2M to 2031 EBITDA.
- Delivered volume is about 7 MWp in 2030 and about 16 MWp in 2031; the target is 15–20 MWp a year from 2031. For orientation, this is a few per cent of the new floating solar forecast for Germany, France and the Netherlands combined: roughly 0.4–0.5 GW a year in Wood Mackenzie's outlook to 2033, most of it on calm inland water. The nearshore and sheltered-coastal segment has no published size.

Conditions for the plan:

- Kit price: the kit margin target is met at today's production cost, provided the kit price holds close to today's level. Cost reductions from series moulds, a lighter bottom plate, casting near the markets where that lowers cost, and volume purchasing give room to lower the price where buyers need it, or to absorb a breakwater at more exposed sites.
- Investment support for the first plants: at first-of-a-kind cost, the first plants need investment support of about a quarter of their cost to match the host's alternative supply. Support of that order is sought from national schemes for first-of-a-kind energy technology. Without support, plants built from the kit are competitive where power is dearer than at the first Norwegian sites: in industry with higher power prices, at sites limited by grid capacity, and in southern Europe with higher yield. In markets with poor solar irradiation, national support for floating solar is assumed.
- References: the plan is most sensitive to the timing of the reference plants and to kit volume and margin in 2030–2031. A one-year delay lowers the net result over 2027–2031 by about €3.6M and needs about €0.5M more equity; lower kit volume or margin moves 2031 EBITDA by €0.4–0.9M. All depend on the first plants operating as designed. The plan ties hiring and investment to decision gates.

## 5. Results that feed into KER1: roles, agreements and fair price

The proposal foresaw that partners whose results contribute to the global solution, then a 50 MWp open-sea plant, would be compensated through licences or royalties. The first commercial product is the platform for sheltered sites. It is built on Sunlit Sea's own designs and on its own nearshore wave and wind models (from ERA5 and GEBCO data). From the first site that needs one of the breakwater classes, expected from about 2030, the breakwater is part of KER1; the small wavebreakers at the first sheltered port site are bought per project. SINTEF's and MARIN's test results, and Ceit's fatigue and crack-propagation analysis, informed the hinge redesign. The redesign is Sunlit Sea's result; its use of the partners' results is covered by access rights under the Grant Agreement and the Consortium Agreement, which Sunlit Sea requests within one year after the project (Sections 7.1 and 9).

The partner results reach KER1 in three ways:

- The breakwater (KER3, with KER2 materials) is a component of KER1 from the first breakwater site. This covers the light and medium classes for inland and nearshore sites as well as the exposed-site and open-sea configurations. Clement Germany engineers and supplies it, and it is bought per project. If Sunlit Sea or a third party were to build a breakwater from Clement's design, a licence on fair and reasonable conditions, which may include royalties, is agreed under the Consortium Agreement.
- Testing, modelling, monitoring and sustainability results reach KER1 as services, contracted per campaign or project.
- If a partner's model or tool were embedded in Sunlit Sea's planning software, a licence would be agreed in the same way.

Each owner is paid once, when and where its result is used; the kit price carries no partner charge, and each owner keeps the right to exploit its result outside floating PV. The basis differs by type of use: services such as testing, modelling and studies are priced per campaign at the owner's market rates; supplied components such as the breakwater are priced per project on cost and market benchmarks; and a licence to build or embed a partner's design or tool is priced as a share of the value it creates, as a royalty or a fixed fee. Prices and detailed terms are set in the bilateral agreements.

## 15. Conclusions

SUREWAVE leaves seven exploitable results with named owners and routes. KER1, the integrated floating PV system with breakwater, is commercialised by Sunlit Sea. It starts with the floating platform at sheltered coastal sites, where the first reference plants can be built at low capital cost, and moves to exposed sites with a directional breakwater and to the open sea as the record and the cost base allow. Protection on the exposed side starts with small wavebreakers at the first reference plant in 2027. The breakwater classes follow at inland and inner waters exposed to wind waves from about 2030, where a light breakwater can share its cost with other uses. The economic assessment confirms the Mediterranean as the first open-sea market. Sunlit Sea's financial plan reaches EBITDA break-even in 2030 with about €5–6 million of equity in two rounds, provided the kit price holds and the first plants perform as designed.

The partners' results reach KER1 as engineering, supply, testing, modelling, monitoring and sustainability services, paid per contract under the bilateral agreements (Section 5). Each partner also exploits its result outside floating PV, in concrete construction, marine infrastructure, research and testing services, monitoring and sustainability studies. Parallel and follow-on projects use the results. The consortium will review exploitation annually through 2030 and report as the Grant Agreement requires.
