## 4. KER1 definitive business plan

### 4.1 The result and the product

KER1 is the SUREWAVE global solution: floating PV for coastal and offshore waters, protected by a floating breakwater where the waves require it. It integrates components designed by two partners:

- the internal FPV array of floats joined by flexible connections, designed by Sunlit Sea (D3.1 and the global system design D3.4, both led by Sunlit Sea);
- the external protective breakwater, designed by Clement Germany (D3.2);
- the transition element, a floating PVC pipe between breakwater and array attached with elasticated ropes, designed by Clement Germany (D3.3);
- the connections between the breakwater units, and the anchoring and mooring, designed by Clement Germany (D3.3), with the mooring optimised by MARIN (D5.1);
- the electrical system of the array.

KER1 integrates Clement Germany's breakwater, transition element, mooring and anchoring (KER3) with Sunlit Sea's array.

The open-sea reference plant studied in the project is an array inside a ring breakwater of 346 m diameter. The proposal set its size at 10 MWp, the minimum plant size of its cost-of-energy calculations. The global system design D3.4 placed 7.16 MWp in the ring with the floats used at the time and noted that a more efficient use of the sheltered space would raise it. Clement Germany compiled the material quantities of the plant for 10 MWp, about 18,600 panels of 537 Wp, and for a 2 MWp version with a ring 173 m across. The economic assessment D7.4 uses these layouts and assesses a single 2 MWp array, five 2 MWp arrays and a 10 MWp plant. Its 10 MWp plant has 10.4 MWp installed (10,375 kWp), and its investment figures refer to that capacity. D7.4 states plant sizes in MW of installed peak capacity; this plan writes MWp for the plants it assesses.

This plan uses the 10 MWp layout of Clement Germany's material quantities and the economics of D7.4 version 2.0 (November 2026), the newest assessment of the plant. The technical brochure D8.3 gives 7 MWp for the reference plant, from the D3.4 layout, and the economics of D7.4 version 1.0 (Section 4.6). It describes the redesigned hinge as about three times as strong in tension as the first-generation hinge; this is the estimate of SINTEF's test report D6.3 for the full connection length, and the measured values follow below. D8.3 and this plan give the same readiness level, TRL 4–5.

Maturity at project end:

- D7.6 (November 2025) assessed the integrated SUREWAVE concept at TRL 5. It rests on D7.6's technical risk assessment of the system with the redesigned hinge, made after the two 1:10-scale wave-basin campaigns of the integrated system at MARIN and the component tests.
- The EIC Transition proposal (September 2026) sets TRL 4, technology validated in the laboratory, as the starting point for the protected floating PV system it develops further. Its evidence is the project's interim report for April to October 2024: the first MARIN campaign, the reduced-scale breakwater prototype as manufactured, the laboratory tests of the circular concrete, the modelling chain and the connection tests. It records that long-term, grid-connected operation at a real site has not been demonstrated.
- The reduced-scale concrete breakwater prototype was exposed at sea in the Port of Gijón for about nine months, without the PV array. Its results were reported in November 2026 (Preprints 202611.1592), after D7.6, and are not part of D7.6's assessment. The integrated system with the array has not been at sea.
- This plan uses TRL 4–5 for the integrated system.
- Sunlit Sea rates its CONNECT platform at TRL 4–5. The redesigned hinge has been tested at SINTEF (D6.3) and in MARIN's second basin campaign (D8.3); prototype 3 of the Generation 2 float has been cast and prototype 4 is in casting; no Generation 2 plant has operated yet.
- Neither the breakwater classes for nearshore sites (Section 4.1.1) nor a directional layout, with a breakwater only on the exposed side, have been tested.

Sunlit Sea's commercial product from KER1 is the Generation 2 floating platform, Sunlit Sea CONNECT. Each float is a cast polyurethane frame on a flat bottom plate, carrying one standard glass-glass solar panel at a small fixed tilt just above the water. The floats are joined along their full edge by flexible polyurethane hinges, so the array follows the waves.

The hinge design comes from SUREWAVE testing. D7.6 numbers the short hinge at the float corners as the second generation and the full-edge hinge as the third; SINTEF's test reports call the hinge tested before the redesign the first generation. The figures below are measured or calculated on different things, and each is named with what it applies to:

- First-generation hinges. In SINTEF's laboratory tests, complete hinges reached a mean tensile load of 3.69 kN and buckled at low compressive loads (D6.1; Preprints 202611.1612). In MARIN's first basin campaign the hinges buckled in harsh seas (Energies 17(9) 2059, 2024), with snap loading after the buckling (D8.3).
- Redesign. Sunlit Sea redesigned the connection and delivered injection-moulded test components to SINTEF (D6.3). The third-generation hinge spans the full float edge, 1.88 m (D7.6).
- Tests of the redesigned connection (D6.3). SINTEF tested a 360 mm segment, one fifth of the full connection length. Its mean tensile load was 2.04 kN with the polypropylene rope that holds the two halves together, and 8.36 kN with a steel rod in place of the rope. Its mean compressive capacity was 20.23 kN, which D6.3 expects to avoid the early buckling of the first generation. With the rope, the fatigue analysis gives about 2.5 × 10¹¹ load cycles to failure, against 7.88 × 10⁸ cycles for 25 years at one wave a second. With a steel rod, the 25 years are reached if the force in the polyurethane part stays below 6.9 kN. Ageing of the polyurethane is not included. The test components had shrinkage gaps of 1–2 mm and some early cracks, and D6.3 finds that control of the production process is critical for series production.
- Second basin campaign. With the redesigned hinge, MARIN observed no measurable hinge buckling or snap loading (D8.3). The measured motions were used to validate the hydrodynamic model of the 2 MWp farm (Energies 19(7) 1609, 2026).
- Design values (D7.6). The design break load (ultimate limit state, the load at which the part is designed to break) of the full-width third-generation hinge is 37 kN, against 14 kN for the second-generation corner hinge. The maximum hinge loads simulated for extreme conditions are 34 kN in the Baltic Sea and 33 kN in the Mediterranean, below the design value, and about 40 kN in the North Sea, above it; with the earlier hinge the North Sea peak was 54 kN. D7.6 states that the robustness of the redesigned hinges could not be fully validated within the project. Exposed open-sea sites in the North Sea need a further hinge generation (Section 4.8).

Sunlit Sea's site-planning tool determines for each site where a breakwater is needed and estimates the energy lost to wave motion (Section 4.1.1). Where waves exceed what the platform alone can take, a breakwater is placed only on the sides the strong waves come from.

#### 4.1.1 Breakwaters for nearshore sites

SUREWAVE designed one breakwater cross-section: Clement Germany's unit for the open sea (D3.2), in a full ring around the plant (D3.3). Most sites in Sunlit Sea's markets are nearshore or inland. There the waves are limited by the fetch, the stretch of open water the wind blows over, so they are lower and shorter than at sea and come from some directions only. Sunlit Sea has sized three breakwater classes for such sites; the SUREWAVE unit is the heaviest of them. The two lighter classes are scaled from it with its design data (D3.2, D5.1), with the same construction and double-keel shape. The costs use D7.4's breakwater and anchoring costs as modelled, plus Sunlit Sea's own estimates for fabrication, fittings, transport, supplier margin and mobilisation, which D7.4 does not contain (Section 4.6.2). The classes are a sizing study, not a design: they are to be confirmed, engineered and supplied by Clement Germany, the owner of the breakwater design (KER3), on the terms in Section 5.

The sizing method:

- Design waves: fetch-limited wave spectra of the JONSWAP type, the standard spectrum for wind seas, with the growth curves of the US Shore Protection Manual of 1984. The design wind is 22 m/s at 10 m height, a working assumption of Sunlit Sea to be checked against the wind data of each site.
- Wave transmission behind the breakwater: the Macagno formula, which gives the share of the wave height that passes a fixed floating breakwater, as used in D3.2 and D5.1.
- Criteria at the array: short waves, with periods of 3 s or less, at most 0.45 m significant height. D3.2 names this band as critical for the array, and D5.1 finds the array's hinge loads peaking in it. The 0.45 m is a 0.5 m limit with a margin of 10 %. All waves at most 0.6 m for the platform for sheltered sites and 0.8 m for the exposed-site version under development. These limits are Sunlit Sea's working assumptions, to be confirmed in the demonstrator.
- Cross-sections: a width-to-draft ratio of 2–3, as for the SUREWAVE unit, and a minimum width of 2.5 m for stability and access. A search found the lightest section meeting both criteria for each wave class, and the chosen classes are somewhat larger, as a margin: 2.5 × 1.0 m against 2.5 × 0.9 m (light), 4.0 × 1.5 m against 3.0 × 1.2 m (medium), and the SUREWAVE unit against 4.5 × 1.5 m (heavy).

| Breakwater class | Site wave conditions (design significant wave height in a 22 m/s design wind) | Cross-section | Status | Platform behind it |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Light | Mild, up to about 0.65 m, where the short waves exceed 0.45 m or the design wave height exceeds 0.6 m: lakes and inner waters with a fetch of up to about 1.6 km | About 2.5 m wide, 1 m draft; about 30 t per 20 m unit | Desk sizing | CONNECT, the platform for sheltered sites |
| Medium | Medium, up to about 1.0 m: fetch up to about 3.8 km | About 4 m wide, 1.5 m draft; about 73 t per 20 m unit | Desk sizing | CONNECT |
| Heavy | Rough nearshore, up to about 1.3 m: fetch up to about 6.4 km | The SUREWAVE breakwater unit: 5 m wide, about 2 m draft, 20 m long, about 123 t | Designed by Clement Germany with CFD (D3.2) and tested in the SUREWAVE wave-basin campaigns; a reduced-scale prototype built and deployed by ACCIONA (D4.3) and exposed for nine months in the Port of Gijón | CONNECT qualified for exposed sites, under development (Section 10) |

The draft is measured to the bottom of the keels, so a unit weighs less than a solid block of the stated width and draft. A light unit of about 30 t can be cast, trucked and lifted with ordinary harbour equipment.

The platform needs no breakwater where the short waves stay below about 0.45 m and the design wave height below about 0.6 m (working limits, to be confirmed in the demonstrator). At mild sites the short waves usually exceed their limit first, so a light breakwater is needed even where the design wave height is close to 0.6 m. Longer waves pass a floating breakwater largely unreduced, so at medium and rough sites the limit for all waves decides the class, and rough sites need the exposed-site version of the platform even behind the SUREWAVE unit. Above about 1.3 m the waves that pass the breakwater exceed that version's working limit. There, or where open-sea swell reaches the site, the full SUREWAVE open-sea design applies: the same unit in a ring, with mooring and anchoring sized for open-sea storms, for design waves of up to 6.5 m in the Mediterranean (D5.1).

Each site is classified with Sunlit Sea's site-planning tool. The tool is built on the SUREWAVE nearshore wave and wind models, from ERA5 and GEBCO data, and gives the wave and wind conditions along the full perimeter of a planned plant. From these conditions and fetch-limited wave growth, the design waves are set for each direction. This gives the site a wave class and an exposure sector, from about 20° (waves from one side) to half a circle (180°). The breakwater is placed only across the exposed sector, widened by 10° on each side for the spread of wave directions in a wind sea, as an arc or a straight line, whichever is shorter. It is extended at the ends so that waves bending round them do not reach the array. It stands 15 m from the array, the distance D7.4 uses for its 2 MWp ring. Its length, and so its cost, follows from the sector, the plant size and that distance.

Three findings from this work shape the product:

- The breakwater cost per kilowatt-peak roughly halves from a plant of about 3 MWp to one of 10 MWp (light class, one exposed side) and rises steeply below 1 MWp: at a plant of a few hundred kWp it is close to ten times the value at 10 MWp. Small plants need natural shelter.
- A breakwater can serve other uses at the same time: berths, bathing or sauna platforms, boathouses or a café. Where the site owner has such a use, part of the breakwater's cost is carried by it. This fits leisure lakes, marinas and harbours.
- All three classes have natural roll and heave periods close to the peak periods of their design waves: about 2.7 and 2.5 s against 2.3 s (light), 3.4 and 3.2 s against 3.1 s (medium), 3.8 and 3.6 s against 3.7 s (heavy), scaled from the periods D5.1 gives for the SUREWAVE unit. The Macagno formula treats the breakwater as rigid and is least reliable near these periods, where a moving breakwater lets more wave energy through. The classes may need more mass or damping than the first sizing gives. This is to be checked with Clement Germany and in basin tests.

### 4.2 Organisation and objectives

Sunlit Sea AS is a Norwegian floating solar company founded in 2019 and based in Oslo. It has built two Generation 1 plants in its own factory: a 60 kWp plant in commercial operation in Germany since 2023, and its own 105 kWp plant in Norway, which has sold its power under a power purchase agreement since 2024, to October 2026. It moved to subcontracted production in 2024 to concentrate on Generation 2. The core team is small. The CEO invented Generation 1 and leads the Generation 2 development; the technical lead is responsible for product development, sales and administration. Sunlit Sea works with partners for structural simulation (the Institute for Energy Technology, IFE), PV module integration, sales in Germany, and local construction and permitting.

Objectives:

- 2027–2028: freeze the CONNECT design, reach TRL 6 for sheltered sites through the first reference plants in Norway, start a DNV design-basis assessment and prepare series production with casting partners.
- 2029–2032: sell CONNECT as a kit to developers and contractors in the Nordic countries, Germany, the Baltic region and later the Mediterranean; win a first 10 MW-class supply contract, a kit supply of about 10 MWp, by 2030; extend the product to exposed sites with a directional breakwater.

### 4.3 Market

Floating solar reached at least 8.7 GW worldwide at the end of 2024 (IEA PVPS, Trends in Photovoltaic Applications 2025). Of the 7.7 GW installed by the end of 2023, almost 90 % was in Asia; the Netherlands and France were the largest markets outside Asia (IEA PVPS Task 13, Floating PV Plants, 2025). Most European plants are on quarry lakes, sandpit lakes, irrigation ponds and other man-made inland water. IEA PVPS rates floating PV on inland water above TRL 8 and offshore floating PV at TRL 3–5, with only a few pilot and demonstration projects (Task 13, 2025).

Floating solar at sea is at pilot stage in Europe. The largest European marine units reported are 0.5–1 MW: SolarDuck's 0.5 MWp pilot in the Dutch North Sea, installed in July 2024 (RWE, 2024), and BlueNewables' 1 MW demonstrator of two 500 kW platforms at the Port of Valencia, in testing since October 2026 (pv magazine, 2026). Sunlit Sea's review of company announcements and trade press from 2024 to 2026 found no commercial multi-megawatt floating solar plant on European coastal water. The potential is documented in two countries:

- Spain: 4.45–6.48 GW of floating solar at sea within the national maritime spatial plans, in a study by the University of A Coruña (Journal of Cleaner Production, 2026, reported by pv magazine, June 2026).
- The Netherlands: scenarios with 200 GWp of solar power in 2050, of which 45 GWp at sea (TNO, floating solar programme page, accessed 2026).

The first commercial niche is plants of 0.3–10 MWp in sheltered coastal water next to industry and ports. Such sites have local power demand and a water area, but no land to spare. In Norway, the energy regulator NVE expects grid scarcity in large parts of the country and land-use conflict to limit ground-mounted solar before 2030: by February 2024 it had received applications for close to 1.4 GW of ground-mounted plants and licensed three plants with 14 MW, against about 10 GW needed for the political goal of 8 TWh of solar power by 2030 (NVE, note on solar power and other local generation, 5 February 2024). Sunlit Sea has not yet sized the sheltered coastal segment. The count of suitable coastal sites per country, planned with the customer interviews in early 2027 (Section 4.3.1), gives the first estimate.

Two customer segments:

1. Coastal industry and ports with local power demand: local, fixed-price renewable power without waiting for grid capacity, bought under long-term power purchase agreements.
2. Developers and independent power producers building 0.3–10 MWp plants on coastal water: a wave-tested floating system delivered as a kit, with site engineering that lenders and insurers accept.

An adjacent segment for the platform is large lakes and hydropower reservoirs exposed to waves. For the breakwater classes, lakes exposed to wind waves are the first sites (Section 4.6.2).

At sea in Europe, the competitors are few and at pilot stage. Outside Europe and on inland water, suppliers have installed bases, but for calm water or tropical lagoons. Status from public sources:

- Fred. Olsen 1848 (pretensioned rope mesh): pilot stage; DNV completed a technical review against DNV-RP-0584 for significant wave heights up to 3.5 m (DNV, 2026).
- Ocean Sun (membrane on a floating ring): Bureau Veritas type certificate for its floating structure (Ocean Sun, November 2025).
- SolarDuck (elevated platforms): the 0.5 MWp North Sea pilot above (RWE, 2024), with a Bureau Veritas prototype certificate (pv magazine, March 2024).
- Oceans of Energy (offshore platforms): an array of about 1,400 panels at an offshore wind farm in the Dutch North Sea, installed in August 2025 and withdrawn in 2026 after thermal incidents traced to a connector defect (CrossWind, May 2026); partner in the EU project BAMBOO.
- BlueNewables (raised catamaran platforms): the 1 MW Valencia demonstrator above (pv magazine, 2026).
- Swimsol (rigid platforms for tropical lagoons): sea-based plants in the Maldives, the largest 2.4 MW (April 2026).
- Inland float suppliers such as Ciel & Terre: plastic pontoons for calm water; Ciel & Terre had completed about 820 MW in 280 projects by mid-2023 (TaiyangNews, 2023) and is a partner in the EU project SuRE (CORDIS).

The withdrawn North Sea array failed on an electrical connector, not on its structure. Sunlit Sea's competitor review finds electrical and balance-of-system reliability the recurring failure at sea in 2025–2026; the qualification programme of Section 4.5 covers the electrical components as well as the structure.

In Sunlit Sea's assessment, competition at sea is decided on references, on independent review of the design and on the price per project, since no supplier publishes list prices.

Value proposition for segment 1: renewable power produced on the host's own water area, next to its load, at a fixed long-term price, from a system designed for the site's waves. For segment 2: a wave-tested floating system with site engineering that sizes structure and breakwater to the site's wave conditions, at a cost per watt that makes coastal sites investable.

#### 4.3.3 Sustainability evidence

The integrated sustainability assessment D7.6, led by IFEU, compares the open-sea SUREWAVE plant of about 10 MWp, in several European sea scenarios, with electricity from coal. Compared with coal, every scenario is more climate-friendly, uses far fewer fossil resources, has a very low land-use footprint and has lower social supply-chain risks. Its impacts are higher than those of land-based PV or wind power. Local environmental impacts can occur and have to be managed. Every indicator reaches an acceptable outcome in at least one scenario, and the Mediterranean is the most advantageous location. More profitable technical choices tend to be more sustainable too, and large uniform plants are preferred over five units of about 2 MWp for their lower material intensity.

D7.6 sets conditions for a sustainable deployment:

- target markets where the plants replace fossil-based power and avoid competing with wind and land-based PV;
- high efficiency: high solar yield, mild waves and wind, small depths, short distance to shore or grid and to a harbour;
- no substantial disturbance of sensitive shallow-water habitats or marine-mammal migration routes;
- socially beneficial procurement.

D7.6 recommends demonstrating floating PV at sea at an intermediate scale as quickly as the remaining technological de-risking allows. The KER1 plan takes the de-risking steps at sheltered and exposed nearshore sites first, from 2027, and plans an open-sea pilot proposal about 2031 (Section 4.6.1).

This evidence supports the value proposition to lenders and permitting authorities. D7.6 assessed the open-sea plant; for sheltered and nearshore sites the evidence is indicative until it is assessed for those plants.

Revenue streams:

- the delivered plant, and development and engineering fees, in phase 1;
- the floating system as a kit, priced per watt, in phase 2;
- site engineering;
- power sales from the own reference plant;
- spare hinges and support for the installed base.

Production is subcontracted: Sunlit Sea designs the parts, owns the moulds and controls quality. The first plants are sheltered enough to need little or no breakwater; the small wavebreakers at the first reference plant are part of that plant's cost. At sites that need one of the breakwater classes (Section 4.1.1), designed and supplied by Clement Germany, the plant owner buys the breakwater from Clement Germany. Where Sunlit Sea delivers the full system as EPC contractor, it buys the breakwater and includes it in the EPC price. Clement Germany can also lead a delivery, with Sunlit Sea as floating PV subcontractor (Section 5).

Sales channels are added step by step:

- 2027: direct sales, with local contractors as joint developers.
- 2028–2029: a sales lead, contractors and developers as resellers, and EU demonstration projects with SINTEF and partners.
- From 2030: distributors or licensees in markets Sunlit Sea will not serve directly.

Markets follow the same order: Norway from 2027, Germany from 2028, the Baltic region from 2029, and the Mediterranean after 2031, starting with sites that need no breakwater or a light one; open-sea sites follow the exposed-site field record.

#### 4.4.1 Value chain

Sunlit Sea's value chain analysis for the Horizon Results Booster (November 2026) maps who does what in a CONNECT plant. A plant passes through nine steps:

1. site screening and design basis;
2. permits and grid;
3. offtake and financing;
4. product design and qualification;
5. components;
6. kit packing and logistics;
7. assembly, launch and mooring;
8. operation and maintenance;
9. sale or exit.

In phase 1 a plant involves eight to ten parties: the host and offtaker, the plant company, the investment-support agency, a bank, a local contractor, a marine contractor, the casting vendor, the module supplier, DNV and Sunlit Sea. As EPC contractor, Sunlit Sea carries the interfaces between them. In phase 2 the developer carries most interfaces, and Sunlit Sea keeps site screening and engineering, design and qualification, components and kit packing, with support in assembly and operation.

The SUREWAVE partners and Sunlit Sea's own partners enter at three steps: design and qualification (IFE for structural simulation, SINTEF and MARIN for tests, DNV for the design basis), assembly, where the breakwater classes come from Clement Germany from about 2030, and operation, with Ceit's monitoring as an option for larger plants.

The analysis finds these single points of failure, with the interventions planned:

| Point | Effect if it fails | Intervention |
|---|---|---|
| Two key people, both on the critical path of most phase 1 steps | All milestones slip | Procedures for design, casting and the site-planning tool documented and deputies named by Q2 2027; staff funded by the extension of Sunlit Sea's scope in SuRE (CINEA decision pending) at the first reference plant in 2027; hires after the first equity round in the order the chain needs them (Section 4.8) |
| No casting source qualified for series production; prototypes are cast by one supplier in China | Deliveries stop; the multi-megawatt plant and the kit ramp-up are delayed | In-house moulds for pilot volumes; a series source qualified before the first megawatt-scale order, in Europe where it is cost-competitive; moulds owned by Sunlit Sea, so production can move |
| Float production for the first multi-megawatt plant, cast with pilot moulds in the months after the first winter inspection | Commissioning slips into 2029 | Casting capacity confirmed with the vendor before the EPC contract is signed in Q4 2027, and the commissioning date set from it |
| One local contractor on both larger Norwegian projects | Permit or installation delays at both sites at once | Written agreement on roles and the permit in Q1 2027; a second contractor invited and qualified for the later plants |
| Plant financing outside Sunlit Sea's control | No investment decision at the first multi-megawatt site | Investment-support case prepared with the national agency; investment decision as a decision gate of the financial plan, with hiring tied to it (Section 4.7) |
| Wavebreakers and breakwaters: no supplier yet for the small wavebreakers; the breakwater classes depend on Clement Germany | Cost or timing of the first reference plant; exposed sites delayed after 2030 | A wavebreaker quote before the first reference plant is ordered; a bilateral agreement with Clement Germany sought in 2027 |
| Field data from owners and operators | A weaker case for kit sales and the second equity round | Data access written into power purchase agreements, EPC and kit contracts; measured yield published |
| No kit-packing partner contracted | Kit sales from 2028 handled case by case at higher cost | A framework agreement, planned for 2027–2028, with a module-integration and packing partner in Lithuania |

Most of the margin sits in design, the moulds and the kit, which Sunlit Sea keeps in both phases. Delivery earns a thin margin for the most risk, so the EPC role is limited to the three Norwegian reference plants and passes to developers in phase 2.

### 4.5 Development and qualification

CONNECT will be qualified before series production against the DNV floating solar documents (DNV, May 2026): DNV-ST-C108, Structural design of floats for floating photovoltaic systems; DNV-ST-E309, Station keeping of floating solar photovoltaic systems; and the recommended practice DNV-RP-0584, Design, development and operation of floating solar photovoltaic systems. The programme covers:

- fatigue of hinge and mooring under wave cycles;
- durability of the polyurethane in sun and seawater: accelerated UV ageing to IEC TS 62788-7-2 followed by mechanical testing, which also covers the ageing that the fatigue analysis of D6.3 left out;
- control of the casting process, since the test components of D6.3 had shrinkage gaps and early cracks;
- corrosion protection of the bottom plate;
- marine growth;
- marine electrical components and cable movement;
- field monitoring of the first plants.

Milestones:

- written requests to SINTEF and MARIN for access rights to the test results used in the hinge redesign (Q1 2027, before the design freeze);
- design freeze with prototype 4 (Q2 2027);
- first reference plant in operation (Q3 2027);
- freedom-to-operate review and decision on design registration (Q3 2027);
- DNV design-basis assessment (from Q4 2027);
- TRL 6 for sheltered sites after the first reference plant's first winter (Q2 2028);
- industrial moulds and kit packing (2027–2028).

The exposed-site version needs a demonstrator with a directional breakwater. The EIC Transition proposal (Section 10) plans it, to take the protected floating PV system from TRL 4 to TRL 6.

#### 4.5.1 Regulatory framework, uptake barriers and replication

| Market | Permitting | Main uptake barriers |
|---|---|---|
| Norway | Solar plants below 10 MW need no national licence since 1 July 2025 and are approved by the municipality (Ministry of Energy, 2025); a harbour permit is needed for plants at sea | Grid capacity, power prices in northern regions, investment support for first plants |

Across markets, the main barriers are bankability (lenders and insurers want an independently reviewed design and reference plants), permits before debt, and marine contractors and capital for each plant. The DNV floating solar standards of 2026 give the basis for an independently reviewed design.

Replication rests on four elements:

- the kit model, which lets local developers and contractors build plants without Sunlit Sea's balance sheet;
- the site-planning tool, which screens new sites and sizes the protection;
- distributors or licensees from 2030 in markets Sunlit Sea does not serve directly;
- the step from sheltered to exposed sites and then to the Mediterranean open sea, which widens the range of usable sites.

### 4.6 Economics of the reference plant

The SUREWAVE economic assessment D7.4, led by Sunlit Sea (version 2.0, 20 November 2026), covers the full life cycle of the open-sea reference plant: construction, 50 years of operation with PV replacement after 25 years, and decommissioning. The electricity sale price is €0.12/kWh, the target price set in the SUREWAVE application, and the base discount rate is 8.5 %.

| Site, 10 MWp plant (10.4 MWp installed) | NPV | IRR | LCOE | Payback |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Mediterranean | €7.3M | 15.6 % | €0.076/kWh | 10 years |
| Baltic Sea | about €0 | 8.5 % | €0.120/kWh | not reached |
| North Sea | −€5.8M | 3.4 % | €0.173/kWh | not reached |

Version 2.0 answers the request for revision of 16 October 2026. It adds the clean-up of the site at the end of life, and the rebuilt cost model, checked against a separate implementation with automated tests, corrected errors of version 1.0: structure maintenance and the capital cost of inverters, transformers and the SCADA control system were described but not calculated; the PV replacement in year 25 was missing from the LCOE; and a non-standard discount factor was used, operation was counted from year 0, and the IRR was calculated on discounted cash flows over 25 years only. In the Mediterranean, the LCOE rises from €0.062 to €0.076/kWh and the NPV falls from €8.9M to €7.3M; the IRR rises from 10.7 % to 15.6 % because version 2.0 calculates it on undiscounted cash flows over the full 50-year life (section 2.5 of D7.4). The version 1.0 figures appear in the technical brochure D8.3 and in the integrated sustainability assessment D7.6; the ranking of the three sites is the same in both versions.

D7.4 assesses plants of 2 MWp and 10 MWp built from the SUREWAVE reference design. The Mediterranean is the first market for the open-sea configuration. The Baltic Sea breaks even at the 8.5 % base rate, which is its IRR. The North Sea is viable only at discount rates of 3 % or lower, as available for publicly backed demonstration projects, or with lower cost or shared infrastructure. Excluding cables, D7.4 finds non-panel costs at sea close to those of onshore PV, because the modular structures avoid most of the offshore installation premium known from wind. The breakwater and bridge pontoons, with PVC pipes and ropes, are about €212/kWp, under a quarter of the reference plant's investment. Starting at sheltered sites, where little or no breakwater is needed, removes most of that cost from the first plants.

#### 4.6.2 First sites with breakwater protection

The first breakwater protection in operation will be the small wavebreakers at the first reference plant, a Norwegian port, in 2027, on the side exposed to local wind waves. It is the first field test of placing protection only where the waves come from. The first sites for the breakwater classes are expected to be inland lakes and inner waters exposed to wind waves from one direction. Lakes are an adjacent segment for the platform alone (Section 4.3), but they come first for the classes, because their waves are limited by the fetch and the light class is enough. The baseline site of the EIC Transition demonstrator, the step to exposed sites, is such a lake, reached through Clement Germany's relationship with the site owner (Section 10). There a light breakwater covers the exposed side, a sector of about 45°, and the site owner's other uses, such as berths or bathing platforms, can carry part of its cost. The EIC Transition proposal targets a first commercial installation of 1–5 MWp after the project: immediately after completion in its summary, about 2030, and within 12–24 months after completion in its exploitation plan, subject to the site owner, permits and finance. The financial plan models it in 2031, the first full year after the project (Section 4.7). If Sunlit Sea takes part in the EUPI-PV proposal and it is funded, a nearshore demonstration with a breakwater could come earlier, in 2029 (Section 10).

The table shows the breakwater's share of the plant cost, defined as the breakwater cost divided by the cost of PV plant and breakwater together, as the plant grows from the demonstrator (50–300 kWp in the EIC Transition proposal) to commercial size. Basis:

- PV plant: the cost of a plant built from the kit (Section 4.7), taken as the same per watt at every size. A demonstrator costs more per watt, so the shares in the demonstrator rows are upper values.
- Breakwater: a light breakwater over a 45° sector, widened by 10° on each side, at 15 m from the array, as an arc or a straight line, whichever is shorter (straight at 30 MWp), with its length rounded up to whole 20 m units and extra anchor sets at the open ends.
- Breakwater cost: the breakwater and anchoring costs of D7.4 version 2.0 as modelled, plus Sunlit Sea's estimates for fabrication, fittings, transport, supplier margin and mobilisation, for a plant built by a kit buyer.
- "Half carried by other uses" is an assumption.

The figures are indicative and are to be confirmed by Clement Germany.

| Plant | Share, half carried by other uses | Share, no other uses |
|---|---:|---:|
| Demonstrator, 50 kWp | about two thirds | about 80 % |
| Demonstrator, 300 kWp | about 30 % | about 46 % |
| About 1 MWp | about 15 % | about 25 % |
| About 5 MWp | about 6 % | about 11 % |
| About 30 MWp | about 2 % | about 4 % |

The demonstrator's breakwater is funded by the demonstration grant.

From about 5 MWp, the breakwater is about a tenth of the plant cost or less, and a light breakwater scaled from the SUREWAVE design opens sites with wind waves to floating PV. A first plant of about 1 MWp serves as a reference and earns little margin. With the breakwater half carried by other uses, it produces power at about €0.10/kWh (financial plan), above the average award of €0.048/kWh in Germany's solar tender of July 2025 (Bundesnetzagentur, August 2025). Selling to the grid does not cover its cost; it needs a buyer who values the power behind the meter, or investment support. The step from demonstrator to commercial plants also gives the field record for the exposed-site configuration.
