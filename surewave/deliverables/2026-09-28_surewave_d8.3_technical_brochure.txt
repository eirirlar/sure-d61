About this brochure

SUREWAVE is an integrated offshore floating photovoltaic (FPV) system designed to survive the wave, wind and
current conditions of the open sea. It couples a novel external floating breakwater with a redesigned FPV array,
backed by a validated numerical modelling toolchain and a structural health management platform. Between
2022 and 2026 a seven-partner European consortium built and validated the system through laboratory testing,
wave-basin campaigns and a reduced-scale prototype in the water at the Port of Gijón, taking offshore FPV from
technology readiness level 2­3 to TRL 4­5.

The brochure is written for offshore energy developers, floating PV manufacturers, EPC contractors, marine
engineering firms, port and grid operators, insurers and investors who evaluate offshore FPV as a portfolio
option. It summarises what the technology does, how it performs, what it costs, and what remains between
here and commercial deployment.

1. Why offshore floating photovoltaics

Solar photovoltaic capacity is being deployed faster than any other electricity technology. The land constraint
that limits onshore PV in dense coastal economies does not exist over water. Land-based floating PV -- on
reservoirs, lakes and ponds -- has grown to more than 2.5 GW installed globally. Offshore FPV is at pilot and
early-commercial scale, with sub-MW to low-MW arrays now in the water in several European and Asian loca-
tions. Volume-scale commercial roll-out is still ahead; the sea offers scale, but it also brings wave loads, wind
loads, currents, salinity and biofouling that a lake-tuned float cannot handle.

Europe's offshore renewable energy strategy identifies floating PV as one of the technologies needed to diversify
beyond offshore wind. Even a small fraction of Europe's exclusive economic zone under offshore PV would
exceed today's total European solar generation. What has been missing is a design that survives open-sea
conditions economically over a 25- to 40-year operating life.

SUREWAVE targets that gap. The project has advanced offshore FPV from technology readiness level 2­3 (con-
cept formulated) to TRL 4­5 (validated in relevant environment) by combining a novel external floating break-
water with a redesigned FPV array, backed by validated numerical models and a structural health management

2. The SUREWAVE concept

SUREWAVE is an integrated system. Four subsystems work together:
    · An external floating breakwater built from circular concrete formulations attenuates incoming waves be-
       fore they reach the PV array.
    · A flexible transition-element system connects the breakwater to the FPV array while absorbing differential
       motion.
    · The FPV array itself uses marine-grade aluminium modules connected by polyurethane hinges into large
       matrices, arranged for wave shielding by neighbouring floats.
    · A catenary mooring system holds the whole installation on station across mild to harsh European sea
       states.

The reference plant is 7 MWp, laid out on a 346 m diameter circular footprint. Sixty-eight matrices of fourteen
by fourteen FPV modules sit inside a perimeter of external floating breakwater units, with two internal linear
breakwater lines dividing the array into sub-farms for structural and hydrodynamic reasons. A 420 kWp pilot-
scale variant is designed for mild-sea early deployments.
The system is dimensioned against three reference site classes taken from twenty-five years of ERA5 hindcast
data at representative European locations: a mild Baltic site (16 m water depth, significant wave height 0.6 m),
a mid-condition Western Mediterranean site (38 m, 0.5 m) and a harsh Greater North Sea site (49 m, 1.6 m).
Extreme fifty-year design values cover wind above 25 m/s, current above 1.2 m/s and wave height above 14 m.

3. System design highlights

The floating breakwater
The floating breakwater was designed and dimensioned by Clement Germany across ten candidate cross-
sections and mooring layouts, with wave attenuation evaluated using coupled aero-hydrodynamic CFD in

Figure 2: The integrated SUREWAVE system -- isometric cutaway showing the four subsystems and their con-

FLOW-3D HYDRO and validated against classical Macagno, Wiegel, Cox and Fousert-van Tol theoretical models.
The selected unit is 20 m long, 5 m wide, 2.5 m high, with double keels. It weighs 123.4 t at 2.03 m draft. The
breakwater specifically attenuates more than 40 % of the wave energy in the 1­3 second short-period wind-sea
band -- the frequencies that drive FPV module fatigue. Long-period swell is handled by the mooring and hinge
design, which lets each subsystem be sized for its own job rather than compromising a single float geometry
across all frequencies.

Larger draft and width improve efÏciency across all wave conditions. Keels raise performance for longer wave
periods without a proportional weight penalty. The circular layout produces uniform load distribution and lower
peak connector forces than square, octagonal or elliptical layouts, and simplifies anchorage design at scale.

The FPV modules

The FPV modules use marine-grade aluminium shells with sealed cavities, individually potted, arranged in
string modules that pre-assemble into complete matrices for rapid deployment. Modules connect through
polyurethane hinges that flex under wave-induced motion while transmitÝng only limited force between
neighbours. The flat profile minimises wind drag and simplifies stacking on shipping barges. Cooling is passive
by convection and evaporation over the seawater below the array.

Mooring, anchoring, connections

Catenary mooring with stud-link steel chain is dimensioned to DNV-OS-E301 for each of the three site classes.
Nominal chain diameter is 54 mm at the Baltic site and 95 mm at the Mediterranean and North Sea sites; the 16
mm corrosion allowance built on top gives outer diameters of 70 and 111 mm respectively. Mediterranean and
North Sea share the same chain size but the North Sea layout uses more mooring lines and higher anchor count.
The corrosion allowance is sized for a 40-year design life and is refreshed at mid-project chain replacement in the
50-year economic model. Driven anchor piles are sized for medium-dense sand seabed conditions; site-specific
geotechnical adjustments apply elsewhere.

The transition element connecting each floating breakwater to the FPV array uses elasticized rope with safety
factors of 3.2 to 4.4 at critical connection points. It allows differential motion between the heavy breakwater
and the lightweight FPV array without transmitÝng damaging peak loads to either.

4. Materials innovation

SUREWAVE designed and characterised three new circular concrete formulations for the floating breakwater.

High-performance concrete (HPC)
Compressive strength 128 MPa achieved (target 120 MPa). The optimised mix (Sample 2 in the consortium
record) uses reduced cement content substituted by ground granulated blast-furnace slag and fly ash, plus
a Krystaline Add1 waterproofing admixture. Durability testing confirmed marine-exposure code compliance
(XS2/XS3) with no measurable water penetration. Mechanical testing on small-scale prototypes gave HPC 55
% higher strength than the LWAC alternative at a better strength-to-weight ratio. HPC was selected for the
structural shell of the reduced-scale breakwater prototype.

Lightweight aggregate concrete (LWAC)
Compressive strength 68 MPa achieved, density approximately 1950 kg/m³. Optimised mixes (Samples 17 and

Achieved density 365 kg/m³ (target 300 kg/m³). Optimised mix (Sample 26) provides the inner core of the break-

the current prototype design as a manufacturing safety margin.

5. Modelling and simulation

The SUREWAVE consortium built and validated a coupled modelling toolchain covering hydrodynamics, aerody-
namics, structural integrity and fatigue.
Coupled aero-hydrodynamics
Hydrodynamic behaviour of the coupled FPV-breakwater system was analysed with linear methods at low to
mid fidelity, calibrated against Stadt towing-tank test results. Motion response, connector loading with varying
float sizes, and mooring design loads were evaluated across the three reference sites. Aerodynamic simulations
in CFD characterised lift and drag coefÏcients as functions of PV module inclination and array geometry, and
produced correlations for breakwater shielding effectiveness at different angles of attack.
The floating breakwater substantially reduces vertical loads on the FPV array. Its horizontal shielding effect is

Figure 10: SUREWAVE integrated modelling toolchain -- from metocean data through coupled aero-hydro, struc-
tural FE, fatigue and corrosion-sensor inputs to the SHMS platform.

more limited across large farms, which motivated the sub-farm mooring strategy adopted in the reference plant.

Mooring optimisation
Mooring designs were optimised via surrogate modelling and genetic algorithms, delivering up to 56 % chain-
weight reduction against a first-iteration deterministically-sized baseline at equal structural safety. The optimi-
sation covered chain size, catenary geometry, anchor spacing and pretensioning strategy.

Structural integrity
Advanced finite element analysis with submodelling techniques was used to assess critical breakwater compo-
nents. Global FE models fed reinforced-concrete submodels subjected to extreme and fatigue load cases across
the three sites. Fatigue analysis used Goodman and Soderberg criteria and demonstrated infinite-life design for
the breakwater under specified loading. Detailed stress-field outputs feed the crack propagation and health-
management tools.
Beyond static and extreme-load analysis, the toolchain includes a fatigue-assessment tool for critical breakwater
rebar locations, validated against literature and demonstrated end to end in a North Sea case study. The tool
also feeds the Structural Health Management System described in Section 7.

Validation
All modelling methodologies were validated against international standards (ABS and DNV guidelines) and ex-
perimental data from the WP4 material tests and WP6 physical prototyping. The validation report closes the
modelling loop; identified limitations include localised pitÝng corrosion (generalised corrosion only for now),
lack of probabilistic uncertainty quantification, and limited long-term field data for calibration -- all identified
as forward-work priorities.

6. Testing and validation

Laboratory scale -- materials

Durability testing on HPC and LWAC shell materials with and without waterproofing admixture confirmed com-
pliance with marine-exposure code XS2/XS3. Static and cyclic bending, pullout and drop-tower impact tests on
600 × 150 × 100 mm sandwich beams (HPC or LWAC shell over cellular concrete core) demonstrated HPC as
mechanically superior for both static and dynamic loading.

Component-level -- connections

Three critical connection systems were tested at SINTEF: polyurethane hinges between FPV modules, cable
systems between floating breakwaters, and new tailored connections between breakwaters and FPV modules.
First-generation polyurethane hinges showed a component tensile ultimate of 3.5 kN on average -- below the
8 to 10 kN operational-load band expected from coupled hydrodynamic analysis -- and low compressive resis-
tance. Steel-wire connections between breakwaters performed as specified at 402 kN ultimate load against a
450 kN rating. The new breakwater-to-module connection met its 41 kN requirement, with the rope element
identified as the weak point.

The first-generation hinge finding drove a redesign led by Sunlit Sea. The redesigned polyurethane component
was validated in a second round of testing at SINTEF and delivered approximately three times the first-generation
component tensile ultimate on the same test scale, with buckling strength raised to 20 kN. Fatigue testing at
the design operational stress (approximately 11 MPa in the polyurethane) gave a calculated failure life of 2.5 ×
10¹¹ cycles -- well above the 7.88 × 10 cycles required for 25 years of service; the margin is stress-conditional
and would tighten if a stiffer connecting element raised the component stress. Production quality control is the
remaining sensitivity: material shrinkage of 1 to 2 mm during casting drives performance variability and needs
strict process control in mass production.

At the full-hinge system level, integrated finite-element analysis under 50-year design storms gives an ultimate
limit state of 37 kN. The Baltic (34 kN) and Mediterranean (33 kN) design loads clear the ULS with margin; the
North Sea design load of 40 kN exceeds the current Gen-3 ULS and a further design iteration is required for
North Sea qualification. Two distinct load regimes drive the design: the 50-year extreme values set the ULS; the
8­10 kN operational band sets the fatigue life.

Reduced-scale prototype

A 3.4 m by 1.7 m footprint reduced-scale breakwater prototype was manufactured at Acciona's workshop. It
combines an HPC bottom slab and walls (0.94 m³), an LWAC top slab (0.35 m³), a cellular concrete core (0.50
m³) and EPS buoyancy blocks. Design parameters -- metacentric height above 0.25 m, freeboard above 0.3
m -- were set for deployment at the Port of Gijón, taking wave loading and marine growth into account. A
four-line catenary spread mooring with 10 mm chain and 20 mm polyester rope holds the prototype on station
while minimising vertical loads. At approximately 1:6 in plan against the 20 × 5 × 2.5 m production breakwater
the prototype validates the concrete formulations, the buoyancy design and the mooring behaviour in a real
marine environment; the structural response of the full-scale unit is separately validated through the modelling
toolchain described in Section 5.

Manufacturing proceeded without major complications. Surface finish came out rougher than target due to
LWAC casting characteristics but is acceptable for the prototype's monitoring purpose.

The deployment site is El Musel Port in Gijón, on the northern Spanish coast -- one of Atlantic Europe's main
maritime ports and host to Acciona's R&D maritime environment demo area. Design-basis metocean data for
the site was drawn from the Spanish national ports oceanographic archive. Field deployment feeds long-term
degradation and structural-health data back into the modelling toolchain: the prototype is instrumented and
monitored under WP6 with periodic inspection and sensor-driven readouts, validating both the durability of the
three circular concrete formulations and the mooring behaviour in real wave, tide and current conditions.

Basin testing

Two wave-basin campaigns were completed across the project.

The first was at the Stadt Towing Tank in Norway, provided by Sunlit Sea. Six FPV floats in a representative string
configuration were tested under regular and irregular waves to measure motion response, inter-float connec-
tor loading and matrix-level dynamic behaviour. The results calibrated the linear hydrodynamic methodology
used subsequently across all three reference sites and gave the first physical evidence for the connector-force
distribution assumed in the design of the polyurethane hinges.

The second was at MARIN's wave basin in Wageningen. A scaled model of individual floating-breakwater units

7. Structural Health Management System

The SUREWAVE Structural Health Management System (SHMS) is a MATLAB platform that integrates finite-
element stress fields, crack-propagation algorithms and degradation data from a purpose-built eddy-current
corrosion sensor for embedded reinforcement in offshore concrete. It delivers five design- and operations-
phase capabilities: material comparison for design optimisation, degradation-impact assessment relating sen-
sor readings to residual capacity, automated inspection-interval recommendations (a four-year cycle plus event-
triggered checks), critical crack-orientation identification for sensor placement, and quality-control criteria for
production.

The corrosion sensor is a non-destructive eddy-current device that detects rebar degradation through the con-
crete cover at sub-millimetre resolution. Placement follows the structural-integrity stress field: highest-load
breakwaters, mooring and connector attachments, geometric stress concentrators. Full sensor specifications

8. Environmental impact

Life-cycle assessment and local environmental impact assessment were carried out across the three reference
sites.
Climate benefit
Offshore FPV installed under SUREWAVE assumptions avoids of the order of 170,000 tonnes CO-equivalent per
reference plant over 25 years, measured against the fossil generation it displaces at the moment of production.
In the European markets considered, that marginal generation is still predominantly coal during peak-demand
hours, which coincide with the solar production profile. D7.3 gives 180,000 t for the uniform North Sea layout
and 160,000 t for the split North Sea layout; Baltic and Mediterranean reference plants deliver comparable re-
ductions. Measured against the 24-hour EU average generation mix the savings are smaller but still substantial.

Figure 16: 25-year cumulative CO2 emissions per plant -- SUREWAVE offshore FPV against a coal-fired reference,
across the three sites.
Other environmental impacts
Impacts higher than onshore renewables are documented; offshore FPV is positioned to complement, not re-
place, land-based solar. The dominant contributions are:

    · Vessel fuel consumption during installation and O&M -- the largest single life-cycle impact category.
    · Submarine cable routing through sensitive marine biotopes.
    · Shading of underlying marine flora within the array footprint.
    · Underwater noise from anchoring operations, affecting marine mammals during deployment.
Design recommendations
Environmental performance is optimised by high-irradiation site selection, minimising cable length by tapping
into existing offshore infrastructure where possible, adopting sustainable marine fuels for the installation and

O&M fleet, and applying site-specific mitigation measures informed by full Environmental Impact Assessments
per deployment location.

9. Economic case

The economic assessment covers three configurations (2 MW, 10 MW modular, 10 MW full-scale) at each of the
three reference sites, across pessimistic, base and optimistic scenarios. The base-case model uses a weighted-
average cost of capital of 8.5 % and a notional ofÚake electricity price of 0.12/kWh carried from the grant-
application assumptions; a bankable project would need to negotiate its own PPA on project-specific terms.
The 50-year project lifetime is sustained by a mid-life PV module reinstallation at year 25 and a mooring-chain
replacement at year 40, both costed into the model as scheduled OPEX reserves.

The Mediterranean 10 MW full-scale configuration is commercially viable under conventional discount rates.
The Baltic configuration is marginal -- viable for developers with a lower cost of capital or with policy support.
The North Sea configuration is not viable without significant financial support or a hybrid framing (co-siting with
offshore wind, dedicated demonstration funding, or grid-integration credits).

Sensitivities
CAPEX dominates the LCoE across all sites. Sensitivity to discount rate is high -- North Sea only turns positive
at discount rates below 6 %. Cable connection is the single most sensitive economic variable: short floating

                          SUREWAVE  D8.3 Technical Brochure  September 2026 p.14
cables to existing offshore infrastructure preserve viability; adding a full dedicated submarine cable connection
to a greenfield site erodes it substantially. For reference, a Mediterranean 10 MW SUREWAVE plant tied in
via a short connection to a nearby offshore substation delivers 0.062/kWh; the same plant with a dedicated
20 km submarine connection sits in the 0.09­0.11/kWh range depending on landing conditions. Any serious
bankability case for the technology begins with the connection route.
Project insurance (nominally in the range 1­3 % of CAPEX per year for first-of-kind marine assets) and decom-
missioning liability (typically 5­10 % of CAPEX, project-funded via a sinking fund or bond) are not included in
the LCoE figures above. Both are project-specific line items that a developer or lender model would add in their
own commercial pro-forma.
The three circular concrete formulations were specified to carry the breakwater's structural duty while reducing
embodied carbon in the units. The economics of a SUREWAVE plant are dominated by the cable connection
route and overall CAPEX rather than by the breakwater material choice; the circular formulations contribute to
the sustainability case documented in Section 8 without penalising the economics.

Recommended deployment sequencing
Mediterranean first for commercial pilots. Baltic second, with cost-of-capital arrangements or policy support.
North Sea third, deferred to hybrid or demonstration contexts until either the cost stack or the market environ-
ment changes.

10. Social sustainability

Social life-cycle assessment across the offshore FPV supply chain and construction/operation phases shows off-
shore FPV social risks can be substantially lower than the fossil reference, provided that:

    · High-risk supply chains are avoided or actively managed. Solar cell production is heavily concentrated
       in China, with documented forced-labour concerns in Xinjiang traceable through the polysilicon supply
       chain. Steel for anchoring and reinforcement, aluminium and copper for cables and frames, and marine
       fuel consumption are additional risk hotspots.

    · Material intensity per kWh is minimised. Location choice -- solar irradiation, cable length, metocean
       conditions -- dominates over technology-design choices for social risk. Site and supply chain are the
       primary social-risk drivers rather than the choice of breakwater material or layout.

    · Construction and maintenance labour rights and offshore worker safety are actively managed. These carry
       documented risks even though their overall contribution to total social risk is small.

Recommendations align with the EU Corporate Sustainability Due Diligence Directive: EU low-risk-country sourc-
ing where possible, certified suppliers in higher-risk nations, direct engagement models with critical upstream
partners, and diversification of PV production capacity within the EU as a strategic priority.
Local social engagement -- with fishing, tourism, navigation and other maritime economic actors -- is addressed
by a stakeholder engagement framework developed in the project. Effective site-level deployment requires
broad and early stakeholder engagement, careful marine spatial planning, and consideration of trade-offs across
environmental, social and economic dimensions.

Where SUREWAVE fits in the offshore renewables portfolio
SUREWAVE does not compete with offshore wind. It complements it. Floating PV at coastal irradiation levels
and moderate sea states is a distinct addressable segment, particularly attractive where sea-basin conditions
permit but seabed conditions or wind resource make offshore wind less attractive, and where existing offshore
infrastructure -- cables, substations, ports -- can be leveraged to reduce marginal deployment cost.
Co-location with offshore wind is a natural extension. A floating PV array inside the perimeter of a floating
offshore wind farm shares grid infrastructure, mooring corridors, monitoring assets and operational logistics.
SUREWAVE has established the design basis and validated toolchain that make such co-location engineering
feasible.

13. About Sunlit Sea

Sunlit Sea AS is a Norwegian technology company developing floating solar photovoltaic solutions for offshore
and coastal deployment. Founded in Trondheim, Sunlit Sea's technology combines pressed marine-grade alu-
minium float modules with modular string assembly for rapid deployment and low CAPEX. The company has
delivered pilot installations in Norwegian coastal waters and is preparing utility-scale deployments in Norway
and abroad. Sunlit Sea leads SUREWAVE Work Packages 2 and 8, contributes to Work Package 3 on internal FPV
component design and to Work Package 6 on component-level mechanical validation, and is responsible for
the economic assessment in Work Package 7. Sunlit Sea's independent Gen 2 product line and the SUREWAVE
consortium output share the same core architecture -- flat aluminium modules, flexible hinge connections,
matrix-based deployment. The overlap makes technology transfer from SUREWAVE into a shipping product an
incremental step rather than a new programme.
