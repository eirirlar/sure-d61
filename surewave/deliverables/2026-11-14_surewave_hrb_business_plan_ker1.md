# Business plan — KER1 Sunlit Sea CONNECT floating photovoltaic platform for nearshore and sheltered coastal waters

Horizon Results Booster G2M input · Sunlit Sea AS · sections 2–5, 15 November 2026

## The organisation

### Mission

Sunlit Sea builds solar power plants on coastal water, so that industry and ports with no land to spare can produce renewable electricity next to where they use it.

### Company profile

Sunlit Sea AS is a Norwegian floating solar company, founded in 2019 and based in Oslo. It sells floating solar systems and site engineering, and in Norway it also owns and operates its own plants as an independent power producer, selling the power to local industry under power purchase agreements (PPAs).

The company has brought two product generations to market:

- Generation 1, a floating solar system for calm water, has been in commercial operation since 2023, at Mannheim Cablepark in Germany (60 kWp, customer Rixen Cableway) and at Skiftestjørna in Haugaland Næringspark, Norway (105 kWp, Sunlit Sea's own plant, since 2024). Generation 1 was made in Sunlit Sea's own factory; in 2024 the company moved to subcontracted production to concentrate on Generation 2.
- Generation 2, Sunlit Sea CONNECT, is designed for waves and is the subject of this plan. It was developed in the Horizon Europe projects SUREWAVE (2022–2026) and SuRE (2024–2027), with testing at the research institutes SINTEF and MARIN.

CEO and board chair Per Lindberg invented Generation 1 and leads research and development on Generation 2. Technical lead Eirik Larsen is responsible for product development, sales and administration. The core team is small and works with partners for simulation (the Institute for Energy Technology, IFE), photovoltaic module integration (Metsolar, Lithuania) and sales in Germany (Rixen Cableway). Development has been financed by EU and Norwegian public funding and by private equity from Norwegian investors.

### Objectives

Short term (2027–2028):

- Freeze the CONNECT design and reach technology readiness level (TRL) 6 for sheltered sites through the first reference plant.
- Commission the first CONNECT plants in Norway: Skien havn (350 kWp, own plant, 2027) and Gunneklevfjorden in Hærøya Industripark (3.2 MWp, 2028).
- Start a DNV design-basis assessment and set up European series production with partners.
- Raise growth capital from strategic investors in 2027.

Long term (2029–2032):

- Sell CONNECT as a kit to developers and contractors across the Nordics, the Baltic and the Mediterranean.
- Win the first 10 MW-class supply contract by 2030.
- Extend the product to exposed sites with a directional breakwater.

Economic objectives: about 3.5 MWp in operation by the end of 2028 and 4–5 MWp by 2029, and revenue of about €1–2 million a year from 2029. The break-even year and funding need are set in the financial plan, which follows the December workshop.

### Sunlit Sea CONNECT

Each float is a cast polyurethane frame on a flat bottom plate (aluminium in the current design), carrying one standard 710–740 Wp glass-glass solar panel at a small, fixed tilt just above the water. Floats are joined along their full edge by flexible polyurethane hinges, so the array follows the waves instead of resisting them. The panels come from the mainstream solar market, so the product follows the price development of the global module market.

Where waves are higher than the platform alone can take, a floating breakwater is placed only on the sides the strong waves come from. Sunlit Sea's planning tool, built on SUREWAVE wave and wind models, works out for each site where that protection is needed and how much energy is lost to wave motion. The wave conditions each plant is designed for are set in its design basis; the first plants are at sheltered sites.

What sets CONNECT apart:

- Load capacity: SUREWAVE testing raised the ultimate load capacity of the critical connection from 14 kN to 37 kN with the third hinge generation.
- Standard components: mainstream panels, polyurethane casting and a flat bottom plate, with no custom panels or specialist steelwork.
- Installation from ordinary quays, without specialist offshore vessels. A damaged hinge can be replaced without taking the array out of the water.
- Protection sized to the site, which keeps breakwater cost to the sides that need it.
- Social and environmental benefits: renewable power next to coastal industry and on island grids without taking land, and, on reservoirs and irrigation canals in dry regions, less evaporation.

### Intellectual property

Sunlit Sea protects CONNECT through know-how, design rights and speed to market. Patenting was assessed with a patent attorney and not pursued, because the field of flexible floating solar connections has extensive prior art. The protection consists of:

- trade secrets in the casting process and in the site-adaptation rules of the planning tool, kept under confidentiality agreements and controlled access;
- copyright in drawings, CAD files and software;
- a decision on design registration for the hinge and float, in the EU and Norway, by Q3 2027;
- a freedom-to-operate review in 2027 covering flexible connections between floats and arrangements that combine breakwater and array, with support from the Horizon Results Booster's IP service;
- speed to market and the reference plants.

### Prices and customers

The target price for the floating system (floats, hinges and mooring interfaces, excluding panels) is €0.30–0.40 per watt for early projects, falling with volume. Site engineering is priced at 8–12 % of project capital cost. On its own plants, Sunlit Sea earns from selling power under PPAs.

Customers and partners today: Rixen Cableway (Germany, Generation 1 customer and sales partner, with follow-up leads in Germany, Türkiye and Italy), Aaltvedt Betong (PPA offtaker for Skien havn), Hærøya Industripark (host for Gunneklevfjorden) and Haugaland Næringspark (host for Skiftestjørna and Storavatnet).

### Value chain

1. Site screening and engineering: Sunlit Sea, with the planning tool.
2. Components: polyurethane casting by subcontractors (prototypes in Asia today; series production planned in Europe), bottom plates from metal suppliers, panels from the module market, module integration with Metsolar.
3. Packing and shipping to site as a kit, planned from Lithuania.
4. Assembly and launch at a local quay by a local contractor, supervised and trained by Sunlit Sea.
5. Mooring by local marine contractors; breakwater, where needed, from Clement Germany, the SUREWAVE breakwater partner, or another qualified supplier.
6. Operation and maintenance by the owner or a local contractor, with Sunlit Sea support and spare hinges.

### Development and qualification

CONNECT is qualified against the 2026 DNV floating solar standards before series production. The programme covers fatigue of hinge and mooring under wave cycles, durability of the polyurethane in sun and seawater (accelerated UV ageing to IEC TS 62788-7-2 followed by mechanical testing), corrosion protection of the bottom plate and its connections, marine growth, and field monitoring of the first plants. Storm failures of floating solar plants elsewhere have started at moorings and float-to-float connections, which is the load path the full-edge hinge is designed to spread.

## The market

### Summary

Floating solar is growing fast worldwide, but almost all of it sits on calm inland water. Floating solar at sea is at the pilot stage in Europe, and no supplier yet has a commercial multi-megawatt reference on European coastal water. Sunlit Sea targets the first commercial niche at sea: plants of 0.3–10 MWp in sheltered coastal water next to industry and ports, starting in Norway, where grid capacity is scarce and land for ground-mounted solar is contested.

### Market size

| Level | Market | Size |
|---|---|---|
| Total | Floating solar worldwide | 8.7 GW installed at the end of 2024 (IEA PVPS); 77 GW forecast for 2033 (Wood Mackenzie) |
| Serviceable | Nearshore and sheltered coastal floating solar in Europe | No published size. National studies indicate 4.5–6.5 GW of potential for floating solar at sea in Spain and 45 GWp of solar at sea in Dutch scenarios for 2050 |
| Obtainable, near term | Sunlit Sea's own pipeline | 6.75 MWp at three Norwegian sites (Skien havn 0.35, Gunneklevfjorden 3.2, Storavatnet phase 1 3.2), 30–50 MW long-term potential at Storavatnet, and inbound leads from Germany, Türkiye, Italy and Denmark |

The sales forecast is built bottom-up from the pipeline. The target market share for 2030–2032 is set in the financial plan.

### Sector development

Global solar installations reached a record 664 GW in 2025 (SolarPower Europe). Floating solar grew to 8.7 GW, almost 90 % of it in Asia; the Netherlands and France are the largest markets outside Asia (IEA PVPS). In Europe, rules on coverage and distance limit floating solar on inland water, which pushes the market towards the sea and towards large, wave-exposed lakes and reservoirs. In the EU, more hours with negative power prices reduce the value of solar that sells into the grid, and favour plants that supply a local load directly, as Sunlit Sea's do.

Floating solar at sea is at an early stage: IEA PVPS rates offshore floating PV at TRL 3–5 and treats nearshore floating PV as a separate category. The largest European marine units are 0.5–1 MW. In Asia, offshore solar has reached gigawatt scale, mostly on piles in shallow water.

In Norway, the energy regulator NVE expects ground-mounted solar to be held back by grid scarcity and land-use conflicts: by early 2024 it had received applications for about 1.4 GW but licensed 14 MW, against about 10 GW needed for the national 2030 goal. The grid operator Statnett reports a fast-growing queue for grid connection, with about 3,500 MW of mature applications in the first half of 2026. On-site solar supplies power without waiting for a grid connection.

### Barriers to entry

- Bankability: lenders and insurers review storm and fatigue loads, mooring, cable movement, warranties and maintenance access, and want permits in place before debt. This favours suppliers with an independently reviewed design and reference plants.
- Standards: DNV published floating solar standards for floats (DNV-ST-C108) and mooring (DNV-ST-E309) in 2026, alongside DNV-RP-0584. They set a clear path to a bankable design.
- Permits: in Norway, solar plants below 10 MW have needed no national licence since July 2025 and are approved by the municipality, with a harbour permit for plants at sea. Sweden handles floating plants as water operations under its Environmental Code. Germany limits floating solar on inland water; how its rules apply to coastal and harbour water is being clarified before market entry.
- Marine operations and capital: mooring and breakwaters need marine contractors, and each plant needs project finance.

### Role of innovation

Module prices are at historic lows: about €0.11–0.12 per watt for imported modules in Europe in October 2026. The cost of floating solar is now set mainly by the floating structure, mooring, protection and installation. The US National Renewable Energy Laboratory (NREL) found floating solar on calm water about 25 % more expensive than ground-mounted solar, with structural costs about four times higher. Innovation in the structure, and in how much protection a site needs, decides whether floating solar at sea can compete. This is where CONNECT's hinge and site-specific breakwater work.

### Political and regulatory factors

- EU: the Net-Zero Industry Act requires non-price criteria, including resilience, in at least 30 % of auctioned renewable volume from the end of 2025, which gives European-made components more weight.
- EU shore power rules require major ports to supply shore power to most container and passenger ships from 2030, which adds power demand in ports.
- EU and Nordic funding supports first-of-a-kind plants: Horizon Europe, the European Innovation Council, the EU Innovation Fund, and Enova, the Norwegian state agency for energy investment, which supports Sunlit Sea's Gunneklevfjorden project.
- Norway: the 10 MW licensing threshold covers Sunlit Sea's whole plant range.

### Sector summary (five forces)

| Force | Assessment |
|---|---|
| Rivalry | Low to medium: few players, all at pilot stage, often partners in EU projects |
| New entrants | Medium: inland float suppliers are developing heavier-duty products and start-ups are appearing, but all need wave-tested designs and references |
| Substitutes | High: grid power, remote PPAs, rooftop solar, offshore wind for large buyers |
| Buyer power | Medium: few, well-informed industrial buyers who compare with their grid price |
| Supplier power | Low for panels; medium for casting and marine contractors |

The niche is attractive for a first mover: rivalry is low, and the main pressure comes from substitutes, which coastal plants beat where land is blocked and grid capacity is scarce.

### Customers and end users

Two customer segments, with one value proposition each:

1. Coastal industry and ports with local power demand and no free land (process industry, ports, quarries, terminals). They want local, fixed-price renewable electricity without waiting for a grid connection, and lower Scope 2 emissions. They buy power under a long-term PPA and want one supplier responsible for the whole floating system.

   Value proposition: renewable power produced on your own water area, next to your load, at a fixed long-term price, from a system designed for your site's waves.

2. Renewable energy developers and independent power producers building 0.3–10 MWp plants on coastal water. They buy the floating system and engineering and need a design their lenders and insurers accept.

   Value proposition: a wave-tested floating system delivered as a kit, with site engineering that sizes structure and breakwater to the real wave conditions, at a cost per watt that makes coastal sites investable.

An adjacent segment is large lakes and hydropower reservoirs exposed to waves. Sunlit Sea's EIC Transition application targets it with a utility partner.

In the Nordics, power-intensive industry signs more than half of the disclosed PPA volume, and developers aim for contracts of at least 10 years to match their debt (Nordic Energy Research, 2025). The recurring buyer and lender concerns are bankability, storm resilience, insurance and maintenance access.

Early adopters in the first segment are Norwegian industrial parks and harbours (Aaltvedt Betong at Skien havn, Hærøya Industripark, Haugaland Næringspark), the quarry and port operator at Storugns on Gotland, Sweden, and Generation 1 customer Rixen Cableway. Each has a local power demand, a water area it controls or can lease, and no land for solar, and each host has committed time to permits and studies. In the second segment, the first developer customers are targeted from 2028 through Rixen Cableway and EU demonstration projects. A structured round of customer interviews on decision criteria and acceptable price is planned for early 2027.

- Know-how in casting and site adaptation, protected as trade secrets.
- Speed: the first nearshore reference plants in the Nordics in 2027–2028.
- Close ties to customers through own plants, and to suppliers through long-term partners.
- Economies of scale from standard panels and simple cast parts.
- Continuous development: hinge generations, the exposed-site version and the edge system.
- A reputation built on measured performance and an independently reviewed design.

### Unique value proposition

Our SUREWAVE-validated nearshore floating solar platform helps coastal industry and renewable developers who want to grow renewable power where onshore land is blocked, by matching the design to the real wave conditions of each site and by bringing the cost of electricity down with standard solar panels and simple manufacturing, unlike calm-water floating solar that fails in waves and offshore concepts that need costly full protection or specialist vessels.

## The business model and marketing strategy

### How Sunlit Sea makes money

Four revenue streams:

1. Supply of the floating system as a kit, priced per watt, to plants owned by others.
2. Site engineering for plants owned by others: site screening, structural and mooring design, breakwater placement and yield estimate.
3. Power sales from Sunlit Sea's own plants, where it acts as an independent power producer and sells the power under PPAs.
4. Sale of own plants to long-term owners once they have an operating record.

On its own plants, the floats and installation are an investment, not a sale; Sunlit Sea earns from the power.

### Actors

- Customers: industrial offtakers and site hosts (PPAs); developers and independent power producers (kits and engineering).
- Suppliers: casting subcontractors, metal suppliers, module manufacturers, Metsolar for module integration.
- Delivery partners: local solar and electrical contractors (for example Prosolar in Norway), marine contractors for mooring, Clement Germany or others for breakwaters, and the SUREWAVE partner Ceit for structural health monitoring as an option.
- Financing: banks at plant level, Enova and EU programmes, equity investors at company level.
- Assurance: DNV for design-basis assessment; SINTEF and MARIN for testing.

### How the model changes with scale

| Stage | Typical project | Sunlit Sea's role | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| Start (2027–2028) | 0.3–3 MWp, Norway | Independent power producer with own plants, plus engineering and supply to partner-owned plants | Creates the reference record that later buyers need |
| Technology supplier (from 2028) | 1–10 MWp | Sells kits and engineering to developers and contractors | Scales without tying up capital in each plant |
| EPC contractor (from about 2030) | Tens of MWp | Takes responsibility for delivering the full floating system | Larger buyers want one responsible contractor |
| Portfolio owner (long term) | Several larger plants | Owns and operates plants, and sells them when it pays | Highest value once capital is available |

The near-term plan is the first two stages; the later stages depend on the capital available. Both ends of the model are established in the sector: Swimsol owns and operates sea-based solar plants in the Maldives, financed with debt, and developers of inland floating plants in Europe sell completed plants to long-term owners. EPC is taken on when the balance sheet can carry the delivery risk.

### Breakwater supply

The breakwater comes from a partner, and the supply route depends on the project:

- Sunlit Sea delivers the full system and buys the breakwater from Clement Germany, which installs it.
- The customer buys the breakwater directly from Clement and coordinates installation.
- Clement leads the project, with Sunlit Sea as subcontractor for the floating solar system.

The first Norwegian sites are sheltered enough to need little or no breakwater.

### Manufacturing

Production is subcontracted. Sunlit Sea designs the parts, owns the moulds and controls quality. Series production is planned in Europe, with in-house moulds in Norway for pilot volumes and a European casting partner for series volumes. Metsolar in Lithuania integrates modules and is a candidate for packing kits for shipment straight to site.

### Pricing strategy

- Kit price: €0.30–0.40 per watt for early projects, set to cover cost plus a target gross margin of 30–40 % at scale, to be tested in the financial plan. NREL's benchmark for calm-water floats falls from USD 0.40 per watt at 2 MW to 0.30 at 10 MW and 0.20 at 50 MW, so prices for large plants will be lower.
- Engineering: 8–12 % of project capital cost.
- PPAs on own plants: priced against what the host pays today for power delivered at its site. In 2025, Norwegian service industries paid on average 62.5 øre/kWh (about €0.053/kWh) and power-intensive industry 42 øre/kWh (about €0.036/kWh) for electricity, both before grid tariff and taxes (Statistics Norway). A plant behind the meter also saves grid tariff and avoids waiting for a grid connection. First sites are therefore chosen in southern Norway, where power prices are highest, and with hosts that need more power than their grid connection can deliver.
- Prices differ by market, depending on site conditions and the local price of power.
- The financial plan will show the unit economics with a worked example for a Norwegian plant.

### Market segment and early adopters

The first segment is coastal industry and ports in Norway because:

- land conflicts, grid queues and growing industrial power demand create the need;
- the sites are sheltered (fjords and industrial basins), so the product works without a costly breakwater;
- Sunlit Sea knows the permitting process and has local partners;
- Enova and municipal support lower the risk of the first plants.

### Sales and promotion channels

Channels are added step by step, to keep costs under control:

1. 2027: direct sales by management, with local contractors as joint developers (Prosolar in Norway); Rixen Cableway in Germany; industrial park owners and harbour authorities.
2. 2028–2029: a sales lead recruited after the funding round; contractors and developers as resellers of the kit; EU demonstration projects with SINTEF and partners.
3. From 2030: distributors or licensees in markets Sunlit Sea will not serve directly.

Promotion: reference plant visits, published measured yield, the Horizon Results Platform, the European Cluster Collaboration Platform, industry events and LinkedIn. Promotion is part of the business development budget of about €0.3 million in 2027, which also covers sales and permitting.
