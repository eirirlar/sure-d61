# Business plan — KER1 Sunlit Sea CONNECT floating photovoltaic platform for nearshore and sheltered coastal waters

Horizon Results Booster G2M input · Sunlit Sea AS · sections 2–5, 15 November 2026, revised 23 November 2026 to match the financial plan · confidential: contains Sunlit Sea's costs and prices, for the Booster mentors only

## The organisation

### Mission

Sunlit Sea builds solar power plants on coastal water, so that industry and ports with no land to spare can produce renewable electricity next to where they use it.

### Company profile

Sunlit Sea AS is a Norwegian floating solar company, founded in 2019 and based in Oslo. In Norway it develops and delivers the first CONNECT plants itself, as developer and EPC (engineering, procurement and construction) contractor to the plant companies of industrial hosts, and plans to own one reference plant, Skien havn, that sells its power under a power purchase agreement (PPA) from 2027. From 2030 it sells the CONNECT floating system mainly as a kit, with the solar panel included, and site engineering to developers and contractors who build the plants.

The company has brought two product generations to market:

- Generation 1, a floating solar system for calm water, has been in commercial operation since 2023, at Mannheim Cablepark in Germany (60 kWp, customer Rixen Cableway) and at Skiftestjørna in Haugaland Næringspark, Norway (105 kWp, Sunlit Sea's own plant, under a PPA from 2024 to October 2026; its future use is open). Generation 1 was made in Sunlit Sea's own factory; in 2024 the factory was wound down and Generation 1 production ended, to concentrate on Generation 2, whose production is subcontracted.
- Generation 2, Sunlit Sea CONNECT, is designed for waves and is the subject of this plan. It was developed in the Horizon Europe projects SUREWAVE (2022–2026) and SuRE (2024–2027), with testing at the research institutes SINTEF and MARIN.

CEO and board chair Per Lindberg invented Generation 1 and leads research and development on Generation 2. Technical lead Eirik Larsen is responsible for product development, sales and administration. The core team is small and works with partners for simulation (the Institute for Energy Technology, IFE), photovoltaic module integration (Metsolar, Lithuania) and sales in Germany (Rixen Cableway). Development has been financed by EU and Norwegian public funding and by private equity from Norwegian investors.

Long term (2029–2032):

- Sell CONNECT as a kit to developers and contractors in the Nordics, Germany and the Baltic, and later the Mediterranean.

Economic objectives, from the financial plan of 22 November 2026: 4.5 MWp delivered by the end of 2029 and about 28 MWp by 2031; revenue of €2.5 million in 2028, €3.9 million in 2030 and €8.2 million in 2031; EBITDA break-even in 2030 and net break-even in 2031; €5.5 million of equity in two rounds, €3.5 million in the first quarter of 2027 and €2.0 million in the second half of 2029.

### Sunlit Sea CONNECT

Each float is a cast polyurethane frame on a flat bottom plate (aluminium in the current design), carrying one standard 710–740 Wp glass-glass solar panel at a small, fixed tilt just above the water. Floats are joined along their full edge by flexible polyurethane hinges, so the array follows the waves instead of resisting them. The panels come from the mainstream solar market, so the product follows the price development of the global module market.

Where waves are higher than the platform alone can take, a floating breakwater is placed only on the sides the strong waves come from. Sunlit Sea's site-planning tool, built on SUREWAVE wave and wind models, works out for each site where that protection is needed and how much energy is lost to wave motion. The wave conditions each plant is designed for are set in its design basis. The first plants are at sheltered sites; the first, Skien havn, uses small wavebreakers on its exposed side.

What sets CONNECT apart:

- Load capacity: after SUREWAVE testing at SINTEF and MARIN, the full-width third-generation hinge has a design ultimate-limit-state break load of 37 kN, against 14 kN for the second-generation corner hinge (SUREWAVE D7.6). D7.6 states that the redesigned hinge could not be fully validated within the project, and exposed North Sea sites need a further hinge generation.
- Standard components: mainstream panels, polyurethane casting and a flat bottom plate, with no custom panels or specialist steelwork.
- Installation from ordinary quays, without specialist offshore vessels. A damaged hinge can be replaced without taking the array out of the water.
- Protection sized to the site, which keeps breakwater cost to the sides that need it.
- Social and environmental benefits: renewable power next to coastal industry and on island grids without taking land, and, on reservoirs and irrigation canals in dry regions, less evaporation.

### Intellectual property

Sunlit Sea protects CONNECT through know-how, design rights and speed to market. Patenting was assessed with a patent attorney and not pursued, because the field of flexible floating solar connections has extensive prior art. The protection consists of:

- trade secrets in the casting process and in the site-adaptation rules of the site-planning tool, kept under confidentiality agreements and controlled access;
- copyright in drawings, CAD files and software;
- a decision on design registration for the hinge and float, in the EU and Norway, by Q3 2027;
- a freedom-to-operate review in 2027 covering flexible connections between floats and arrangements that combine breakwater and array, with support from the Horizon Results Booster's IP service;
- speed to market and the reference plants.

### Prices and customers

The kit is priced including the solar panel, at about €500 per kWp, falling slowly with volume. That is about €0.39 per watt for the floating system alone. Site engineering for kit buyers is about 4 % of plant cost, and 8–12 % where Sunlit Sea also does the development work. In phase 1, the delivered plant is priced at €0.70–0.73 per watt to the plant company. At Skien havn, Sunlit Sea earns from selling the power under a PPA.

Customers and partners today: Rixen Cableway (Germany, Generation 1 customer and sales partner, with follow-up leads in Germany, Türkiye and Italy), Aaltvedt Betong (PPA offtaker for Skien havn), Herøya Industripark (host for Gunneklevfjorden) and Haugaland Næringspark (host for Skiftestjørna and Storavatnet).

### Development and qualification

CONNECT will be qualified against the 2026 DNV floating solar standards before series production. The programme covers fatigue of hinge and mooring under wave cycles, durability of the polyurethane in sun and seawater (accelerated UV ageing to IEC TS 62788-7-2 followed by mechanical testing), corrosion protection of the bottom plate and its connections, marine growth, and field monitoring of the first plants. Storm failures of floating solar plants elsewhere have started at moorings and float-to-float connections, which is the load path the full-edge hinge is designed to spread.

## The market

### Summary

Floating solar is growing fast worldwide, mostly on inland water: IEA PVPS rates floating PV on inland water above TRL 8 and offshore floating PV at TRL 3–5 (Task 13, 2025). Floating solar at sea is at the pilot stage in Europe, and no supplier yet has a commercial multi-megawatt reference on European coastal water. Sunlit Sea targets the first commercial niche at sea: plants of 0.3–10 MWp in sheltered coastal water next to industry and ports, starting in Norway, where grid capacity is scarce and land for ground-mounted solar is contested.

### Market size

| Level | Market | Size |
|---|---|---|
| Total | Floating solar worldwide | 8.7 GW installed at the end of 2024 (IEA PVPS); 77 GW forecast for 2033 (Wood Mackenzie) |
| Serviceable | Nearshore and sheltered coastal floating solar in Europe | No published size. A study by the University of A Coruña gives 4.45–6.48 GW of potential for floating solar at sea within Spain's maritime spatial plans (Journal of Cleaner Production, 2026), and TNO scenarios have 45 GWp of solar at sea in the Netherlands in 2050 |
| Obtainable, near term | Sunlit Sea's own pipeline | 6.75 MWp at three Norwegian sites (Skien havn 0.35, Gunneklevfjorden 3.2, Storavatnet phase 1 3.2), 30–50 MW long-term potential at Storavatnet, and inbound leads from Germany, Türkiye, Italy and Denmark |

The sales forecast is built bottom-up from the pipeline. The financial plan delivers about 7 MWp in 2030 and 16 MWp in 2031, with 25–45 MWp a year in 2033–2035 in its indicative outlook. The 2031 volume is a few per cent of the new floating solar forecast for Germany, France and the Netherlands combined, roughly 0.4–0.5 GW a year in Wood Mackenzie's outlook to 2033.

### Sector development

Global solar installations reached a record 664 GW in 2025 (SolarPower Europe). Floating solar grew to 8.7 GW, almost 90 % of it in Asia; the Netherlands and France are the largest markets outside Asia (IEA PVPS). In Europe, rules on coverage and distance limit floating solar on inland water, which pushes the market towards the sea and towards large, wave-exposed lakes and reservoirs. In the EU, more hours with negative power prices reduce the value of solar that sells into the grid, and favour plants that supply a local load directly, as Sunlit Sea's do.

Floating solar at sea is at an early stage: IEA PVPS rates offshore floating PV at TRL 3–5 and treats nearshore floating PV as a separate category. The largest European marine units are 0.5–1 MW. In Asia, offshore solar has reached gigawatt scale, mostly on piles in shallow water.

In Norway, the energy regulator NVE expects ground-mounted solar to be held back by grid scarcity and land-use conflicts: by early 2024 it had received applications for about 1.4 GW but licensed 14 MW, against about 10 GW needed for the national 2030 goal. The grid operator Statnett reports a fast-growing queue for grid connection, with about 3,500 MW of mature applications in the first half of 2026. On-site solar supplies power without waiting for a grid connection.

### Barriers to entry

- Bankability: lenders and insurers review storm and fatigue loads, mooring, cable movement, warranties and maintenance access, and want permits in place before debt. This favours suppliers with an independently reviewed design and reference plants.
- Standards: DNV published floating solar standards for floats (DNV-ST-C108) and mooring (DNV-ST-E309) in 2026, alongside DNV-RP-0584. They set the basis for an independently reviewed design.
- Permits: in Norway, solar plants below 10 MW have needed no national licence since July 2025 and are approved by the municipality, with a harbour permit for plants at sea. Sweden handles floating plants as water operations under its Environmental Code. Germany limits floating solar on inland water; how its rules apply to coastal and harbour water is being clarified before market entry.
- Marine operations and capital: mooring and breakwaters need marine contractors, and each plant needs project finance.

### Role of innovation

Module prices are at historic lows: about €0.11–0.12 per watt for imported modules in Europe in October 2026. The cost of floating solar is now set mainly by the floating structure, mooring, protection and installation. The US National Renewable Energy Laboratory (NREL) found floating solar on calm water about 25 % more expensive than ground-mounted solar, with structural costs about four times higher. Innovation in the structure, and in how much protection a site needs, decides whether floating solar at sea can compete. This is where CONNECT's hinge and site-specific breakwater work.

### Political and regulatory factors

- EU: the Net-Zero Industry Act requires non-price criteria, including resilience, in at least 30 % of auctioned renewable volume from the end of 2025, which gives European-made components more weight.
- EU shore power rules require major ports to supply shore power to most container and passenger ships from 2030, which adds power demand in ports.
- EU and Nordic funding supports first-of-a-kind plants: Horizon Europe, the European Innovation Council, the EU Innovation Fund, and Enova, the Norwegian state agency for energy investment, which gave Sunlit Sea a pre-project grant for Gunneklevfjorden; Enova's investment support for the plants goes to the plant companies.
- Norway: the 10 MW licensing threshold covers Sunlit Sea's whole plant range.

### Customers and end users

Two customer segments, with one value proposition each:

1. Coastal industry and ports with local power demand and no free land (process industry, ports, quarries, terminals). They want local, fixed-price renewable electricity without waiting for a grid connection, and lower Scope 2 emissions. They buy power under a long-term PPA and want one supplier responsible for the whole floating system.

   Value proposition: renewable power produced on your own water area, next to your load, at a fixed long-term price, from a system designed for your site's waves.

2. Renewable energy developers and independent power producers building 0.3–10 MWp plants on coastal water. They buy the floating system and engineering and need a design their lenders and insurers accept.

   Value proposition: a wave-tested floating system delivered as a kit, with site engineering that sizes structure and breakwater to the site's wave conditions, at a cost per watt that makes coastal sites investable.

An adjacent segment is inland lakes and reservoirs exposed to wind waves. The SINTEF-coordinated EIC Transition proposal, in which Sunlit Sea is a partner, targets it, with a demonstrator on a lake near Leipzig.

In the Nordics, power-intensive industry signs more than half of the disclosed PPA volume, and developers aim for contracts of at least 10 years to match their debt (Nordic Energy Research, 2025). The recurring buyer and lender concerns are bankability, storm resilience, insurance and maintenance access.

Early adopters in the first segment are Norwegian industrial parks and harbours (Aaltvedt Betong at Skien havn, Herøya Industripark, Haugaland Næringspark), the quarry and port operator at Storugns on Gotland, Sweden, if Sunlit Sea applies for the EUPI-PV demonstrator there, and Generation 1 customer Rixen Cableway. Each has a local power demand, a water area it controls or can lease, and no land for solar. The Norwegian hosts have committed time to permits and studies. In the second segment, the first developer customers are targeted from 2028 through Rixen Cableway and EU demonstration projects. A structured round of customer interviews on decision criteria and acceptable price is planned for early 2027.

### Unique value proposition

Our SUREWAVE-validated nearshore floating solar platform helps coastal industry and renewable developers who want to grow renewable power where onshore land is blocked. It matches the design to the wave conditions of each site and brings the cost of electricity down with standard solar panels and simple manufacturing. Calm-water floating solar is not built for waves, and offshore concepts need costly full protection or specialist vessels.

## The business model and marketing strategy

### How Sunlit Sea makes money

Four revenue streams:

1. Delivery of the first Norwegian plants as EPC contractor to the plant companies, priced per watt for the delivered plant (phase 1, 2027–2031).
2. Supply of the floating system as a kit with the solar panel included, priced per kWp, to plants built by others (first kits from 2028, mainly from 2030).
3. Development and site engineering: site screening, structural and mooring design, wavebreaker or breakwater placement and yield estimate.
4. Power sales from the reference plant at Skien havn under a PPA.

Skien havn is an investment, not a sale: Sunlit Sea earns from its power, and the plant is the price of the first CONNECT plant in operation and of the record from its first winter. Sale of plants to long-term owners belongs to a later stage.

### Actors

- Customers: industrial offtakers and site hosts (PPAs); developers and independent power producers (kits and engineering).
- Suppliers: casting subcontractors, metal suppliers, module manufacturers, Metsolar for module integration.
- Delivery partners: local solar and electrical contractors (for example Prosolar in Norway), marine contractors for mooring, Clement Germany or others for breakwaters, and the SUREWAVE partner Ceit for structural health monitoring as an option.
- Financing: banks at plant level, Enova and EU programmes, equity investors at company level.
- Assurance: DNV for design-basis assessment; SINTEF and MARIN for testing.

The near-term plan is the first two stages; the later stages depend on the capital available. Both ends of the model are established in the sector: Swimsol owns and operates sea-based solar plants in the Maldives, financed with debt, and developers of inland floating plants in Europe sell completed plants to long-term owners. In phase 1, EPC is limited to the three Norwegian reference projects, with a contingency in the price, an advance and staged payments, back-to-back subcontracts and capped liability. EPC for large plants is taken on when the balance sheet can carry the delivery risk.

### Breakwater supply

The breakwater comes from a partner, and the supply route depends on the project:

- Sunlit Sea delivers the full system and buys the breakwater from Clement Germany, which installs it.
- The customer buys the breakwater directly from Clement and coordinates installation.
- Clement leads the project, with Sunlit Sea as subcontractor for the floating solar system.

The first Norwegian sites are sheltered. Skien havn uses small wavebreakers on its exposed side, and the other two sites need no breakwater on the current site data. The plant owner buys the breakwater classes. Where Sunlit Sea delivers the full system as EPC contractor, it buys the breakwater and includes it in the EPC price. The small wavebreakers at Skien havn are part of the cost of Sunlit Sea's own plant (tested in the financial plan at about €0.1 million). The financial plan has no breakwater class in its revenue or costs, since the Norwegian EPC sites need none on the current site data and kit buyers buy any breakwater themselves as plant owners. Sunlit Sea's gain from a breakwater site is the kit and engineering for a site that would otherwise be closed to floating PV.

### Manufacturing

Production is subcontracted. Sunlit Sea designs the parts, owns the moulds and controls quality. In-house moulds in Norway serve pilot volumes. For series volumes Sunlit Sea keeps the lowest-cost qualified casting source; Europe is preferred where it is cost-competitive, to protect casting know-how, secure supply and meet European-content preferences. Metsolar in Lithuania integrates modules and is the candidate for packing kits for shipment straight to site, under a framework agreement in 2027–2028 that can be extended to casting. A production joint venture with Metsolar is assessed at the series-production decision in 2029.

### Pricing strategy

- Kit price: about €500 per kWp including the panel, falling slowly with volume, which gives a gross margin of about 44 % at today's delivered cost of about €280 per kWp (production cost about €250 plus packing and freight) and about 49 % as costs fall. The floating system alone is then about €0.39 per watt, the top of the earlier €0.30–0.40 band. NREL's benchmark for calm-water floats falls from USD 0.40 per watt at 2 MW to 0.30 at 10 MW and 0.20 at 50 MW, so prices for large plants will be lower.
- Engineering: about 4 % of plant cost for kit buyers; 8–12 % where Sunlit Sea also does the development work.
- EPC in phase 1: €0.70–0.73 per watt for the delivered plant, a margin of about 12–14 % after a first-of-a-kind contingency.
- PPAs on own plants: priced against what the host pays today for power delivered at its site. In 2025, Norwegian service industries paid on average 62.5 øre/kWh (about €0.053/kWh) and power-intensive industry 42 øre/kWh (about €0.036/kWh) for electricity, both before grid tariff and taxes (Statistics Norway). A plant behind the meter also saves grid tariff and avoids waiting for a grid connection. First sites are chosen in southern Norway, where power prices are highest, and with hosts that need more power than their grid connection can deliver.
- Prices differ by market, depending on site conditions and the local price of power.
- A first-of-a-kind 3.2 MWp plant in southern Norway produces power at about €0.092 per kWh. Matching the host's value of power behind the meter, about €0.072 per kWh at Skien havn, needs investment support of about a quarter of the plant cost; the plant companies seek this from Enova.

### Market segment and early adopters

The first segment is coastal industry and ports in Norway because:

- land conflicts, grid queues and growing industrial power demand create the need;
- the sites are sheltered (fjords and industrial basins), so the product works without a costly breakwater;
- Sunlit Sea knows the permitting process and has local partners;
- Enova and municipal support lower the risk of the first plants.

### Branding and reputation

Sunlit Sea CONNECT is the product brand. Its reputation is built on evidence: measured performance from the reference plants, the DNV design-basis assessment, the results of SUREWAVE and SuRE, and a visible presence in the European floating solar community.
