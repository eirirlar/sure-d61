## 4. KER1 definitive business plan

### 4.1 The result and the product

KER1 is the SUREWAVE global solution: floating PV for coastal and offshore waters, protected by a floating breakwater where the waves require it. It integrates Sunlit Sea's floating PV array (D3.1 and the global system design D3.4, both led by Sunlit Sea) with Clement Germany's breakwater (D3.2), transition element, mooring and anchoring (D3.3; KER3), the mooring optimised by MARIN (D5.1), and the electrical system of the array. Integrating these components in KER1 transfers no ownership of them (Section 7.1); the confirmation of contributions that the IPR management plan D1.8 asks for is set out in Section 7.4.

The open-sea plant studied in the project, called the D7.4 reference plant in this plan, is an array inside a ring breakwater 346 m across. Clement Germany's D3.3 set the plant at 10 MWp, the minimum plant size of the proposal's cost-of-energy calculations. The economic assessment D7.4 uses modules of about 730 Wp, a change it attributes to the new hinge system: its 10 MWp plant has 10,375 kWp installed (10.4 MWp), and its investment figures refer to that capacity. This plan uses D7.4 version 2.0 (November 2026). The technical brochure D8.3, led by Sunlit Sea, gives 7 MWp from the D3.4 layout and the economics of D7.4 version 1.0. It also gives the hinge as "about three times as strong" (a complete first-generation hinge against five one-fifth segments held by a rope, SINTEF's scaling in its test report D6.3), the 50-year wave heights of the use-case basis D2.1 instead of D5.1's design sea states (up to 4.0 m in the Baltic Sea, 6.5 m in the Mediterranean, 11 m in the North Sea), the basin model as a model of the integrated system, up to 56 % lighter chains, and the Stadt Towing Tank tests as a SUREWAVE campaign. This plan follows the sources named below and in Section 6.5.

Maturity at the end of the SUREWAVE project:

- The proposal aimed at TRL 4–5 for the system at project end, from TRL 2–3 at the start (Annex 1), and set TRL 5 for its result R1, the complete system validated in a relevant environment through laboratory tests, basin validation and long-term exposure at sea (Section 3.4).
- SINTEF's technological risk assessment (D7.2, led by SINTEF; Preprints 202611.1173) rates the integrated SUREWAVE concept at TRL 5, for the system with the redesigned hinge, and IFEU's integrated sustainability assessment D7.6 (November 2025) adopts the rating. It rests on the component tests at SINTEF and MARIN's two basin campaigns with a 1:10 model of a 3 × 5 module array behind one breakwater section spanning the basin; the ring was only simulated (D5.1; Energies 17(9) 2059; Energies 19(7) 1609).
- The EIC Transition proposal (September 2026) sets TRL 4, technology validated in the laboratory, as the starting point for the protected floating PV system it develops further, and records that long-term, grid-connected operation at a real site has not been demonstrated.
- This plan uses TRL 4–5 for the integrated system, since the integrated system with the array has not yet been at sea; the breakwater prototype at Gijón was exposed without the array (Section 6.2). This meets the proposal's TRL 4–5 for the system; R1's TRL 5 is met for the laboratory and basin elements, not yet for long-term exposure of the integrated system.
- Sunlit Sea rates its CONNECT platform at TRL 4–5 (Horizon Results Booster business plan, November 2026); prototype 4 is in casting. The breakwater classes of Section 4.1.1 and a directional layout at plant scale have not been tested.

Sunlit Sea's commercial product from KER1 is the Generation 2 floating platform, Sunlit Sea CONNECT: cast polyurethane floats, each carrying one standard panel, joined along their full edge by flexible hinges, first for sheltered sites and in an exposed-site version, under development, for sites behind a directional breakwater. The SUREWAVE array used Sunlit Sea's Generation 1 floats of pressed marine-grade aluminium (D3.1), and D7.4 and D7.6 assessed the array with them. Sunlit Sea started the Generation 2 redesign in SUREWAVE, from its hinge tests and models, and continues it in the parallel project SuRE (Section 10). The system concept, the breakwater, the mooring and the structure of the economics carry over to CONNECT; the float material and its cost do not. The polyurethane float's cost enters through the financial plan (Section 4.7); its life-cycle assessment has not been made.

This plan calls the hinge tested before the redesign the first generation, the paired corner hinges the second and the full-edge hinge the third (v2 and v3 in D7.2 and D7.6). Each figure is named with what it applies to:

- First generation: complete hinges reached a mean tensile load of 3.69 kN in SINTEF's tests and buckled at low compressive loads (D6.1), against 8–10 kN expected from the WP5 simulations, and buckled in harsh seas in MARIN's first basin campaign.
- Third generation, tested as a segment (D6.3): a segment of one fifth of Sunlit Sea's redesigned full-edge connection reached a mean tensile load of 2.04 kN with the polypropylene rope that holds the two halves together and 8.36 kN with a steel rod in place of the rope. D6.3 scales this to about 10 kN for the full connection with the rope and about 42 kN with a steel rod, recommends a stiffer holding element than the rope, and finds control of the production process critical, since the components had shrinkage gaps and early cracks.
- Second basin campaign: MARIN used paired second-generation connectors, longer and thicker than the first generation (Energies 19(7) 1609); the full-edge hinge was not in the basin model.
- Design values (D7.2, summarised in D7.6): the design break load of the full-width third-generation hinge at the ultimate limit state is 37 kN, against 14 kN for the paired second-generation hinges. The maximum simulated hinge loads in the 50-year sea states are 34 kN (Baltic) and 33 kN (Mediterranean), below the design value, and about 40 kN in the North Sea, about 8 % above it. D7.6 states that the robustness of the redesigned hinges could not be fully validated within the project. The simulated loads come from MARIN's linear diffraction model DIFFRAC, which MARIN validated on motions against Sunlit Sea's earlier Stadt Towing Tank tests, made outside SUREWAVE; hinge loads were not measured there (D5.1).

The configurations enter the market in order: the platform without breakwater at sheltered sites (Section 4.4), with a directional breakwater at exposed nearshore sites (Section 4.6.2), and the open-sea plant with ring breakwater, planned in the 2030s (Section 4.6.1).

#### 4.1.1 Breakwaters for nearshore sites

SUREWAVE designed one breakwater cross-section, Clement Germany's open-sea unit (D3.2). At nearshore and inland sites the waves are limited by the fetch, so they are lower and shorter and come from some directions only. Sunlit Sea has sized three breakwater classes for such sites, the two lighter ones scaled from the SUREWAVE unit with its design data and double-keel shape, using fetch-limited wave spectra, a 22 m/s design wind and the Macagno transmission formula of D3.2 and D5.1. The working limits at the array, Sunlit Sea's assumptions to be confirmed in the demonstrator, are at most 0.45 m significant height for waves of 3 s or less, the band D3.2 names as critical, and at most 0.6 m for all waves together (0.8 m for the exposed-site version). The classes are a sizing study, to be confirmed, engineered and supplied by Clement Germany, the owner of the breakwater design (KER3), on the terms in Section 5.

| Class | Site waves (design significant wave height) | Cross-section and status |
|---|---|---|
| Light | Up to about 0.65 m: lakes and inner waters | About 2.5 m wide, 1 m draft; desk sizing |
| Medium | Up to about 1.0 m | About 4 m wide, 1.5 m draft; desk sizing |
| Heavy | Up to about 1.3 m; needs the exposed-site version of CONNECT | The SUREWAVE unit (D3.2), tested at 1:10 by MARIN |

Above about 1.3 m, or where open-sea swell reaches the site, the full SUREWAVE open-sea design applies. Sunlit Sea's site-planning tool, built on the SUREWAVE nearshore wave and wind models, gives each site a wave class and an exposed sector, across which alone the breakwater is placed, and estimates the energy lost to wave motion. The breakwater cost per kilowatt-peak roughly halves from about 3 MWp to 10 MWp and is close to ten times the 10 MWp value at a few hundred kWp, so small plants need natural shelter; other uses of a breakwater, such as berths, can carry part of its cost. Near their natural periods, where the Macagno formula is least reliable, the classes may let more wave energy through than sized; this is to be checked with Clement Germany and in basin tests.

### 4.2 Organisation and objectives

Sunlit Sea AS, founded in 2019 in Oslo, has built two Generation 1 plants: a 60 kWp plant in commercial operation in Germany since 2023, and its own 105 kWp plant in Norway, which sold its power from 2024 to October 2026. The small core team (the chief executive officer for design and casting, the technical lead, Sunlit Sea's WP8 lead, for the site-planning tool, sales and projects) works with partners for structural simulation (IFE), module integration, sales in Germany, and local construction and permitting.

Objectives: in 2027–2028, freeze the CONNECT design, reach TRL 6 for sheltered sites through the first Norwegian reference plants, start a DNV design-basis assessment and prepare series production; in 2029–2032, sell CONNECT as a kit in the Nordic countries, Germany, the Baltic region and later the Mediterranean, win a first 10 MW-class kit supply contract by 2030, and extend the product to exposed sites.

### 4.3 Market

Floating solar reached at least 8.7 GW worldwide at the end of 2024, almost 90 % of it in Asia (International Energy Agency Photovoltaic Power Systems Programme, IEA PVPS, 2025). IEA PVPS rates offshore floating PV at TRL 3–5, and Sunlit Sea's review of 2024–2026 announcements found only pilots on European coastal water. Potential at sea is documented for Spain: 4.45–6.48 GW within the maritime spatial plans (University of A Coruña, 2026).

The first niche is plants of 0.3–10 MWp in sheltered coastal water next to industry and ports, with local demand and water but no land to spare; in Norway the energy regulator NVE expects grid scarcity and land-use conflict to limit ground-mounted solar before 2030 (NVE, February 2024). The segments are coastal industry and ports buying local, fixed-price power under long-term power purchase agreements, and developers and independent power producers buying a wave-tested kit with site engineering that lenders accept. A count of suitable sites per country is planned for early 2027.

The competitors at sea (Fred. Olsen 1848, Ocean Sun, SolarDuck, Oceans of Energy, BlueNewables) are few and at pilot stage, with first certificates and reviews from 2024–2026. Electrical reliability is the recurring failure at sea in Sunlit Sea's review, and the qualification programme covers it (Section 4.5). In Sunlit Sea's assessment, competition at sea is decided on references, independent review and the price per project.

#### 4.3.1 Value proposition testing

Six hypotheses were tested against evidence collected from 2023 to November 2026. Coastal industry's interest in local power on its own water is supported where power prices are high or the grid is constrained (three Norwegian hosts have committed to permits and site studies), and the first market is set to coastal industry in southern Norway. Acceptance by lenders on test results alone is not confirmed, which added the DNV assessment and the Norwegian reference plants before kit sales. Buyers' concern with wave-induced energy losses is indicated by questions at the Stakeholder Advisory Board webinar of November 2024. No developer has yet ordered a plant kit before reference plants exist, hence phase 1 (Section 4.4). At first-of-a-kind cost, the first plants need investment support of about a quarter of their cost. The open-sea ring plant is viable in the Mediterranean only (D7.4), so sheltered sites come first.

The customer interviews of early 2027 are the first formal test. The Horizon Results Booster expert (META Group) reviewed the KER1 characterisation, roadmap and risk assessment on 13 November 2026.

#### 4.3.2 Value innovation

In the four actions of value innovation (Kim and Mauborgne, 2005), and against calm-water floating PV and open-sea platforms, KER1 raises wave tolerance, reduces the structure against waves to the exposed sides only, eliminates specialist vessels and steelwork for the platform (ordinary quays, standard panels, cast floats) and creates site-specific design through the site-planning tool. References are still to be raised (Section 4.5).

#### 4.3.3 Sustainability evidence

IFEU's D7.6 finds the open-sea SUREWAVE plant of about 10 MWp, in every sea scenario, more climate-friendly than coal power, with fewer fossil resources, a low land-use footprint and lower social supply-chain risks, but higher impacts than land-based PV or wind power; the Mediterranean is the most advantageous location. D7.6 assessed the aluminium floats of the SUREWAVE array; for nearshore sites and CONNECT's polyurethane float the evidence is indicative until assessed for those plants.

### 4.4 Business model and route to market

In phase 1 (2027–2029, with deliveries to 2031) Sunlit Sea is developer and engineering-procurement-construction (EPC) contractor for the Norwegian reference plants, to build the operating record lenders need. In phase 2 (mainly from 2030; first kits from 2028) it sells the floating system as a kit with site engineering to developers and contractors, without tying up capital in each plant.

The three Norwegian reference plants are at sheltered sites. The first, a port plant of a few hundred kWp, is owned and built by Sunlit Sea, with the investment decision in Q1 2027, operation from Q3 2027 and small wavebreakers on its exposed side. The second, a multi-megawatt plant in an industrial basin, is delivered as EPC contractor to the plant company, with a minority share, for operation in Q3 2028. The third, a multi-megawatt lake plant built in sections, follows rezoning, with the investment decision in 2029 and delivery in 2030–2031. None needs a breakwater class on current site data.

Revenue comes from delivered plants, kits priced per watt, site engineering, power from Sunlit Sea's own plant, and spares. Production is subcontracted: Sunlit Sea designs the parts, owns the moulds and controls quality. Where a site needs a breakwater class, Clement Germany designs and supplies it (Section 5). Channels grow from direct sales with local contractors to resellers and, from 2030, distributors or licensees; markets from Norway (2027) to Germany (2028), the Baltic region (2029) and the Mediterranean (after 2031).

#### 4.4.1 Value chain

Sunlit Sea's value chain analysis for the Horizon Results Booster maps nine steps of a CONNECT plant, from site screening to sale or exit. The SUREWAVE partners enter in qualification (SINTEF and MARIN tests), assembly (Clement Germany's breakwater classes) and operation (Ceit's monitoring, as an option). Its single points of failure (key people, a single casting vendor, one local contractor, plant financing, the wavebreaker supplier) are addressed in Sections 4.7 and 4.8. Most of the margin sits in design, the moulds and the kit, which Sunlit Sea keeps in both phases; delivery earns a thin margin and carries the most risk, and the EPC role is limited to the three Norwegian reference plants.

### 4.5 Development and qualification

CONNECT will be qualified before series production against the DNV floating solar standards of May 2026, DNV-ST-C108 (floats) and DNV-ST-E309 (station keeping), and the updated recommended practice DNV-RP-0584. The programme covers break load and fatigue of the hinge, with the holding element chosen in the prototype 4 tests; fatigue of the mooring; accelerated ultraviolet (UV) ageing of the polyurethane; casting process control; corrosion of the bottom plate; marine growth; marine electrical components; and field monitoring. Before the design freeze, in Q1 2027, Sunlit Sea requests access rights from SINTEF and MARIN and asks SINTEF, Ceit and MARIN to confirm the contributions to the hinge redesign, as the WP8 lead proposes (Sections 7.4 and 9). TRL 6 for sheltered sites is the WP8 lead's rating of one winter at the first Norwegian reference plant. The exposed-site version needs a demonstrator with a directional breakwater, planned in the EIC Transition proposal (Section 10) to take the protected system from TRL 4 to TRL 6.

#### 4.5.1 Regulatory framework, uptake barriers and replication

In Norway, solar plants below 10 MW need no national licence since 1 July 2025 and are approved by the municipality. Germany's Water Resources Act limits inland plants to artificial or heavily modified water, at most 15 % coverage and 40 m from the shore. For coastal sites in Germany, Sweden, the Baltic states and the Mediterranean, and for the open sea (maritime spatial plan, concession, environmental assessment), the routes are to be mapped per country before entry. Across markets the main barriers are bankability, permits before debt, grid capacity and capital for each plant. Replication rests on the kit model, the site-planning tool, distributors or licensees from 2030, and the step from sheltered to exposed sites and then to the open sea.

### 4.6 Economics of the D7.4 reference plant

D7.4, led by Sunlit Sea (version 2.0, 20 November 2026), covers 50 years of operation with PV replacement after 25 years, at an electricity price of €0.12/kWh, the target price of the SUREWAVE planning and application process, and a base discount rate of 8.5 %. It gives net present value (NPV), internal rate of return (IRR), levelised cost of energy (LCOE) and payback time.

| Site, 10 MWp plant (10.4 MWp installed) | NPV | IRR | LCOE | Payback |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Mediterranean | €7.3M | 15.6 % | €0.076/kWh | 10 years |
| Baltic Sea | about €0 | 8.5 % | €0.120/kWh | not reached |
| North Sea | −€5.8M | 3.4 % | €0.173/kWh | not reached |

Version 2.0 adds end-of-life clean-up and the scope limits, in reply to the project officer's request for revision of 16 October 2026, and corrects the errors of version 1.0 with a rebuilt, independently checked cost model. In the Mediterranean the LCOE rises from €0.062 to €0.076/kWh and the NPV falls from €8.9M to €7.3M; the IRR rises from 10.7 % to 15.6 % because it is now calculated on undiscounted cash flows over 50 years. The North Sea is viable only at discount rates of 3 % or lower, or with lower cost or shared infrastructure. The breakwater costs about €1.2M in conventional concrete, about 13 % of the investment.

#### 4.6.1 Business case for the open-sea plant

The open-sea plant is the global solution at the scale of D7.4: about 10 MWp inside a ring breakwater in the Mediterranean, which leads on solar yield and has lower maintenance cost than the Baltic Sea and the North Sea. The investment is about €9.75M for the 10,375 kWp installed, €940/kWp, with the conventional-concrete breakwater and a 300 m floating cable to an existing offshore substation; export cable, grid connection, development, permits, lease, insurance, financing and contingency are outside the figure. ACCIONA's circular mixes cost more (about 24 % with a lightweight aggregate concrete shell, 76 % with a high-performance concrete shell, which raises the LCOE to €0.085/kWh), and D7.4 finds the conventional breakwater preferable unless structural or regulatory constraints demand alternatives.

A utility or independent power producer owns the plant. Sunlit Sea supplies the floating PV system and integrates the plant (KER1); Clement Germany engineers, supplies and installs the breakwater with its mooring and anchoring (KER3), with ACCIONA's mixes (KER2) as an option; MARIN, SINTEF, Ceit and IFEU offer their services (KER4–KER7), each paid per project (Section 5). Sunlit Sea's planned path is the exposed-site field record in 2028–2030, an open-sea pilot proposal about 2031, the pilot in operation after about 2033 and commercial plants from about 2035, with the plant cost re-established from engineering quotes before the pilot investment decision. Its conditions are a price at or above €0.12/kWh or support of equal value, a cost of capital close to 8.5 % and a nearby grid connection point.

#### 4.6.2 First sites with breakwater protection

The first protection in operation will be the small wavebreakers of the first Norwegian reference plant in 2027, bought from a wavebreaker supplier rather than built from the SUREWAVE design. The first sites for the breakwater classes are expected to be lakes and inner waters exposed to wind waves from one direction, where the light class is enough. The EIC Transition demonstrator's baseline site is such a lake; the demonstrator would run in 2028–2030, with its breakwater funded by the grant, if the proposal is funded (Section 10). The proposal targets a first commercial installation of 1–5 MWp after the EIC Transition project, subject to the site owner, permits and finance; Sunlit Sea's financial plan models it in 2031. If Sunlit Sea joins a funded application under the call HORIZON-CL5-2026-11-D3-14 of the European Partnership for Innovation in Photovoltaics (EUPI-PV), a nearshore demonstration with a breakwater could come in 2029.

The breakwater's share of the plant cost falls steeply with plant size. At a demonstrator of 50–300 kWp, the size in Sunlit Sea's own planning input to the EIC Transition proposal, the breakwater costs from about as much as the PV plant to several times as much; at about 5 MWp its share is around a tenth, and at a few tens of MWp a few per cent, lower where other uses carry part of it. The estimate rests on D7.4 version 2.0's breakwater and anchoring costs plus Sunlit Sea's estimates for fabrication, fittings, transport, supplier margin and mobilisation, for a kit-buyer plant; it is indicative and to be confirmed by Clement Germany. The first commercial plant with a breakwater class serves as a reference; its cost of power is well above the average award in Germany's solar tender of July 2025, and it needs a buyer who values the power behind the meter, or investment support.

### 4.7 Financial plan

The financial plan for KER1, the annex to Sunlit Sea's Horizon Results Booster business plan (22 November 2026), projects Sunlit Sea's income statement, cash flow and balance sheet for 2027–2031 in four scenarios for public funding, with sensitivities on cost, price, volume and timing. This section gives company-level results, in relative terms where a euro amount combined with the volumes would reveal a price or a cost per watt; projects, customers, prices and costs are commercially sensitive and are not given. Sunlit Sea updates it after the Booster mentor's review and the first equity round, for adoption by its board in Q2 2027 (M2).

Volumes are built bottom-up from the pipeline: the three Norwegian reference plants, kit leads in Germany, Denmark and the Baltic states, and a first 10 MW-class kit supply contract assumed signed early in 2030. Revenue in 2030–2031 is about three and a half to four times the 2027–2029 total, mainly from kits. The gross margin is lowest in 2028 and 41–44 % in 2030–2031. EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortisation) is negative each year to 2029 and positive in 2030 and 2031 in all four scenarios. In the base case about 28 MWp is delivered over 2027–2031, about 7 MWp in 2030 and 16 MWp in 2031; 28 % of it is at sites with permits, host commitment or named leads, and 72 % is target volume not yet identified, including the 10 MW-class contract. The second equity round is conditional on a letter of intent for that contract.

The four scenarios differ only in public funding:

- Base: the extension of Sunlit Sea's scope in SuRE (about €0.4M of grant in 2027) is approved, and no new EU project is won. The CINEA decision on the extension is pending; its demonstrator scope is placed at the first Norwegian reference plant, with no cost funded twice. SuRE and SUREWAVE fund different work: SUREWAVE the integrated system up to 2026, SuRE the Generation 2 prototypes and, with the extension, their demonstration (Section 10).
- EIC: the base case plus the EIC Transition project for the exposed-site version, with a Sunlit Sea share of about €0.75M over 2027–2030 and a first commercial plant at the demonstrator site in 2031.
- EU upside: the EIC case plus the EUPI-PV nearshore demonstrator, if Sunlit Sea applies and it is funded (about €0.9M of grant-funded work over 2027–2030).
- Grant downside: no SuRE extension, or none for the first Norwegian reference plant, and no new EU project.

| Scenario | Net result, total 2027–2031 | Lowest year-end cash (year) |
|---|---:|---:|
| Base | −€1.4M | €0.89M (2028) |
| Grant downside | −€1.6M | €0.73M (2028) |

The EIC and EU-upside cases improve the net result and cash against the base case. The net result is negative through 2030 and positive from 2031. Without EPC deliveries, kit and engineering sales give a positive EBITDA from about 7 MWp of kits a year and cover all costs from about 9 MWp a year. The plan raises €5.5M of equity, €3.5M in Q1 2027 and €2.0M in the second half of 2029; the base case uses a small part of the second round, the rest being a buffer. Plants are financed at plant level, except Sunlit Sea's own first plant and its minority share in the second. The indicative outlook has kit sales of 25–45 MWp a year in 2033–2035.

The plan has no breakwater class in its revenue or costs, since kit buyers buy any breakwater as plant owners; the small wavebreakers of the first plant, not yet quoted, are tested at about €0.1M.

Conditions and downside cases:

- The plan meets its kit gross margin target at today's kit price and production cost.
- One-year delay of the Norwegian reference plants and kit sales: the largest single effect; 2031 EBITDA slightly negative and lowest year-end cash about −€0.3M; about €0.5M more equity keeps a buffer of €0.2M, and the second round moves to 2030.
- 10 MW-class contract not won: 2031 EBITDA and cash stay positive; the net result is lower.
- Kit volume 30 % lower in 2030–2031, or a kit price a few per cent lower, or a kit cost higher by the same amount: 2031 EBITDA lower but positive; cash positive.
- Combined downside (one year later, volume 30 % lower, no further cost reduction): negative net result every year and negative 2031 EBITDA; about €1.0M more equity, or the 2029–2031 hires and the second mould set held back.

Hiring and investment are tied to four decision gates: the first equity round (Q1 2027), the investment decision at the first multi-megawatt site (Q4 2027), the first plant's first winter within design values (Q2 2028) and a year of operation of the multi-megawatt plant with a letter of intent for the 10 MW-class contract (2029).
