## 5. Results that feed into KER1: roles, agreements and fair price

The proposal foresaw that partners whose results contribute to the global solution, then a 50 MWp open-sea plant, would be compensated through licences or royalties. The first commercial product is the platform for sheltered sites. It is built on Sunlit Sea's own designs and on Sunlit Sea's nearshore wave and wind models, developed in SUREWAVE from ERA5 and GEBCO data. From the first commercial site that needs one of the breakwater classes (Section 4.6.2), the breakwater is part of KER1. In the EIC Transition demonstrator, if the project is funded, the breakwater would be built within that project, under its grant and consortium agreements (Section 10). The small wavebreakers of the first Norwegian reference plant are part of that plant's cost (Section 4.7).

SINTEF's connection tests and MARIN's basin tests informed the hinge redesign. D1.8 names Sunlit Sea as the principal generating partner of the modified hinge designs and asks the partners concerned to confirm the contributions before any patent, design registration, licence or commercial deployment; the WP8 lead proposes this confirmation for Q1 2027 (Section 7.4). Sunlit Sea's use of the partners' test results after the SUREWAVE project requires access rights under the Grant Agreement and the Consortium Agreement, which it requests as set out in Section 9.

The partner results reach KER1 in three ways:

- The breakwater (KER3, with KER2 materials) is a component of KER1 from the first commercial site that needs a breakwater class. This covers the light and medium classes for inland and nearshore sites as well as the exposed-site and open-sea configurations. Clement Germany designed the breakwater and its mooring and anchoring (D3.2, D3.3), and engineers, supplies and installs it. The supply route depends on the project. The plant owner buys the breakwater from Clement Germany; where Sunlit Sea delivers the full system as EPC contractor, Sunlit Sea buys it and includes it in the EPC price; and where Clement Germany leads a project, Sunlit Sea supplies the floating PV system as its subcontractor. If Sunlit Sea or a third party were to build a breakwater from Clement Germany's design, a licence on fair and reasonable conditions, which may include royalties, would be agreed under the Consortium Agreement.
- Testing, modelling, monitoring and sustainability results reach KER1 as services, contracted per campaign or project.
- If a partner's model or tool were embedded in Sunlit Sea's site-planning tool, a licence would be agreed in the same way.

The WP8 lead proposes that each owner is paid where its result is used, for that use, and that the kit price carries no general partner charge. Each owner keeps the right to exploit its result outside floating PV. The basis differs by type of use. Services such as testing, modelling and studies are priced per campaign at the owner's market rates. Supplied components such as the breakwater are priced per project on cost and market benchmarks. A licence to build or embed a partner's design or tool is priced as a share of the value it creates, as a royalty or a fixed fee. These bases apply the Grant Agreement's definition of fair and reasonable conditions: appropriate conditions, including possible financial terms or royalty-free conditions, that take into account the actual or potential value of the results or background and the scope, duration or other characteristics of the exploitation. The terms for the access rights to the test results used in the hinge redesign are set when Sunlit Sea requests them (Section 9). Prices and detailed terms would be set in the bilateral agreements.

Sunlit Sea reported six results:

- Nearshore wave and wind modelling framework. It combines data on waves, wind, bathymetry and the land–water boundary to estimate how wave energy is distributed near the shore. It draws on the offshore datasets ERA5 and GEBCO (Section 4.1.1) and couples nearshore and offshore modelling at the interface zone. It is built into Sunlit Sea's web-based site-planning tool, where users evaluate wave and wind conditions along the full perimeter of a proposed plant. The site-planning tool uses this to place breakwaters and other protective elements.
- Wave-corrected performance ratio. With IFE, Sunlit Sea used the SUREWAVE nearshore models in the national MariSol project (Research Council of Norway, 2020–2025) to calculate a performance ratio that combines module tilt and rotation, solar irradiance, electrical configuration and the losses from wave-induced motion. The method has been used in research and is now a core part of Sunlit Sea's assessment of floating PV performance under marine conditions.
- Wave-loss module. The performance ratio was developed into an interactive module of the site-planning tool. Users simulate a floating PV configuration and see the expected energy loss from waves under different site conditions, early in the design.
- Generation 2 floating platform. Laboratory and wave-basin tests in SUREWAVE showed that the Generation 1 platform, square aluminium floats joined by polyurethane hinges, took excessive hinge loads, including snapping loads, which led to fatigue and possible failure of hinges and brackets. Sunlit Sea redesigned the hinge and the product architecture. Several prototypes were CAD-modelled, 3D-printed and cast in polyurethane.

  Sunlit Sea reports that hinges distributed along the full edge of the floats improved load capacity and durability. The inputs to the redesign are in Section 3.1, and the hinge record, from the first-generation tests to SINTEF's tests of a segment of the third generation, is in Section 4.1. Sunlit Sea continues the Generation 2 redesign in the parallel Horizon Europe project SuRE and commercialises the platform as Sunlit Sea CONNECT (Section 4.1). Sunlit Sea expects the new design to be more robust and lower in cost than Generation 1.
- Network. As WP8 lead, Sunlit Sea built a network of stakeholders, research partners and industrial contacts. SuRE, coordinated by IFE, grew out of contacts made through the Stakeholder Advisory Board that Sunlit Sea ran: Sunlit Sea reports that it initiated SuRE in partnership with a board member, and that another board member works with it in several joint initiatives (Section 10; Annex A.10).
- Visibility. Sunlit Sea reports that the project website (surewave.eu) and the SUREWAVE LinkedIn page raised traffic to its own channels and brought contacts with new partners, customers and suppliers.

The modelling framework and the wave-loss module are in use in the site-planning tool. ### 6.2 ACCIONA (KER2)

Value proposition: circular concrete mixes for marine structures: a cellular concrete that gives floating structures a mineral core in place of expanded polystyrene (CLC), a lighter structural concrete with recycled glass aggregate (LWAC), and a stronger concrete with high resistance to water and chloride ingress that allows thinner shells (HPC).

Market: the proposal names international contractors of large infrastructure projects; ACCIONA names its own ongoing and future construction projects and the clients of its consultancy.

ACCIONA led WP4, New circular material solutions, with the material design and characterisation (D4.1), the laboratory tests of the breakwater materials (D4.2) and the reduced-scale breakwater prototype (D4.3). It reported three results:

- Circular cellular lightweight concrete (CLC) for non-structural use: density 365 kg/m³ with 25 kg of cement per cubic metre. Recycled lightweight aggregate from glass fully replaces the expanded clay aggregate. ACCIONA positions it as the inner core of floating breakwaters, in place of the expanded polystyrene of conventional pontoon breakwaters, which it describes as costly. Clement Germany set the requirement for the core concrete at a density of about 300 kg/m³; ACCIONA cast 26 trial mixes aiming at 300–400 kg/m³ (D4.1, ACCIONA).
- Circular lightweight aggregate concrete (LWAC) for structural use: density 1,950 kg/m³ against 2,400 kg/m³ for normal-weight concrete, compressive strength 68 MPa at 28 days. Recycled glass aggregate replaces the conventional lightweight aggregate, and a cement that ACCIONA describes as low-carbon, a CEM III cement, replaces standard cement (CEM I). Recycled aggregate from construction and demolition waste also replaces 20 % of the natural coarse aggregate (D7.3). The mix was developed against both mechanical and durability requirements, as a lighter alternative for the structural shell of the breakwater. D4.1 set the LWAC requirement at a strength of at least 45 MPa and a density of at most 1,900 kg/m³. It selected Sample 17 (49 MPa, 1,898 kg/m³) as the optimised mix and reports that Sample 18, the same mix with the CEM III cement, reached 68 MPa at a density near 2,000 kg/m³. The prototype's top slab used Sample 18 (D4.3). This plan gives ACCIONA's figures for that mix.
- Circular high-performance concrete (HPC) for structural use: compressive strength 128 MPa at 28 days, against a requirement of 120 MPa (D4.1). D4.1's mix uses 400 kg/m³ of cement instead of 500, with fly ash and slag (ACCIONA). Its strength allows a thinner breakwater shell.

D4.1 records the development: eight reference and ten circular LWAC trial mixes, with recycled concrete aggregate, steel slag and recycled glass aggregate in place of natural aggregates; a reference and a circular HPC mix; and the 26 cellular-concrete trial mixes.

In the laboratory tests of D4.2 (ACCIONA), no water penetrated the HPC. A crystalline waterproofing admixture, part of all the optimal shell mixes, reduced the mean water penetration of the LWAC with CEM III cement from 8.2 mm to 0.6 mm. The chloride-migration coefficients of the shell mixes were 0.4–0.9 × 10⁻¹² m²/s, which D4.2 classes as extremely high resistance to chloride penetration (D4.2, Table 5).

SINTEF tested shell–core beams cast by ACCIONA from the HPC and LWAC (Section 6.4), among them beams that ACCIONA had first subjected to a month of thermal cycles between −15 °C and +60 °C in the climate chamber of its R&D facilities. In static three-point bending, HPC was more than 55 % stronger than LWAC and about 50 % better in strength-to-weight ratio. Thermal cycling had a negligible effect on the beams, and both mixes dissipated impact energy through cracking in the drop-tower tests. The low pull-out values came from the cellular core, which acts as a filler and carries no structural load, not from the interfaces. D4.2 concludes that HPC is the most suitable material for the shell (D4.2, ACCIONA, with the mechanical tests by SINTEF).

At breakwater level, the environmental assessment finds no relevant greenhouse-gas saving from the circular mixes against a conventional breakwater with a concrete shell and an expanded polystyrene core. In the shell, the cement content needed for the strength is similar, and the steel reinforcement makes up about half of the shell's footprint. In the core, the emissions of the recycled-glass aggregate, lime and aluminium powder balance those of the polystyrene. D7.3 notes that the mixes contain substantial amounts of by-products from other industrial processes, and that their circularity was analysed in the technological assessment (D7.3, IFEU).

In the economic assessment, both breakwater variants in ACCIONA's mixes cost more than the conventional breakwater; D7.4's conclusion is in Section 4.6.1.

ACCIONA designed and cast the reduced-scale breakwater prototype, with SINTEF and Clement Germany as contributing beneficiaries and with mixes developed to production requirements set by Clement Germany (D4.3). ACCIONA deployed it at its R&D maritime demonstration site in the Port of Gijón and exposed it for about nine months, on a four-line catenary mooring with gravity anchors. The exposure is Task 6.5 of the technical validation work package, led by ACCIONA with Ceit, SINTEF and Clement Germany as partners. Its results are reported in a joint preprint by ACCIONA, SINTEF and Ceit (Preprints 202611.1592, November 2026), from which the figures below are taken. The exposure is the subject of ACCIONA's public deliverable D6.4, Marine long-term degradation of concrete breakwater.

The last extension of the project, by three months to 31 December 2026, gives time to complete this exposure and its reporting. The prototype measures 3.40 × 1.70 × 1.05 m. It has HPC in the bottom slab and walls, LWAC in the top slab, and a core of cellular concrete with fully encapsulated expanded polystyrene, which the buoyancy of the reduced scale required.

Ceit monitored the prototype. The joint preprint reports that biofouling covered more than 85 % of every evaluated surface, with mussel layers up to 35 mm thick. Chloride exceeded the 0.6 % code limit at reinforcement depth only in the LWAC top slab, near the openings made for monitoring equipment; possible local pitting was seen there, but no measurable loss of reinforcement thickness. The authors judge the short-term marine performance satisfactory and identify the openings made for instrumentation as the most critical durability details. They call for longer exposure under open-sea conditions to assess durability over the life cycle, corrosion development and the long-term effect of biofouling on the hydrodynamic response and mooring loads.

Route: integration of the three mixes into ACCIONA's ongoing and future construction projects; further research to enhance their properties, widen their applications and adapt them to different project requirements, with academic institutions, research organisations and industry partners; and consultancy on sustainable and innovative construction, based on the expertise gained. ACCIONA's mid-term plan in D8.5 also named certification of the mixes against international sustainability standards. The final summary does not name certification, and this plan includes no certification action.

### 6.4 SINTEF (KER4, KER5)

Value proposition: modelling, testing and qualification methods for floating structures, flexible connections and circular concrete, developed and applied on the SUREWAVE designs and test campaigns.

Market: for Surewave-ANN, the proposal names concrete component manufacturers for civil and energy infrastructure; SINTEF names floating PV manufacturers, developers of flexible marine structures, industrial partners working with circular concrete, and public and private research funders.

SINTEF's work in SUREWAVE. SINTEF coordinated the project (WP1), including the IPR management with the IPR management plan D1.8 (Task 1.4) and the synergy-building and clustering with other EU projects (Task 1.5). It led the use-case definition (D2.1), which set the three reference sites and their design conditions, used in the anchoring design (D3.3) and the coupled analysis (D5.1). It led WP6, the technical validation, with the ultimate-strength and fatigue tests of the critical connections (D6.1, D6.3). It led the durability assessment of the interface between shell and core (Task 4.3): it tested 25 HPC and 23 LWAC shell–core beams cast by ACCIONA in static and cyclic three-point bending, pull-out of the interface and impact (D4.2, ACCIONA), and the bending tests calibrated Ceit's concrete model. It led the concrete-property modelling with the Surewave-ANN tool (D5.2), the sustainability framework task with the mass and energy balances (Task 7.1), whose definitions and settings IFEU reported in D7.1, and the technological risk assessment (D7.2). It co-authored the analysis of the Gijón exposure (Preprints 202611.1592).

SINTEF reported six results; the aerodynamic and the hydrodynamic models are described together:

- Aerodynamic and hydrodynamic modelling. An aerodynamic method assesses wind loads on floating PV and the shielding effect of floating breakwaters. From CFD simulations of panel configurations, SINTEF derived correlations for the lift and drag forces as a function of the number of panels, for the design of mooring lines and connections. In the same simulations, a breakwater substantially reduced the lift and drag coefficients of the array for angles of attack up to 10°, with less effect at larger angles for a 2 m breakwater (Energies 17(19) 4873, 2024). High-fidelity hydrodynamic methods assess wave loading, structural response and breakwater performance under representative offshore conditions. SINTEF's smoothed-particle hydrodynamics model of a moored, keeled breakwater, built on the open-source solver DualSPHysics, was assessed against wave-basin experiments (Preprints 202610.0011).
- Finite element analysis of concrete cracking and damage: crack and damage initiation and development, and the structural response of concrete components, used to interpret the experimental results.
- The Surewave-ANN concrete-modelling platform, which predicts concrete properties from mix composition and test data for faster screening of circular and lightweight concrete mixes. It complements laboratory testing and empirical methods.
- Concrete testing with digital image correlation (DIC), developed further to measure deformation, local strain fields and crack initiation and propagation in concrete.
- Mechanical and fatigue testing of floating PV connections. The test arrangements and procedures cover the ultimate strength and fatigue of hinges, ropes and related components: specimen design, loading arrangements, instrumentation, DIC, failure assessment, data processing and interpretation. The steel-wire-rope assemblies between breakwater units, the connection type Clement Germany uses (D6.1), reached a mean of 402 kN against a rated strength of 450 kN, which D6.1 finds in agreement; four of five failures were at the terminations. The connections between breakwater and array, designed by Clement Germany to the requirement of D3.3, reached a mean of 40.88 kN against a design requirement of 42 kN. D6.1 finds this aligned with the requirement; the later preprint gives the mean as about 2.7 % below it (Preprints 202611.1612). The first two specimens failed at the interface between rope and bracket at about 38 kN. The two sources differ on which tests had that interface protected. D6.1 places a protective fabric band at the interface from specimen 4, the third valid test, which covers the last three results (41.1, 43.7 and 42.9 kN); the preprint names the last two (43.7 and 42.9 kN) as the protected ones. SINTEF's tests of the first-generation hinge and of the redesigned hinge connection (D6.1, D6.3) are given in Section 4.1; they supported the development of the third generation of Sunlit Sea's hinge.

Reuse during the project: the connection-testing methods in the Research Council of Norway centre SFI BLUES; the fatigue-testing setup in the national project FlexiShape (2021–2025), for aramid membrane materials; and the finite element methods for concrete cracking and damage in the Horizon Europe project CIRCWIND (2024–2028), where reuse is continuing. SINTEF plans to reuse its hydrodynamic modelling in CIRCWIND and in a national project with GEOPON.

Route: continued development and application of the results through research projects, contract research, modelling and engineering services, specialised laboratory testing, industrial collaboration and scientific publication.

### 6.5 MARIN (KER5)

Value proposition: hydrodynamic software, mooring optimisation and basin testing that support floating PV developers in scaling up their designs, from concept to operation.

Market: the proposal names offshore energy, including installation and maintenance vessels, and floating PV, floating wind and floating islands; MARIN names floating PV developers at every design stage.

MARIN reported three results.

Result 1, scaled-up DIFFRAC. In SUREWAVE, MARIN extended its wave diffraction solver DIFFRAC so that the number of floating bodies it can model rose from about 100 to nearly 4,000. The solver is used in MARIN's research and commercial studies and made available to licence subscribers. MARIN applied it to a 2 MWp farm of 3,660 modules inside a ring of 32 breakwaters. In steep irregular seas the ring reduced the spatially averaged axial connector loads by up to 20 % and the vertical loads and overturning moments on the module connectors by up to 50 %, with local increases from horizontal module motions and from standing waves inside the ring. The paper gives recommendations for the farm layout to reduce fatigue and survival loads on modules and connectors (Energies 19(7) 1609, 2026).

MARIN validated DIFFRAC twice. In the same paper, it compared the model with the motions of the modules and the breakwater measured in the second SUREWAVE basin campaign (Energies 19(7) 1609). In D5.1, it validated DIFFRAC against Sunlit Sea's earlier tests of 1×3, 1×6 and 1×9 module arrays at the Stadt Towing Tank in Norway. These tests were carried out outside SUREWAVE (Section 10), and Sunlit Sea made the results available to the project. MARIN computed the 1×3 and 1×6 arrays: the heave and pitch motions were generally similar to the measured ones, with some differences (heave over-predicted in short waves, pitch in long waves), and D5.1 concludes that DIFFRAC reproduces the general motion behaviour, including the shielding by the forward floats, while the computed hinge loads and the motions in large fields carry high uncertainty. Hinge loads were not measured, so they could not be validated (Section 4.1).

In the D5.1 simulations the breakwater was most effective in sea states with peak periods below 5 s, and D5.1 describes it as highly effective in reducing the vertical hinge loads. It reduced the horizontal hinge loads by about a factor of 3 at peak periods around 4 s, but by less than 50 % for the largest farm in the highest sea states. MARIN also used its CFD code ReFRESCO for the modules in high waves and for the viscous damping of the breakwater. 3D CFD in severe sea states proved too demanding computationally and lacked robustness in steep and breaking waves, and the analyses used efficient linear methods for all sea states, adjusted with safety factors (D5.1). The modelling results were presented at conferences and in publications.

Result 2, mooring optimisation with surrogate models and genetic algorithms. In Task 5.2, MARIN used genetic-algorithm optimisation for the first time, by coupling the optimisation and surrogate-modelling software Dakota to its time-domain software, to optimise the mooring of the SUREWAVE breakwater ring. D5.1 covers two ring sizes, the 10 MWp ring of 56 breakwaters and a 2 MWp ring of 32. At the Mediterranean and North Sea sites the reference moorings were conservative. Without exceeding proof loads, the optimised moorings have 34 % (Mediterranean) and 42 % (North Sea) less mooring-line mass for the 10 MWp ring, and 56 % and 59 % less for the 2 MWp ring (D5.1, Table 9; the D5.1 summary gives 34–56 % and nearly 60 % for the 2 MWp North Sea ring). At the Baltic site the reference design exceeded proof loads, and the optimised mooring that keeps within them is about 3 times (10 MWp) and 2 times (2 MWp) heavier. D5.1 takes mooring-line mass as the measure of mooring cost. Most of the lighter moorings carry higher maximum tensions at the fairlead, up to about 2.5 times the reference value; the 10 MWp North Sea mooring is lighter at an unchanged maximum tension (D5.1, Table 9). D5.1 presents its optima as possible solutions on the Pareto front of mooring mass and fairlead tension: after consultation with Clement Germany, the weighting between the two was left open. MARIN's summary expresses the reduction as a ratio of mooring-chain mass; the largest reductions in D5.1, 56 % and 59 % for the 2 MWp ring, correspond to a ratio of about 2.3–2.4.

Result 3, basin testing of floating PV with elastically scaled hinges. MARIN tested the floating PV system and the floating breakwater in Task 6.4 at several design stages, in two campaigns with a 1:10 model of a module array behind a breakwater section (Section 4.1). The connectors of the first campaign lacked compressive stiffness and buckled frequently; the second campaign used the paired second-generation connectors, and the hinge record is in Section 4.1. The elastic scaling of the polyurethane hinges worked, and the insights into hinge performance were used by Sunlit Sea to re-evaluate and improve its design. MARIN reports the basin tests in D6.2, which is public on Zenodo, with the test data in a Zenodo dataset (doi 10.5281/zenodo.10960431, April 2024). The method and the results of the first campaign were published in Energies 17(9) 2059 (2024), led by MARIN with co-authors from SINTEF, Clement Germany, Ceit and Sunlit Sea.

Route: DIFFRAC licences and studies; mooring assessment and improvement, a MARIN service for existing and new markets, where the SUREWAVE results show that optimisation adds value; and basin testing for floating PV developers at every design stage, which helps build best practice for this market.

### 6.6 Ceit (KER6)

Value proposition: monitoring and structural health management tailored to the client's structures and components and, under investigation, earlier detection of corrosion damage in reinforced concrete.

Market: the proposal names floating PV plant operators, project developers and component manufacturers in the EU; Ceit names industrial end users and technology integrators in aeronautics and in civil and marine reinforced-concrete monitoring, and industrial partners for co-developed SHMS tools.

Ceit led WP5, the predictive modelling and simulation tools for material properties and structural integrity management, with its deliverables D5.3 to D5.7 (the structural integrity assessment, the monitoring strategy, the fatigue crack propagation algorithms, the integrated structural health management system and the validation report of the modelling tools), and the sensing and monitoring task of the technical validation work package (Task 6.1).

Result 1, eddy-current monitoring technology. Ceit plans to transfer the eddy-current technology to two industrial applications. In aeronautics, it will be validated for detecting air gaps in metal–fibre and fibre–fibre assemblies, for the inspection of bonded and multilayer components. In civil and marine reinforced concrete, Ceit will investigate combining eddy-current data with electrochemical sensor measurements to monitor corrosion, with fuller information on rebar condition and earlier detection of corrosion damage. The routes are industrial validation, adaptation to application-specific requirements and collaboration with end users and technology integrators.

In SUREWAVE, Ceit developed the eddy-current corrosion sensor, designed to assess a rebar from a distance of up to 5–6 cm, with the sensor outside the concrete. In the laboratory it quantified material loss around a rebar to about 0.17 mm (D5.4, Ceit, which calls this the resolution; Sensors 24(13) 4211, 2024, which calls it the accuracy). Ceit's wireless accelerometer nodes, based on ultra-wideband (UWB) radio, were checked against a wired reference system in the laboratory and assessed in MARIN's SUREWAVE wave-basin tests (Electronics 13(22) 4485, 2024, with MARIN).

Ceit provided the corrosion and acceleration monitoring on the breakwater prototype in the Port of Gijón, whose data it analysed over the exposure (Section 6.2). Environmental compensation reduced the variability of the eddy-current baseline to a band of ±135 Ω; field detection of active corrosion was not demonstrated, and a long-term corrosion trend has not yet been established. The accelerometers identified dominant structural-response frequencies of about 0.35–0.45 Hz, broadly consistent with the calculated rigid-body natural frequencies (Preprints 202611.1592).

Result 2, structural health monitoring technologies and the structural health management system (SHMS). Ceit exploits it on two routes. On Route 1, contract R&D and consultancy, Ceit applies methods used and extended in SUREWAVE, which build on its existing practice: finite element modelling of large structures, interpretation of complex loading schemes and advanced crack-propagation analysis. On Route 2, customised SHMS tools, Ceit uses the logic and architecture of the SUREWAVE SHMS demonstrator as the framework for co-developing tools with industrial partners. These are adapted to the client's components, monitoring systems, data and structural health criteria, with additional R&D by Ceit as needed. Both routes run through research contracts, joint technology adaptation and implementation with industrial end users.

The SUREWAVE SHMS demonstrator (D5.6, Ceit) combines Ceit's finite element models of the breakwater with fatigue-crack propagation and the corrosion-sensor data, estimates whether the structure reaches its design life and suggests routine and exceptional inspection times. Its inputs are Clement Germany's breakwater geometry, MARIN's load histories and Ceit's concrete model, calibrated on SINTEF's bending tests of ACCIONA's mixes. D5.7 (Ceit) benchmarked the finite element models against the guidance of the classification societies ABS and DNV, compared the inspection planning of the SHMS with the ABS guidance, calibrated the concrete model on WP4 and WP6 test data, used the WP6 corrosion-sensor data in the crack-propagation simulations, and used the second basin campaign to confirm that the effect of slamming loads on the breakwater is negligible. Validation with long-term field data from the prototype is further work (D5.7).

### 6.8 Horizon Results Platform

The Grant Agreement requires that, if despite a beneficiary's best efforts its results are not exploited within one year after the end of the action, by 31 December 2027, the beneficiaries use the Horizon Results Platform to find interested parties, unless otherwise agreed in writing with the granting authority. The Grant Agreement sets no duration for this use.

The WP8 lead proposes to apply the rule to each result, not only to each KER, since a KER can be exploited while one of its results is not, and to keep a result on the platform until it is exploited or the four-year exploitation period ends on 31 December 2030. It proposes this procedure. At the first annual exploitation review, by the end of Q3 2027, each beneficiary reports for each of its results whether it is being exploited. A result counts as exploited when it is used after the SUREWAVE project in a project, contract, licence, product, service or follow-on research, with evidence. A result without such use by 31 December 2027 is entered on the Horizon Results Platform by its owner in Q1 2028. Sunlit Sea uploads the KER1 profile in Q1 2027, to reach partners and investors.
