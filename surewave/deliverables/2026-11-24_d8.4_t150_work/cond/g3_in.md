## 4. KER1 definitive business plan

### 4.1 The result and the product

KER1 is the SUREWAVE global solution: floating PV for coastal and offshore waters, protected by a floating breakwater where the waves require it. It integrates Sunlit Sea's floating PV array with Clement Germany's breakwater, transition element, mooring and anchoring (KER3):

- the internal FPV array of floats joined by flexible connections, designed by Sunlit Sea (D3.1 and the global system design D3.4, both led by Sunlit Sea);
- the external protective breakwater, designed by Clement Germany (D3.2);
- the transition element, a floating pipe of 219 mm diameter between breakwater and array, connected to both with pre-tensioned polypropylene ropes, designed by Clement Germany (D3.3);
- the connections between the breakwater units, and the anchoring and mooring, designed by Clement Germany (D3.3), with the mooring optimised by MARIN (D5.1, led by MARIN);
- the electrical system of the array.

Integrating these components in KER1 transfers no ownership of them (Section 7.1). The confirmation of contributions that the IPR management plan D1.8 asks for before a commercial agreement is set out in Section 7.4.

The open-sea plant studied in the project, called the D7.4 reference plant in this plan, is an array inside a ring breakwater 346 m across. Clement Germany's D3.3 set the plant at 10 MWp, the minimum plant size of the proposal's cost-of-energy calculations, and sized the ring for about 18,600 panels of 537 Wp. The global system design D3.4 placed 7.16 MWp in the ring with the floats used at the time and noted that a more efficient use of the sheltered space would raise it. Clement Germany later compiled the material quantities for the 10 MWp plant and for a 2 MWp version with a ring 173 m across; the economic assessment D7.4 takes its anchoring, mooring, transition-element and connection quantities from them. D7.4 keeps the rings and breakwaters but replaces the panels with modules of about 730 Wp. D7.4 attributes the change of panels to the new hinge system. The larger panels change the number of panels, the array layout and the installed power. Its 10 MWp plant has 10,375 kWp installed (10.4 MWp), and its investment figures refer to that capacity. D7.4 assesses a single 2 MWp array, five 2 MWp arrays and the 10 MWp plant. It states plant sizes in MW of installed peak capacity; this plan writes MWp for the plants it assesses.

This plan uses the 10 MWp plant of Clement Germany's ring and the economics of D7.4 version 2.0 (November 2026), the newest assessment of the plant. The technical brochure D8.3, led by Sunlit Sea, differs from this plan on six points:

- It gives 7 MWp for the D7.4 reference plant, from the D3.4 layout, and the economics of D7.4 version 1.0 (Section 4.6).
- It gives the redesigned hinge as about three times as strong in tension as the first-generation hinge. The factor compares a complete first-generation hinge with five one-fifth segments held by a rope, SINTEF's scaling in its test report D6.3; D8.3 does not say this. This plan gives the measured segment values and the scaling below.
- It gives the 50-year significant wave heights of the use-case basis D2.1, led by SINTEF (4.4, 7.0 and 12.3 m). This plan uses the design sea states that MARIN's D5.1 takes from the 50-year contours of D2.1, with highest significant wave heights of 4.0 m in the Baltic Sea, 6.5 m in the Mediterranean and 11 m in the North Sea.
- It describes the basin model as a 1:10 model of the integrated system and states that the second campaign evaluated the redesigned hinge. MARIN's papers on the campaigns show a down-sized model, a 3 × 5 module array behind one breakwater section across the basin, with the ring only simulated, and a second campaign with the paired second-generation connectors (Energies 17(9) 2059; Energies 19(7) 1609; below). This plan follows MARIN's papers.
- It summarises the mooring optimisation as up to 56 % lighter chains, the figure of D5.1's summary; this plan gives D5.1's figures per site, including the heavier mooring at the Baltic site (Section 6.5).
- It presents Sunlit Sea's earlier float tests at the Stadt Towing Tank as a SUREWAVE campaign that calibrated the linear hydrodynamic method. D5.1 states that these tests were made outside SUREWAVE and that MARIN used their measured motions to validate its diffraction model DIFFRAC (Section 6.5). This plan follows D5.1.

D8.3 and this plan give the same readiness level, TRL 4–5.

Maturity at project end:

- The proposal aimed at TRL 4–5 for the system at project end, from TRL 2–3 at the start (Annex 1), and set TRL 5 for its result R1, the complete system validated in a relevant environment through laboratory tests, basin validation and long-term exposure at sea (Section 3.4).
- SINTEF's technological risk assessment (D7.2, led by SINTEF; Preprints 202611.1173, 2026) rates the integrated SUREWAVE concept at TRL 5, and IFEU's integrated sustainability assessment D7.6 (November 2025) adopts the rating. The rating was made for the system with the redesigned hinge, after the component tests at SINTEF and MARIN's two wave-basin campaigns. The campaigns tested a down-sized 1:10 model: a 3 × 5 array of floating PV modules, with and without one breakwater section spanning the width of the basin, in normal and oblique waves (Energies 17(9) 2059, 2024; Energies 19(7) 1609, 2026). The full ring was analysed numerically (D5.1; Energies 19(7) 1609).
- The EIC Transition proposal (September 2026) sets TRL 4, technology validated in the laboratory, as the starting point for the protected floating PV system it develops further. Its evidence is the project's progress up to October 2024: the first MARIN campaign, the reduced-scale breakwater prototype as manufactured, the laboratory tests of the circular concrete, the modelling chain and the connection tests. It records that long-term, grid-connected operation at a real site has not been demonstrated.
- ACCIONA's reduced-scale concrete breakwater prototype, with Ceit's sensors, was exposed at sea at Gijón without the PV array (Section 6.2). These results came after D7.6 and are not part of its assessment.
- This plan uses TRL 4–5 for the integrated system: the risk assessment rates it at TRL 5 on the basin and component tests, the EIC Transition proposal starts from TRL 4, and the integrated system with the array has not yet been at sea. This meets the proposal's TRL 4–5 for the system; R1's TRL 5 is met for the laboratory and basin elements, not yet for long-term exposure of the integrated system.
- Sunlit Sea rates its CONNECT platform at TRL 4–5 (Horizon Results Booster business plan, November 2026). Its full-edge third-generation hinge has been tested as a one-fifth segment at SINTEF (D6.3) and not yet in a basin (below). Prototype 3 of the Generation 2 float has been cast and prototype 4 is in casting; no Generation 2 plant has operated yet.
- The breakwater classes for nearshore sites (Section 4.1.1) have not been tested. Neither has a directional layout at plant scale, with a breakwater placed across the exposed sector of a full array; the basin campaigns tested a small array behind a straight breakwater section.

Sunlit Sea's commercial product from KER1 is the Generation 2 floating platform, Sunlit Sea CONNECT. Sunlit Sea started the Generation 2 redesign from the SUREWAVE hinge tests and models and continues it in the parallel project SuRE (Section 10), where prototypes 4 and 5 are developed and prepared for pilot-scale deployment. Each float is a cast polyurethane frame on a flat bottom plate, carrying one standard glass-glass solar panel at a small fixed tilt just above the water. The floats are joined along their full edge by flexible polyurethane hinges, so the array follows the waves. CONNECT is first made for sheltered sites. An exposed-site version, for sites behind a directional breakwater, is under development (Sections 4.1.1 and 4.5). Generation 1, Sunlit Sea's earlier product, uses square aluminium floats joined by polyurethane hinges.

The SUREWAVE reference design uses Sunlit Sea's Generation 1 floats, made of pressed marine-grade aluminium sheets (D3.1; Sunlit Sea's input), and D7.4 and D7.6 assessed the array with these floats (D7.6). The system concept, the breakwater, the mooring and the structure of the economics carry over to CONNECT. The float material and its cost do not. The cost of the polyurethane float enters through Sunlit Sea's financial plan (Section 4.7). Its life-cycle assessment has not been made. The WP8 lead proposes one, as a study commissioned from IFEU for the first multi-megawatt plant (Section 6.7).

The hinge design comes from SUREWAVE testing. The sources number the hinges in two ways. SINTEF's test reports and MARIN's 2 MWp paper call the hinge tested before the redesign the first generation. SINTEF's risk assessment and D7.6 call the hinges fitted in pairs at the float corners, shorter than the float edge, v2 and the full-edge hinge v3; this plan calls them the second and third generations. Hinge generations are numbered separately from the platform generations. The figures below are measured or calculated on different things, and each is named with what it applies to:

- First-generation hinges. In SINTEF's laboratory tests, complete hinges reached a mean tensile load of 3.69 kN and buckled at low compressive loads (D6.1; Preprints 202611.1612). D6.1 compares this with the 8–10 kN expected from the WP5 simulations and recommends a redesign that at least meets that requirement and avoids the collisions that the low compressive strength can cause. In MARIN's first basin campaign the hinges buckled in harsh seas (Energies 17(9) 2059, 2024), with snap loading after the buckling (D8.3).
- Redesign. Sunlit Sea redesigned the connection and delivered injection-moulded test components to SINTEF (D6.3). The third-generation hinge spans the full float edge, 1.88 m (D7.6).
- Tests of the redesigned connection (D6.3). SINTEF tested a segment that D6.3 gives as one fifth of the full connection. Its mean tensile load was 2.04 kN with the polypropylene rope that holds the two halves together, and 8.36 kN with a steel rod in place of the rope. Its mean compressive capacity was 20.23 kN; D6.3 concludes that the improved compression behaviour will avoid the early buckling and snap loading of the first generation. With the rope, the fatigue analysis gives about 2.5 × 10¹¹ load cycles to failure, against 7.88 × 10⁸ cycles for 25 years at one wave a second. With a steel rod, the 25 years are reached if the force in the polyurethane part stays below 6.9 kN. D6.3 calls the fatigue analysis a rough estimate that does not cover mixed-mode loading. Ageing of the polyurethane is not included. The test components had shrinkage gaps of 1–2 mm and some early cracks, and D6.3 finds that control of the production process is critical for series production.
- Second basin campaign. The first campaign had shown frequent connector buckling. MARIN's second campaign used second-generation connectors, longer and thicker than the first generation, with higher compressive stiffness, fitted in pairs between adjacent floats (Energies 19(7) 1609, 2026; D7.2, Preprints 202611.1173). Sunlit Sea's technical brochure D8.3 reports no measurable hinge buckling or snap loading in this campaign; MARIN's papers do not state it. MARIN used the measured motions of the modules and the breakwater to validate its diffraction model before simulating the 2 MWp farm (Energies 19(7) 1609). The full-edge third-generation hinge was not in the basin model.
- Design values (SINTEF's technological risk assessment, D7.2 and Preprints 202611.1173, summarised in D7.6). The design break load of the full-width third-generation hinge at the ultimate limit state, the highest load the hinge is designed to carry, is 37 kN, against 14 kN for the paired second-generation corner hinges. The maximum hinge loads simulated in the 50-year sea states (D7.6, summarising D7.2; Preprints 202611.1173) are 34 kN at the Baltic site and 33 kN in the Mediterranean, below the design value, and about 40 kN in the North Sea, about 8 % above it; with the paired second-generation hinges they were 45, 43 and 54 kN, against those hinges' design break load of 14 kN. D7.6 states that the robustness of the redesigned hinges could not be fully validated within the project. Exposed open-sea sites in the North Sea need a further hinge generation (Section 4.8).
- Comparison on the full edge. D6.3 scales the segment result to the full connection: five segments with the rope carry about 10 kN, almost three times the 3.69 kN of a complete first-generation hinge but less than a third of the simulated extreme loads of 33–34 kN at the Baltic and Mediterranean sites; with a steel rod they carry about 42 kN, above those loads, and D6.3 then requires the force in the polyurethane part to stay below 6.9 kN for a 25-year fatigue life. D6.3 recommends a stiffer holding element than the rope. D7.2 and D7.6 do not state which holding element the 37 kN assumes. The holding element of CONNECT, with its break load and fatigue life, is set in the prototype 4 tests before the design freeze (Section 4.5).
- Uncertainty of simulated hinge loads. The hinge loads that MARIN computed in its coupled analysis (D5.1) come from its linear diffraction model DIFFRAC, whose motions MARIN validated against Sunlit Sea's earlier Stadt Towing Tank tests (Section 6.5). Hinge loads were not measured there, and D5.1 expects the nonlinear behaviour of the hinges to make their computation difficult.

Sunlit Sea's site-planning tool determines for each site where a breakwater is needed and estimates the energy lost to wave motion (Section 4.1.1). Where waves exceed what the platform alone can take, a breakwater is placed only on the sides the strong waves come from.

#### 4.1.1 Breakwaters for nearshore sites

SUREWAVE designed one breakwater cross-section: Clement Germany's unit for the open sea (D3.2), in a full ring around the plant (D3.3). Most sites in Sunlit Sea's markets are nearshore or inland. There the waves are limited by the fetch, the stretch of open water the wind blows over, so they are lower and shorter than at sea and come from some directions only. Sunlit Sea has sized three breakwater classes for such sites; the SUREWAVE unit is the heaviest of them. The two lighter classes are scaled from it with its design data (D3.2, D5.1), with the same construction and double-keel shape. The costs use D7.4's breakwater and anchoring costs as modelled, plus Sunlit Sea's own estimates for fabrication, fittings, transport, supplier margin and mobilisation, which D7.4 does not contain (Section 4.6.2). The classes are a sizing study, not a design: they are to be confirmed, engineered and supplied by Clement Germany, the owner of the breakwater design (KER3), on the terms in Section 5.

The sizing method:

- Design waves: fetch-limited wave spectra of the JONSWAP type (from the Joint North Sea Wave Project), the standard spectrum for wind seas, with the growth curves of the US Shore Protection Manual of 1984. The design wind is 22 m/s at 10 m height, a working assumption of Sunlit Sea to be checked against the wind data of each site.
- Wave transmission behind the breakwater: the Macagno formula, which gives the share of the wave height that passes a fixed floating breakwater, as used in D3.2 and D5.1.
- Criteria at the array: short waves, with periods of 3 s or less, at most 0.45 m significant height. D3.2 names the band of 1–3 s as critical for the array (Section 6.3 gives the transmission of the SUREWAVE unit in it), and D5.1 finds the array's hinge loads peaking in the band. The 0.45 m is a 0.5 m limit for the short waves, Sunlit Sea's working assumption for the platform, with a margin of 10 %. All waves together: at most 0.6 m significant height for the platform for sheltered sites and at most 0.8 m for the exposed-site version under development. These limits are Sunlit Sea's working assumptions, to be confirmed in the demonstrator.
- Cross-sections: a width-to-draft ratio of 2–3, as for the SUREWAVE unit, and a minimum width of 2.5 m for stability and access. A search found the lightest section meeting both criteria for each wave class, and the chosen classes are larger, as a margin: 2.5 × 1.0 m against 2.5 × 0.9 m (light), 4.0 × 1.5 m against 3.0 × 1.2 m (medium), and the SUREWAVE unit against 4.5 × 1.5 m (heavy). The margins differ because the medium and heavy search results sit at the limit for the total transmitted wave height, while the light one stays well below it.

| Breakwater class | Site wave conditions (design significant wave height in a 22 m/s design wind) | Cross-section | Status | Platform behind it |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Light | Mild, up to about 0.65 m, where the short waves exceed 0.45 m or the design wave height exceeds 0.6 m: lakes and inner waters with a fetch of up to about 1.6 km | About 2.5 m wide, 1 m draft | Desk sizing | CONNECT, the platform for sheltered sites |
| Medium | Medium, up to about 1.0 m: fetch up to about 3.8 km | About 4 m wide, 1.5 m draft | Desk sizing | CONNECT |
| Heavy | Rough nearshore, up to about 1.3 m: fetch up to about 6.4 km | The SUREWAVE breakwater unit: 5 m wide, about 2 m draft, 20 m long (unit mass in Section 6.3) | Designed by Clement Germany with CFD (D3.2); a breakwater section tested at 1:10 in MARIN's basin campaigns (Section 4.1); a reduced-scale prototype exposed at Gijón (Section 6.2) | The exposed-site version of CONNECT, under development (Sections 4.1 and 4.5) |

The draft is measured to the bottom of the keels, so a unit weighs less than a solid block of the stated width and draft. A light unit can be cast, trucked and lifted with ordinary harbour equipment.

The platform needs no breakwater where the short waves stay below about 0.45 m and the design wave height below about 0.6 m (working limits, to be confirmed in the demonstrator). At mild sites the short waves usually exceed their limit first, so a light breakwater is needed even where the design wave height is close to 0.6 m. Longer waves pass a floating breakwater largely unreduced, so at medium and rough sites the limit for all waves decides the class, and rough sites need the exposed-site version of the platform even behind the SUREWAVE unit. Above about 1.3 m the waves that pass the breakwater exceed that version's working limit. There, or where open-sea swell reaches the site, the full SUREWAVE open-sea design applies: the same unit in a ring, with mooring and anchoring sized for open-sea storms, for design waves of up to 6.5 m in the Mediterranean (D5.1). The open-sea design is checked against another criterion. SINTEF's risk assessment (D7.2) reports the hinge loads of the array simulated in each site's 50-year sea states and compares them with the hinge's design break load (Section 4.1), instead of applying the working wave limits used here for the commercial platform. By that check the hinges carry the extreme loads at the Baltic and Mediterranean sites and not yet in the North Sea.

Each site is classified with Sunlit Sea's site-planning tool. The tool is built on the SUREWAVE nearshore wave and wind models, from ERA5 (the climate reanalysis of the European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts) and GEBCO (the General Bathymetric Chart of the Oceans) data, and gives the wave and wind conditions along the full perimeter of a planned plant. From these conditions and fetch-limited wave growth, the design waves are set for each direction. This gives the site a wave class and an exposure sector, from about 20° (waves from one side) to half a circle (180°). The breakwater is placed only across the exposed sector, widened by 10° on each side for the spread of wave directions in a wind sea, as an arc or a straight line, whichever is shorter. It is extended at the ends so that waves bending round them do not reach the array. It stands 15 m from the array, the distance in D7.4's cost model for its 2 MWp ring. Its length, and so its cost, follows from the sector, the plant size and that distance.

Three findings from this work shape the product:

- The breakwater cost per kilowatt-peak roughly halves from a plant of about 3 MWp to one of 10 MWp (light class, one exposed side) and rises steeply below 1 MWp: at a plant of a few hundred kWp it is close to ten times the value at 10 MWp. Small plants need natural shelter.
- A breakwater can serve other uses at the same time: berths, bathing or sauna platforms, boathouses or a café. Where the site owner has such a use, part of the breakwater's cost is carried by it. This fits leisure lakes, marinas and harbours.
- All three classes have natural roll and heave periods close to the peak periods of their design waves: about 2.7 and 2.5 s against 2.3 s (light), 3.4 and 3.2 s against 3.1 s (medium), 3.8 and 3.6 s against 3.7 s (heavy), scaled from the periods D5.1 gives for the SUREWAVE unit. The Macagno formula treats the breakwater as rigid and is least reliable near these periods, where a moving breakwater lets more wave energy through. The classes may need more mass or damping than the first sizing gives. This is to be checked with Clement Germany and in basin tests.

### 4.2 Organisation and objectives

Sunlit Sea AS is a Norwegian floating solar company founded in 2019 and based in Oslo. It has built two Generation 1 plants, with floats made in its own factory: a 60 kWp plant in commercial operation in Germany since 2023, and its own 105 kWp plant in Norway, which sold its power under a power purchase agreement from 2024 to October 2026. In 2024 it wound down the factory and moved to subcontracted production, to concentrate on Generation 2. The core team is small. The chief executive officer (CEO) invented Generation 1 and holds the design and casting work for Generation 2; the technical lead, Sunlit Sea's WP8 lead, author of its deliverables D2.4 (Stakeholder Advisory Board) and D7.4 (economic assessment) and, with SINTEF, of D8.5 (WP8 master plan), is responsible for the site-planning tool, sales, projects and administration. Sunlit Sea works with partners for structural simulation (IFE), PV module integration, sales in Germany, and local construction and permitting.

Objectives:

- 2027–2028: freeze the CONNECT design, reach TRL 6 for sheltered sites through the first Norwegian reference plants, start a DNV design-basis assessment and prepare series production with casting partners.
- 2029–2032: sell CONNECT as a kit to developers and contractors in the Nordic countries, Germany, the Baltic region and later the Mediterranean; win a first 10 MW-class kit supply contract by 2030; extend the product to exposed sites with a directional breakwater.

### 4.3 Market

Floating solar reached at least 8.7 GW worldwide at the end of 2024 (Photovoltaic Power Systems Programme of the International Energy Agency, IEA PVPS, Trends in Photovoltaic Applications 2025). Of the 7.7 GW installed by the end of 2023, almost 90 % was in Asia; the Netherlands and France were the largest markets outside Asia (IEA PVPS Task 13, Floating PV Plants, 2025). Most European plants are on quarry lakes, sandpit lakes, irrigation ponds and other man-made inland water. IEA PVPS rates floating PV on inland water above TRL 8 and offshore floating PV at TRL 3–5, with only a few pilot and demonstration projects (Task 13, 2025).

Floating solar at sea is at pilot stage in Europe. The largest European marine units reported are 0.5–1 MW: SolarDuck's 0.5 MWp pilot in the Dutch North Sea, installed in July 2024 (RWE, 2024), and BlueNewables' 1 MW demonstrator of two 500 kW platforms at the Port of Valencia, whose first unit has been in testing since mid-July 2026 (pv magazine, 14 July 2026), with the full 1 MW reported in testing in October 2026 (Energías Renovables, October 2026). Sunlit Sea's review of company announcements and trade press from 2024 to 2026 found no commercial multi-megawatt floating solar plant on European coastal water. The potential is documented in two countries:

- Spain: 4.45–6.48 GW of floating solar at sea within the national maritime spatial plans, in a study by the University of A Coruña (Journal of Cleaner Production, 2026, reported by pv magazine, June 2026).
- The Netherlands: scenarios with 200 GWp of solar power in 2050, of which 45 GWp at sea (TNO, the Netherlands Organisation for Applied Scientific Research, floating solar programme page, accessed 2026).

The first commercial niche is plants of 0.3–10 MWp in sheltered coastal water next to industry and ports. Such sites have local power demand and a water area, but no land to spare. In Norway, the Norwegian Water Resources and Energy Directorate (NVE), the energy regulator, expects grid scarcity in large parts of the country and land-use conflict to limit ground-mounted solar before 2030: by February 2024 it had received applications for close to 1.4 GW of ground-mounted plants and licensed three plants with 14 MW, against about 10 GW needed for the political goal of 8 TWh of solar power by 2030 (NVE, note on solar power and other local generation, 5 February 2024). Sunlit Sea has not yet sized the sheltered coastal segment. The count of suitable coastal sites per country, planned with the customer interviews in early 2027 (Section 4.3.1), gives the first estimate.

Two customer segments:

1. Coastal industry and ports with local power demand: local, fixed-price renewable power without waiting for grid capacity, bought under long-term power purchase agreements.
2. Developers and independent power producers building 0.3–10 MWp plants on coastal water: a wave-tested floating system delivered as a kit, with site engineering that lenders and insurers accept.

An adjacent segment for the platform is large lakes and hydropower reservoirs exposed to waves. For the breakwater classes, the first sites are lakes (Section 4.6.2).

At sea in Europe, the competitors are few and at pilot stage. Outside Europe and on inland water, suppliers have installed bases, but for calm water or tropical lagoons. Status from public sources:

- Fred. Olsen 1848 (pretensioned rope mesh): pilot stage; a DNV Statement of Conformity to DNV-RP-0584 for a 3 MW island at significant wave heights up to 3.5 m, and a DNV technical review of its design methodology (DNV, July 2026; pv magazine, September 2026).
- Ocean Sun (membrane on a floating ring): Bureau Veritas type certificate for its floating structure (Ocean Sun, November 2025).
- SolarDuck (elevated platforms): the 0.5 MWp North Sea pilot above (RWE, 2024), with a Bureau Veritas prototype certificate (pv magazine, March 2024).
- Oceans of Energy (offshore platforms): an array of about 1,400 panels at an offshore wind farm in the Dutch North Sea, installed in August 2025 and withdrawn in 2026 after thermal incidents traced to a connector defect (CrossWind, May 2026); partner in the EU project BAMBOO.
- BlueNewables (raised catamaran platforms): the 1 MW Valencia demonstrator above (pv magazine, 14 July 2026).
- Swimsol (rigid platforms for tropical lagoons): sea-based plants in the Maldives, the largest 2.4 MW (pv magazine, April 2026).
- Inland float suppliers such as Ciel & Terre: plastic pontoons for calm water; Ciel & Terre had completed about 820 MW in 280 projects by mid-2023 (TaiyangNews, 2023) and is a partner in the EU project SuRE (CORDIS, the EU's database of funded research projects).

The withdrawn North Sea array failed on an electrical connector, not on its structure. Sunlit Sea's competitor review finds electrical and balance-of-system reliability the recurring failure at sea in 2025–2026; the qualification programme of Section 4.5 covers the electrical components as well as the structure.

In Sunlit Sea's assessment, competition at sea is decided on references, on independent review of the design and on the price per project, since no supplier publishes list prices.

Value proposition for segment 1: renewable power produced on the host's own water area, next to its load, at a fixed long-term price, from a system designed for the site's waves. For segment 2: a wave-tested floating system with site engineering that sizes structure and breakwater to the site's wave conditions, at a cost per watt that makes coastal sites investable.

#### 4.3.3 Sustainability evidence

The integrated sustainability assessment D7.6, led by IFEU, compares the open-sea SUREWAVE plant of about 10 MWp, in several European sea scenarios, with electricity from coal, the fossil benchmark D7.6 uses. Compared with coal, every scenario is more climate-friendly, uses fewer fossil resources, has a low land-use footprint and has lower social supply-chain risks. The plant's impacts are higher than those of land-based PV or wind power. Local environmental impacts can occur and have to be managed. Every indicator reaches an acceptable outcome in at least one scenario, and the Mediterranean is the most advantageous location. More profitable technical choices tend to be more sustainable too, and one large uniform plant is preferred over five units of about 2 MWp for its lower material intensity, if offshore experience supports its durability and shading of sensitive ecosystems is not an issue at the site; D7.6 assessed the split layout for the North Sea only.

D7.6 sets conditions for a sustainable deployment:

- target markets where the plants replace fossil-based power and avoid competing with wind and land-based PV;
- high efficiency: high solar yield, mild waves and wind, small depths, short distance to shore or grid and to a harbour;
- no substantial disturbance of sensitive shallow-water habitats or marine-mammal migration routes;
- socially beneficial procurement.

D7.6 recommends demonstrating floating PV at sea at an intermediate scale as quickly as the remaining technological de-risking allows. The KER1 plan takes the de-risking steps at sheltered and exposed nearshore sites first, from 2027, and plans an open-sea pilot proposal about 2031 (Section 4.6.1).

This evidence supports the value proposition to lenders and permitting authorities. D7.6 assessed the open-sea plant with the aluminium floats of the SUREWAVE array (Section 4.1). For sheltered and nearshore sites, and for the polyurethane float of CONNECT, the evidence is indicative until it is assessed for those plants.

### 4.5 Development and qualification

CONNECT will be qualified before series production against the DNV floating solar documents, two standards new in May 2026 and a recommended practice updated in May 2026 (DNV, May 2026): DNV-ST-C108, Structural design of floats for floating photovoltaic systems; DNV-ST-E309, Station keeping of floating solar photovoltaic systems; and the recommended practice DNV-RP-0584, Design, development and operation of floating solar photovoltaic systems. The programme covers:

- break load and fatigue of the hinge, with the holding element chosen in the prototype 4 tests (Section 4.1), and fatigue of the mooring under wave cycles;
- durability of the polyurethane in sun and seawater: accelerated ultraviolet (UV) ageing to the International Electrotechnical Commission's technical specification IEC TS 62788-7-2, followed by mechanical testing, which also covers the ageing that the fatigue analysis of D6.3 left out;
- control of the casting process, since the test components of D6.3 had shrinkage gaps and early cracks;
- corrosion protection of the bottom plate;
- marine growth;
- marine electrical components and cable movement;
- field monitoring of the first plants.

Milestones:

- access-rights requests to SINTEF and MARIN, before the design freeze, and Sunlit Sea's request to SINTEF, Ceit and MARIN to confirm the contributions to the hinge redesign, as the WP8 lead proposes (Q1 2027; Sections 7.4 and 9);
- design freeze with prototype 4, after the hinge and float pass the test programme (Q2 2027);
- first Norwegian reference plant in operation, with floats cast from the prototype and pilot moulds (Q3 2027);
- freedom-to-operate review and decision on design registration (Q3 2027);
- DNV design-basis assessment (from Q4 2027);
- TRL 6 for sheltered sites after the first Norwegian reference plant's first winter (Q2 2028): the WP8 lead rates one winter at a single plant of a few hundred kWp as TRL 6, technology demonstrated in a relevant environment (Horizon Europe General Annexes); TRL 7, a system prototype demonstrated in an operational environment, needs a longer record at plant scale;
- industrial moulds and kit packing (2027–2028).

The exposed-site version needs a demonstrator with a directional breakwater. The EIC Transition proposal (Section 10) plans it, to take the protected floating PV system from TRL 4 to TRL 6.

#### 4.6.2 First sites with breakwater protection

The first breakwater protection in operation will be the small wavebreakers of the first Norwegian reference plant, a port plant, on its side exposed to local wind waves, in 2027. It is the first field test of placing protection only where the waves come from. The first sites for the breakwater classes are expected to be inland lakes and inner waters exposed to wind waves from one direction. Lakes are an adjacent segment for the platform alone (Section 4.3), but they come first for the classes, because their waves are limited by the fetch and the light class is enough. The baseline site of the EIC Transition demonstrator, the step to exposed sites, is such a lake, reached through Clement Germany's relationship with the site owner (Section 10). There a light breakwater covers the exposed side, and the site owner's other uses, such as berths or bathing platforms, can carry part of its cost. The small wavebreakers of the first Norwegian reference plant are bought from a wavebreaker supplier (Section 4.4.1); they are not built from the SUREWAVE breakwater design or its classes.

The EIC Transition proposal targets a first commercial installation of 1–5 MWp after the EIC Transition project: immediately after its completion in its description of the commercial potential, and within 12–24 months after completion in its exploitation plan, subject to the site owner, permits and finance. With a start in 2027, the 36-month project ends about 2030. Sunlit Sea's financial plan models it in 2031, the first full year after the EIC Transition project would end.

If Sunlit Sea takes part in a Horizon Europe application under the call "Improved system design for innovative PV applications" (HORIZON-CL5-2026-11-D3-14) of the co-programmed European Partnership for Innovation in Photovoltaics (EUPI-PV) (Section 10), and it is funded, a nearshore demonstration with a breakwater could come earlier, in 2029.

The breakwater's share of the plant cost, the breakwater cost divided by the cost of PV plant and breakwater together, falls steeply with plant size. At a pilot-scale demonstrator, for which Sunlit Sea's planning for the EIC Transition proposal uses 50–300 kWp (the proposal leaves the size to site selection), the breakwater makes up about half of the plant cost or more, since it costs from about as much as the PV plant to several times as much. At about 5 MWp its share is around a tenth, and at a few tens of MWp a few per cent; where the site owner's other uses carry part of its cost, the share is lower. The estimate is for a light breakwater on the exposed side of a plant built by a kit buyer. It rests on the breakwater and anchoring costs of D7.4 version 2.0 as modelled, plus Sunlit Sea's estimates for fabrication, fittings, transport, supplier margin and mobilisation. It is indicative and is to be confirmed by Clement Germany.

The demonstrator would run in 2028–2030, and its breakwater would be funded by the EIC Transition grant, if the proposal is funded.

From about 5 MWp, a light breakwater scaled from the SUREWAVE design opens sites with wind waves to floating PV. The first commercial plant with a breakwater class serves as a reference and earns little margin. With the breakwater half carried by other uses, the plant's cost of power is well above the average award of €0.048/kWh in Germany's solar tender of July 2025 (Bundesnetzagentur, August 2025). Selling to the grid does not cover its cost; it needs a buyer who values the power behind the meter, or investment support. The step from demonstrator to commercial plants also gives the field record for the exposed-site configuration.

For orientation, Wood Mackenzie forecasts a cumulative floating solar capacity of 2.2 GW in Germany, 1.2 GW in France and 1 GW in the Netherlands by 2033 (reported by pv magazine, November 2024). Spread by Sunlit Sea over 2024–2033, that is at most about 0.4–0.5 GW of new capacity a year in the three markets, less the capacity already installed. The 2031 volume of about 16 MWp would be about 3–4 % of it, although part of Sunlit Sea's volume is in markets outside these three. Most floating PV in Europe is on man-made inland water (Section 4.3), where CONNECT is not the target product, so the comparison is an upper bound. The nearshore and sheltered-coastal segment that the platform targets has no published size. Sunlit Sea plans a count of suitable sites in Norway, Sweden, Denmark, Germany and the Baltic states with the customer interviews in early 2027.

Breakwater classes are bought as set out in Section 5. The Norwegian sites that Sunlit Sea delivers as EPC contractor need no breakwater class on current site data, and kit buyers buy any breakwater themselves as plant owners, so the plan has no breakwater class in its revenue or costs. Two sensitivities test this. The first Norwegian reference plant, which Sunlit Sea owns, uses small wavebreakers on its exposed side. They are not yet quoted and are tested at about €0.1M. That lowers cash by about €0.1M and the result over the period by about €0.05M, since the cost is depreciated over the plant's life. If the two larger Norwegian sites needed a light breakwater on one side and it were not priced into the EPC contracts, the result over the period would be lower, and 2031 EBITDA and cash would stay positive; the plan prices a breakwater into the EPC contract wherever the site study shows exposure. At sites that need a breakwater class, Sunlit Sea's gain is the kit and engineering for a site that would otherwise be closed to floating PV.

Conditions for the plan:

- Kit price: the plan's kit gross margin target is 40–50 %, and the plan meets it at today's kit price and production cost. Cost reductions from series moulds, a lighter bottom plate, casting near the markets where that lowers cost, and volume purchasing give room to lower the price where buyers need it, or to absorb a breakwater at more exposed sites.
- Investment support for the first plants: at first-of-a-kind cost, the first plants need investment support of about a quarter of their cost to match the host's alternative supply. Support of that order is sought from national schemes for first-of-a-kind energy technology. Without support, plants built from the kit are competitive where power costs more than at the first Norwegian sites. Examples are industry with higher power prices, sites limited by grid capacity, and southern Europe, where the yield is higher. In markets with poor solar irradiation, national support for floating solar is assumed.
- References and volume: the plan is most sensitive to the timing of the Norwegian reference plants and to kit volume and margin in 2030–2031.
  - If the Norwegian reference plants and kit sales come one year later, the net result over 2027–2031 falls more than in any other single sensitivity, 2031 EBITDA is slightly negative, and cash falls below zero (lowest year-end cash about −€0.3M). About €0.5M more equity keeps a cash buffer of €0.2M. The second round then moves to 2030.
  - If the 10 MW-class contract is not won, 2031 EBITDA and cash stay positive, and the net result over the period is lower.
  - Kit volume 30 % lower in 2030–2031, or a kit price lower or a kit cost higher by the same small amount per watt, each lowers 2031 EBITDA, which stays positive in every case; cash stays positive.
  - A combined commercial downside (one year later, kit volume 30 % lower and no further kit cost reduction) gives a negative net result in every year of the plan and a negative 2031 EBITDA. It needs about €1.0M more equity, or the 2029–2031 hires and the second mould set are held back.

All of these depend on the first plants operating as designed, and the plan ties hiring and investment to four decision gates set by those plants:

1. First equity round closed (Q1 2027): order the first Norwegian reference plant and start hiring.
2. Investment decision by the plant company at the first multi-megawatt site (Q4 2027): sign the EPC contract and commit the minority share; if the decision slips, the 2028 hires are delayed.
3. First Norwegian reference plant through its first winter, with loads and yield within design values (Q2 2028): release float production for the multi-megawatt plant and order the industrial moulds.
4. The multi-megawatt plant in operation for a year, and a letter of intent for the 10 MW-class contract (2029): raise the second round, order the second mould set and hire the application engineers.

### 4.8 Main risks for KER1

The KER1 risk assessment, prepared for the Horizon Results Booster and revised on 23 November 2026 to match the financial plan, rates 26 risks. Ratings follow the Booster risk map: criticality and probability are each rated from 1 (low) to 10 (high), and the grade is their product. The feasibility of the intervention is rated on the same scale. A grade above 50 with a feasibility above 5 puts a risk in the action zone, and a grade up to 50 with a feasibility above 5 in the control zone. A feasibility below 5 puts a risk in the warning zone (grade above 50) or the no-action zone (grade up to 50); no KER1 risk has a feasibility below 5. A feasibility of exactly 5 falls on the border between zones. Four risks are in the action zone and come first in the table. The other rows are all risks with a grade of 40 or more, and the lower-graded risks that concern the SUREWAVE partners and results, the float itself (casting and durability), standards, freedom to operate and the EPC contracts. These other rows are in the control zone or on its border. The last row comes from the breakwater-class analysis and is not rated in the risk map.
