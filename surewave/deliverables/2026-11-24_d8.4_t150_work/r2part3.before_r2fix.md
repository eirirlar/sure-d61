## 3. Key Exploitable Results

### 3.1 How the KERs were identified

The KERs were set in five steps.

1. Starting point. The proposal named one global solution, P1 *Offshore FPV plant*, to be marketed by Sunlit Sea, and eleven expected results, R1–R11. R1 is the global solution itself. The proposal's table of preliminary KERs gave R2–R10 an owner, a type, a business strategy and a target market; the table has no R numbers, and the mapping to R2–R10 follows the result titles. R11, the integrated sustainability assessment framework, is listed with the other results but has no row in that table. D8.5 (month 18, March 2024) reported the mid-term status of R2–R10 and added six findings from the first test campaigns, among them snapping loads on the hinges, the benefit of lighter floats, and the cost of full breakwater protection, which led to the idea of protecting only the exposed sides. The other three concern off-the-shelf panels, the use of panel aluminium frames and sustainable concrete materials. Section 2.1 gives the outcome of each.
2. Candidate KERs. From the proposal's results and D8.5, the WP8 lead proposed seven candidate KERs, one anchored in each beneficiary's main contribution. They were listed in a common input template sent to the partners on 29 September 2026. For each result the template asked for the type, the KER it maps to, a short description, the audience, the steps taken towards exploitation, market maturity, current and target TRL, the exploitation route, the time to market, and the expected revenue or deployment four years after the project.
3. Partner input. ACCIONA's summary (26 November 2025) and Clement Germany's (4 September 2026) were written before the template was sent. The other five partners replied to the request of 29 September between 30 September and 5 October 2026, in their own format rather than in the template fields (Section 2.2). The seven summaries give each result's description and exploitation route; none gives a TRL, a time to market or a revenue figure. No partner proposed a KER of its own. The seven summaries report 27 exploitable results. Publications and dissemination activities were collected by a separate request (Section 2.5).
4. Screening. The WP8 lead checked each reported result against the technical deliverables and the technical brochure (D8.3), and classified it: two commercial tools in use (Sunlit Sea's nearshore modelling in its site-planning tool, MARIN's DIFFRAC solver), three validated material formulations (ACCIONA's HPC, LWAC and CLC), one hardware platform on a defined path to market (Sunlit Sea's Generation 2 platform), one sensor technology at demonstrator stage (Ceit's eddy-current corrosion sensor), and methods, software and service lines that the partners add to their existing portfolios. Gains in network and visibility (one from Clement Germany, two from Sunlit Sea) and Clement Germany's contribution to engineering practice and technical committees are reported as impact, not as KERs; the other 23 results form the seven KERs. IFEU reported one result, framed as strengthened visibility, network and expertise in sustainability assessment; KER7 describes IFEU's application, in D7.6, of the integrated life-cycle sustainability assessment method behind that expertise. Where sources differ, this plan uses the owner's own figure: from the owner's deliverable where it gives an exact value, otherwise from the owner's summary. The check found these differences:
   - LWAC density: 1,950 kg/m³ (68 MPa) in ACCIONA's input, near 2,000 kg/m³ for the same 68 MPa mix in D4.1, and about 1,800 kg/m³ in D8.3. This plan uses ACCIONA's figure, the owner's exact value; D4.1 gives an approximate one.
   - Cement in the circular HPC: ACCIONA's input gives 5 % of the cement replaced by fly ash and slag; D4.1's mix uses 400 kg/m³ of cement instead of 500, with fly ash and slag; D7.3 gives a 20 % lower clinker content. All sources agree on the 128 MPa strength. This plan uses the D4.1 recipe.
   - Mooring optimisation: MARIN's input reports a larger reduction in mooring chains than D5.1 gives. D5.1 cut mooring-line mass by 34–59 % at the Mediterranean and North Sea sites without exceeding the proof loads (the loads the mooring lines may not exceed); at the Baltic site, where the reference design exceeded the proof loads, the optimised mooring is 2–3 times heavier. Section 6.5 gives the figures per site and farm size. This plan uses D5.1.
   - Hydrodynamic modelling: the IPR management plan D1.8 attributes the hydrodynamic models to MARIN. SINTEF's input and its preprint on keeled-breakwater dynamics (Preprints 202610.0011, 2026) document SINTEF's own high-fidelity breakwater modelling. This plan lists both, each owned by its developer.
   - Hinge inputs: D1.8 names SINTEF, Ceit and MARIN as the partners whose assessments informed the hinge design. Ceit's structural integrity assessment (D5.3) concerns the breakwater; it identified the hinges as the most failure-prone part of the system and left their modelling to the hinge redesign. This plan names SINTEF's connection tests and MARIN's basin tests as the inputs to the redesign.
   - MariSol: Sunlit Sea's input calls it a subsequent project. The Research Council of Norway record gives 2020–2025, so it started before SUREWAVE; this plan follows the record.
   - Project names: this plan uses the names of the public records for the projects in SINTEF's input, CIRCWIND (Horizon Europe 101147517) and FlexiShape (Research Council of Norway 321508, the national project SINTEF's input calls Mikroflexishape).
5. Mapping. Each result was placed under the candidate KER it mainly contributes to. Every reported result could be placed under one of the seven candidates, so the seven were kept. Each KER is built around its owner's main exploitation route. SINTEF's main contribution is KER4. SINTEF reported its aerodynamic and hydrodynamic modelling together; this plan places the aerodynamic method with SINTEF's other methods in KER4 and the hydrodynamic modelling with MARIN's in KER5, because both serve the same customers for hydrodynamic design services, while ownership stays separate (Section 7.1). The links between results were recorded:
   - KER1 integrates the breakwater, its mooring and anchoring and the transition element designed by Clement Germany (D3.2, D3.3), with MARIN's mooring optimisation (D5.1).
   - SINTEF's connection tests and MARIN's basin tests with elastically scaled hinges informed Sunlit Sea's hinge redesign, which led to the third hinge generation on the Generation 2 platform. Hinge generations are numbered separately from platform generations.
   - Clement Germany's breakwater options use ACCIONA's concrete.
   - Ceit's structural health management system uses SINTEF's bending tests of ACCIONA's concrete mixes, Clement Germany's breakwater geometry, MARIN's hydrodynamic load histories and Ceit's own finite element models.

KER1 has the full business plan, for five reasons:

- The proposal and the Grant Agreement ask for it. P1 is the product the consortium set out to bring to market, and objective O8 asks for a definitive business model and business plan for the global solution. The other expected results were to be exploited by their owners.
- It is the only new product. KERs 2–7 strengthen products and services that their owners sell today: concrete construction, breakwater engineering, research and testing, software and basin tests, monitoring, sustainability studies. No partner reported a spin-off or an investment that would call for a separate business plan.
- It carries the commercial risk. KER1 needs a market entry, a production chain, reference plants and €5.5 million of equity. The other KERs are exploited within existing businesses and budgets.
- It brings the other KERs to market in floating PV. The breakwater, the concrete, the testing, modelling, monitoring and sustainability results reach floating PV customers as parts of, or services to, KER1 (Section 5). The KER1 plan also sets the volume and timing for the partners' floating PV business.
- It was taken into business-plan support. KER1 entered the Horizon Results Booster's Go-to-Market service, which produced the business plan, financial plan, roadmap and risk map summarised in Section 4.

For KERs 2–7, each owner keeps its own business planning, and its main markets lie outside floating PV. This plan gives each of them the individual exploitation plan that the proposal foresees for every consortium member: an exploitation route and a dated action plan with indicators (Section 6). It adds each owner's role and payment basis in KER1 (Section 5) and its protection position (Section 7.2). Publishing partners' internal commercial plans in a consortium document would also expose them to the other partners, some of whom are suppliers or customers of each other.

### 3.5 Non-commercial pathways

The proposal's joint exploitation plan also covers non-commercial pathways: scientific, political and social. In SUREWAVE they take these forms:

- Scientific: the ten publications and four preprints of the partners, and the open records on Zenodo, among them the D7.4 cost model, the metocean design dataset, the anchoring and connection design dataset and the basin-test dataset (Section 2.5). The partners' action plans continue with the journal versions of the preprints and further papers (Section 6).
- Social: the report on societal needs and engagement (D8.2) and the social life-cycle assessment (D7.5), both led by IFEU, and the Stakeholder Advisory Board, which Sunlit Sea established and reported in D2.4.
- Political: the deployment conditions and recommendations of D7.6 and the public cost data of D7.4 are open to planners and regulators. No separate policy action is part of this plan.
