# Financial plan — KER1 Sunlit Sea CONNECT floating photovoltaic platform

Horizon Results Booster G2M annex (Option 1) · Sunlit Sea AS · 22 November 2026 · confidential

This plan completes the financial part of the KER1 business plan of 15 November 2026. It covers the Financials and The resources sections of the Booster template, and sets:

- the break-even year and the funding need;
- the target market share;
- the bottom-up sales forecast;
- the unit economics, with a worked example for a Norwegian plant;
- the test of the kit price against the margin target;
- the figures for the first 10 MW-class supply contract.

All amounts are in euro, nominal, at 11.7 NOK per euro. The plan contains Sunlit Sea's costs, prices and pipeline and is meant for Sunlit Sea's board and the Booster mentors only. It reflects the status in November 2026. The assumptions listed in section 15 are checked before the December workshop.

## 1. Summary

- Sunlit Sea grows in two phases. In 2027–2029 it develops and delivers its first Norwegian plants itself, as developer and EPC contractor to the plant companies, and owns one small reference plant. From 2030 it sells the CONNECT floating system mainly as a kit with the solar panel included, with site engineering, to developers and contractors who build the plants. The first kits are sold from 2028.
- The kit costs about €250 per kWp to produce today, of which the floating system is €140 and the panel the rest. It sells for about €500 per kWp. That gives a gross margin of about 44 % at today's cost, rising to about 49 % as costs fall.
- Revenue is €0.16 million in 2027 and €2.5 million in 2028, the year Gunneklevfjorden is delivered. It drops to €0.5 million in 2029, while Storavatnet waits for rezoning, then rises to €3.9 million in 2030 and €8.2 million in 2031.
- Break-even:
  - EBITDA turns positive in 2030 (€0.16 million) and reaches €1.9 million in 2031, in all four funding scenarios.
  - The net result turns positive in 2031 (€1.5 million).
  - Kit and engineering sales cover all costs from about 9 MWp of kits a year, without EPC deliveries.
- The funding need is €5.5 million of equity in two rounds. Round 1, €3.5 million, closes in the first quarter of 2027 to build the reference plants. Round 2, €2.0 million, follows in the second half of 2029, after a year of operation at Gunneklevfjorden. Cash stays above €0.75 million at every quarter end in 2027–2028 in the base case, and above €0.7 million at every year end in all four funding scenarios.
- The base case alone would need only about €0.1–0.3 million of round 2. The rest is a buffer against delay and funds growth beyond the plan.
- If the reference plants and kit sales come a year later, round 2 moves to 2030. Cash then dips below zero in 2029 and again in 2031, during the kit ramp-up, so about €0.5 million more equity is needed. A combined commercial downside needs about €1.0 million more: a year's delay, kit volume 30 % lower and the kit cost stuck at €0.24 per watt.
- A plant company that buys a first-of-a-kind 3.2 MWp plant from Sunlit Sea produces power at about €0.092 per kWh. To match the host's value of power produced behind its meter (about €0.072 per kWh at Skien havn), it needs investment support of about a quarter of its cost. Markets with poor irradiation, where a kit price of €500 per kWp gives too high a cost of power, are assumed to offer support for floating solar, as Enova does in Norway.

## 3. How Sunlit Sea makes money in each phase

The business model follows Sunlit Sea's own staging. It delivers whole projects first, becomes a technology supplier as projects grow, and takes on EPC for large plants and plant ownership only at much larger scale. The plan period covers the first two stages:

| | Phase 1: project developer and EPC contractor | Phase 2: technology provider |
|---|---|---|
| Years | 2027–2029 (deliveries to 2031) | Mainly from 2030; first kits from 2028 |
| Projects | Skien havn 0.35 MWp (reference plant), Gunneklevfjorden 3.2 MWp, Storavatnet phase 1 3.2 MWp | Kits to plants of 1–10 MWp built by others, from the 10 MW-class contract onwards |
| What Sunlit Sea sells | The delivered plant (EPC contract to the plant company), development and engineering fees, power from the reference plant | Kit with solar panel included, site engineering |
| Price basis | €0.70–0.73 per watt for the delivered plant | €450–500 per kWp for the kit; engineering about 4 % of plant cost |
| Margin | About 12–14 % on the EPC contract, after a first-of-a-kind contingency | 44–49 % on kits; 70 % on engineering before staff cost |
| Why | No developer or contractor in Norway can yet build a CONNECT plant. The first plants must be delivered end to end to create the reference record | Once references exist, kit sales scale without tying up capital or carrying full delivery risk |

The plant companies own the phase 1 plants: the Herøya Industripark project company at Gunneklevfjorden and the Haugaland Næringspark project company at Storavatnet. They sign the PPAs and finance the plants with project debt, owner equity and Enova investment support. Sunlit Sea takes a minority equity share of about €0.2 million in the Gunneklevfjorden company, so that it shares in the plant it builds. The plants themselves do not sit on Sunlit Sea's balance sheet.

Skien havn is modelled as Sunlit Sea's own plant. Two other options are open: a joint venture with the local contractor, or the contractor investing first and Sunlit Sea taking over later. The contractor holds the two-year permit and has prepared the Enova application. With a two-year permit and PPA, the plant is a reference cost more than an investment:

- after about €0.09 million of Enova support, the net cost is €0.25 million;
- the plant earns about €11 thousand a year after operating costs;
- that gives a negative net present value of about €0.17 million over ten years at 6 %.

That is the price of the first CONNECT plant in operation and of the durability record from its first winter. The plan keeps the SuRE grant on the marine works and grid connection (about €0.09 million) in development grants; deducting it from the plant cost instead would lower the net cost and the negative value by that amount. The local development fund has said it will consider support after the pilot, so a grant of about €150 thousand would reduce this cost later. If the permit is not extended after two years, the remaining book value is written down.

The Generation 1 plant at Skiftestjørna (105 kWp, PPA to the end of October 2026) is left out of the plan. Whether it is sold, gets a new PPA or is removed is still open. Its value is small against the figures here.

Early EPC contracts carry delivery risk with a thin margin. The plan therefore limits EPC to the three Norwegian reference projects, on these terms:

- a €0.03 per watt contingency in the price;
- a 30 % advance and staged payments;
- back-to-back terms with the subcontractors for mooring, electrical works and installation;
- liability capped at 20–30 % of the contract value;
- float production for Gunneklevfjorden released only after the winter inspection at Skien havn.

The bank guarantee fees are in the cost base, and the cash buffer covers collateral of €0.3 million (section 10).

Sunlit Sea's earlier five-year models, which mixed technology supply, EPC and plant ownership, did not hold, mainly because their prices were below what Generation 1 achieved. This plan is built from actual production cost.

The host's break-even price at Skien havn is 0.84 NOK/kWh excluding VAT. It is made up of avoided energy purchase, income from exported power and a small grid-tariff saving, at the 2026 spot price level of about 0.97 NOK/kWh. A host with a larger grid-tariff saving, or one that cannot get more grid capacity, values the power more.

The first plants need investment support of about a quarter of their cost to match the host's alternative. The Gunneklevfjorden project seeks Enova support of about 35 % of the total cost. Enova's usual basis is 30–50 % of the extra cost of the new technology against a reference solution, which gives less than that and covers most of what is needed. The "Gunneklevfjorden not built" case in section 10 shows what happens if the support falls short.

Without support, a plant matches the host's value at a total cost of about €0.56 per watt.

A developer that buys kits at €0.47 per watt (2030) builds a plant for about €0.83 per watt, adding electrical works, grid connection, mooring, installation, engineering and its own costs. In southern Norway such a plant produces power at about €0.098 per kWh. That is competitive where power is dearer or the yield higher: in German and Baltic industry, at hosts limited by grid capacity, and in southern Europe.

In markets with poor irradiation, where the kit price gives too high a cost of power, the plan assumes national support for floating solar, as Enova provides in Norway. The kit price is held at about €500 per kWp rather than cut to fit those markets.

### 5.6 Worked example 2: a plant with a breakwater on an inland lake

The first sites that need a breakwater are expected to be inland lakes and inner waters exposed to wind waves, of the type of the EIC Transition demonstrator site near Leipzig. Waves there come from one quarter (south to southeast), so a light breakwater covers a sector of about 45°. The site owner runs a leisure lake, and the breakwater can serve as berths or bathing platforms, so half of its cost is assumed to be carried by those uses.

The example is a kit-buyer plant (phase 2 capex €0.83 per watt) with the breakwater added, yield 960 kWh per kWp (PVGIS 5.2 for the lake at the platform's 2° tilt, with 8 % system losses for still water), and the opex and financing of section 5.2.

| Plant | Case | Breakwater (€/W) | Plant capex (€/W) | LCOE (€/kWh) |
|---|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 MWp | no breakwater | 0 | 0.83 | 0.087 |
| 1 MWp | breakwater, half carried by other uses | 0.14 | 0.97 | 0.099 |
| 1 MWp | breakwater, no other uses | 0.28 | 1.11 | 0.111 |
| 5 MWp | no breakwater | 0 | 0.83 | 0.087 |
| 5 MWp | breakwater, half carried by other uses | 0.05 | 0.88 | 0.092 |
| 5 MWp | breakwater, no other uses | 0.11 | 0.94 | 0.096 |

The breakwater costs are indicative, from the SUREWAVE design and cost data as modelled in D7.4 version 2.0, and are to be confirmed with Clement Germany. At 30 MWp the breakwater falls to about €0.02 per watt with dual use.

What it means:

- At 5 MWp the breakwater adds €0.005–0.009 per kWh. The lake's plant economics then depend on the plant size and the buyer, much more than on the breakwater.
- Selling to the grid does not pay: Germany's solar tender of July 2025 cleared at 4.84 ct/kWh on average, with a ceiling of 6.80 ct/kWh (Bundesnetzagentur). The plant needs a buyer who values power behind the meter well above that, such as the site owner's own operations or a nearby industrial user, or support for floating solar.

## 11. Break-even, operating leverage and market share

### 11.1 Is the revenue model profitable, sustainable and growing with volume?

- Profitable: each MWp of kit sold in phase 2 gives about €253 thousand of gross profit, a 49 % kit margin plus engineering. Against 2031 fixed costs, EBITDA turns positive at about 7 MWp of kit sales a year. All costs, including depreciation, are covered at about 9 MWp.
- Sustainable: revenue in phase 2 comes from repeat supply to developers and contractors, priced per kWp. Engineering is sold with each kit, and spare hinges and support follow the installed base. The company does not depend on owning plants or on grants after 2031.
- Growing with volume: fixed costs grow more slowly than volume, so the EBITDA margin rises with annual kit sales (2031 prices and costs):
